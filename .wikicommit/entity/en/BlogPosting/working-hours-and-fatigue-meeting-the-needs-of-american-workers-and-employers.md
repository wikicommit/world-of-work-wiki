"Working Hours and Fatigue: Meeting the Needs of American Workers and Employers" is a post in the NIOSH Science Bulletin of the US National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH), published on 12 April 2023 by three NIOSH staff members. It introduces a special issue on [[DefinedTerm/work-related-fatigue]] that the *American Journal of Industrial Medicine* published in November 2022, which explores factors that may increase work-related fatigue and actions to reduce work-related injuries and illnesses.

According to the post, the special issue grew out of discussions and collaborations at NIOSH's 2019 Working Hours, Sleep and Fatigue Forum and also draws on NIOSH reports on [[DefinedTerm/long-working-hours]] and [[DefinedTerm/shift-work]]. Six of its articles look at specific sectors — Agriculture, Forestry and Fishing; Healthcare and Social Assistance; Mining; Oil and Gas Extraction; Public Safety; and Transportation and Utilities — and two address topics that cut across all industries: workers at greater risk of workplace injury and illness, and the economics of nonstandard work schedules.

## Key Points

- Fatigue has no standard definition, but has been described as feeling weary or tired; fatigued workers may lack energy to work safely, have trouble paying attention, react more slowly and take more risks that lead to injury or errors.
- Citing published estimates, the post states that work-related fatigue costs US employers an estimated $218 billion or more a year through reduced productivity and health-related absences, that close to 1 in 8 workplace injuries may relate to fatigue, and that more than 1 in 5 fatal vehicle crashes may involve a drowsy driver.
- Unlike most other workplace hazards, work-related fatigue stems from many sources — age, health, lifestyle and nonwork responsibilities as well as scheduling practices such as [[DefinedTerm/night-work]] or extended hours, physically or mentally demanding or repetitive tasks, extreme temperatures and stress — so no single solution fits all situations, organizations or people.
- Limiting work hours, the traditional way to manage fatigue, and other successful strategies apply to jobs with predictable schedules; sleep training programmes mostly do not consider the many sources of fatigue or individual differences in rest and recovery, and restricting hours or promoting sleep in quiet, dark surroundings may not be possible in, for example, remote mining camps or storm-response utility work.
- Fatigue research is most common in Transportation and Utilities, Healthcare and Social Assistance, and Public Safety, where nonstandard schedules prevail; few studies identify fatigue risks or remedies in Oil and Gas Extraction, Mining, and Agriculture, Forestry and Fishing, which the post argues could learn from sectors with more experience of similar tasks and environments.
- Easy-to-understand health and safety information on fatigue mitigation, delivered in culturally appropriate languages and formats, would especially benefit young or new employees, small-business workers, women, minorities and people with low education or socioeconomic status.
- Economic evaluation of the costs of fatigue and the benefits of reducing it, combining disciplines such as economics and epidemiology, is presented as an important tool for organizations and policymakers.

## Context

The post presents the special issue as giving direction to workers, employers and researchers, and invites readers to share how their workplaces manage fatigue. Its authors describe themselves as a NIOSH health communications specialist, the coordinator of the NIOSH Center for Work and Fatigue Research, and a senior science advisor who co-manages NIOSH's Healthy Work Design and Well-Being Cross Sector Program.
