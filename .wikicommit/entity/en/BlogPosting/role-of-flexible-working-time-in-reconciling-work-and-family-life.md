"Paindliku tööaja roll töö- ja pereelu ühitamisel" ("The role of flexible working time in reconciling work and family life") is a blog post published on the Statistics Estonia website on 15 February 2017, written by Kadri Täht, a senior research fellow in sociology at Tallinn University. It starts from the observation that in Estonia a high labour market participation rate of women, including women with children, has long been the norm alongside that of men, and that most women work full-time, and asks what possibilities flexible working time (see [[DefinedTerm/flexible-working-hours]]) offers for reconciling work and family life.

The post treats working-time flexibility broadly, as flexibility in the number of hours worked, in the schedule (morning, day, evening, night, weekday or weekend) and in the place where work is done. Using data from the 2015 Estonian Labour Force Survey conducted by Statistics Estonia, it looks at three forms: part-time work ([[DefinedTerm/part-time-employment]]), work at atypical hours ([[DefinedTerm/atypical-working-hours]]) and working at home ([[DefinedTerm/working-from-home]]). Its conclusion is that households with and without children use these forms alike, and that occupation explains their use better than family status does.

## Key Points

- In Estonia the organisation of family time is associated with rather traditional patterns: time spent on housework and childcare remains strongly tilted towards women regardless of their labour market participation or relationship status.
- According to the 2015 Labour Force Survey, 38% of households in which the head of household worked had at least one member under 18; in 85% of these the respondent's spouse or partner also lived in the household, and most of these partners were also active in the labour market.
- Most measures meant to make workplaces more family-friendly focus on increasing (working-time) flexibility. Ideally, whether flexibility is used would follow preferences rather than forced choices.
- In the equality monitoring survey the post cites, nearly two thirds of respondents thought that a woman with a pre-school child should work part-time (67% of women and 62% of men), while only a fifth saw a home-based role for her (23% of men, 18% of women). About half (54% of men and 47% of women) thought she should work full-time once the youngest child goes to school; not participating in the labour market at all was seen as a marginal choice.
- In 2015, 6% of men and 14% of women in the Labour Force Survey worked part-time. Both men and women worked part-time more often when the household had small children, and less as the youngest child grew older; part-time work became more common again later in family life, but for reasons other than family.
- A considerable share of households with (small) children work in the evening, at night or at weekends. Households with small children tend to avoid frequent evening and night work, while frequent weekend work is in some cases (among men) even more common than in households with older children. Where the youngest child is a teenager, close to 30% of households have at least one partner working evenings, while combinations in which at least one partner regularly works at night concern 5–13% of households with minor children. Compared with European averages, Estonia sits in the middle of the ranking.
- Working at home (by one or both partners) is most common in families with at least one child aged up to 2 (14% of such households) and declines to 9% where the youngest child is 13–17. In households with a pre-school child it is more often the woman who works at home, and overall women make more use of this option, with or without children.
- The author suggests that the flexibility in use may often intensify rather than ease families' time-use practices, and that reconciliation through atypical hours often takes the form of desynchronised schedules — one partner working while the other is at home — which may have negative long-term consequences for family cohesion.

## Context

The post notes that earlier research on reconciling work and family life focused mainly on part-time work, and that much less is known about other options such as atypical working hours and telework. It also refers to earlier studies showing that the positive effect of working-time flexibility on reconciling work and family depends heavily on the institutional framework — whether and how far laws and regulations encourage and allow flexibility — and on the extent to which such arrangements are the households' own choice and preference.
