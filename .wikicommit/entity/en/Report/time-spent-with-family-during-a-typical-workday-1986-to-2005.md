*Time spent with family during a typical workday, 1986 to 2005* is an article in Statistics Canada's *Canadian Social Trends* (catalogue 11-008-X), written by a social sciences researcher in the Social and Aboriginal Statistics Division. It asks whether workers really spend less time with their families than before, and whether the length of the workday is the main reason. Using four cycles of the General Social Survey (GSS) on time use (see [[Dataset/canadian-time-use-survey]]) it documents how the average time workers living in a family spent with family members on a typical workday changed between 1986 and 2005, and analyses which factors account for the change.

The article frames the question against the rise of [[DefinedTerm/work-life-balance]] as a value for workers and a policy issue. It cites a 2003 survey in which Canadian workers ranked achieving work-life balance as the main indicator of a successful career, far ahead of salary, and Human Resources and Social Development Canada's description of work-life balance as "a critical public policy issue in Canada".

## Details

Workers spent on average 250 minutes (4.2 hours) with their spouse, children or other family members on a typical workday in 1986, and 206 minutes (3.4 hours) in 2005. The share spending six hours or more with family fell from 23% to 14%, and the share spending one hour or less rose from 9% to 14%. The time was not shifted to friends: time with friends fell by more than half, from about 44 to about 19 minutes. Instead, time spent alone outside working hours rose from 133 to 174 minutes. The decline in family time was observed for nearly all subgroups of workers, including both women (248 to 209 minutes) and men (250 to 205 minutes), all regions and all education levels.

Statistical models that took account of worker characteristics and of time spent on other activities explained the decline: once these were included, the differences between survey years were no longer statistically significant. A decomposition attributed about 39% of the decline to the increase in time spent in paid work on a typical workday — the largest single factor. Average time in paid employment rose from 506 to 536 minutes, and the share of workers working 10 hours or more rose from 17% to 25%; holding other factors constant, workers who spent 9 to 10 hours on paid work spent an estimated 52 minutes less with family than those who spent 7 to 8 hours. The other main factors were watching television alone (24% of the decline), eating at least one meal, snack or coffee alone (18%), less time spent at meals (about 11%), less time watching television (about 9%), time on personal care including sleep, and less time on social activities outside the home (7%).

Family structure also mattered. Workers living with a child under 5 spent the most time with family, and lone parents with a young child spent about an hour more than workers living with a spouse only. Women living with a spouse and a child under 5 spent an estimated 47 minutes more with family than comparable men, and 23 minutes more when their youngest child was aged 5 to 12, which the author links to women's continuing larger share of child care. With other factors held constant, gender, age, education and area of residence were not significantly associated with time spent with family. The study covers workdays only and gives no information on family time on days off.
