*Labour market characteristics of persons with and without disabilities, 2024* is an article in Statistics Canada's *Labour Statistics at a Glance* series (catalogue no. 71-222-X), released on 14 May 2025 and written by Vincent Hardy and Daniel Vergara. It reports annual labour market indicators for persons with and without disabilities, which data from the supplements program of the [[Dataset/canadian-labour-force-survey]] have allowed Statistics Canada to publish every year since 2022. The article says these annual indicators complement the in-depth portrait provided every five years by the [[Dataset/canadian-survey-on-disability]], which is the official source of information on the prevalence of disability in Canada.

The article looks first at how persons with disabilities fared in 2024, a year in which labour market conditions were generally easing in Canada — the unemployment rate rose from 5.8% in December 2023 to 6.7% in December 2024, and the job vacancy rate fell from 3.6% to 3.0% — covering employment, unemployment, industry, wages, unionization and benefits. It then turns to persons with disabilities outside the labour force: their overrepresentation, differences by age and gender, and how many would like to work. Its overall conclusion is that broad changes in labour market conditions affected persons with disabilities in 2024 while barriers limiting their full integration into the labour market persisted.

## Details

**Employment and unemployment.** From 2023 to 2024 the employment rate fell by 0.7 percentage points both for persons with disabilities (to 46.4%) and for persons without disabilities (to 66.2%). The decline among persons with disabilities was attributable to men (down 2.2 points to 46.9%), most sharply young men aged 15 to 24 (down 7.7 points to 39.7%); among young women with disabilities the rate fell 6.5 points to 49.2%, partly offset by a gain among core-aged women aged 25 to 54. The article suggests the larger fall among youth with disabilities may point to fewer opportunities for barrier-free employment as job vacancies decline. In 2024, 54.7% of persons with a less severe disability were employed, compared with 26.4% of those with a more severe disability. The [[DefinedTerm/unemployment-rate]] rose less among persons with disabilities (up 0.5 points to 8.1%) than among those without (up 1.0 point to 5.6%), narrowing the gap from 3.0 to 2.5 points — but alongside a fall in their labour force participation rate (down 0.6 points to 50.4%) and slower growth in the number looking for work; the gap widened only among young men (from 7.4 to 9.9 points). Employment rates of persons with disabilities were below those of persons without disabilities in every province, with the widest gaps in Ontario and Quebec (21.3 points) and the narrowest in Nova Scotia (16.6) and Alberta (16.8).

**Jobs, wages and benefits.** Workers with disabilities were more likely to work in health care and social assistance (15.7% against 13.1%) and public administration (7.6% against 5.6%), and 28.0% of employees with disabilities worked in the public sector against 22.8% of those without. They were less likely to work in the relatively high-paying private industries of professional, scientific and technical services and of finance, insurance, real estate, rental and leasing. Average hourly wages grew more slowly for employees with disabilities (up 4.6% against 5.3%), widening the hourly gap from $1.91 to $2.22, so that they earned 93.8% of the average hourly wage of employees without disabilities; on a weekly basis the figure was 91.2%, reflecting shorter usual weekly hours (34.6 against 35.5); had employees with disabilities had the same age distribution as those without, their average weekly wage would have been 89.2%. In 2024, 33.8% of employees with disabilities were covered by a collective agreement (29.4% of those without), partly because of their concentration in the public sector, and 66.1% had access to paid sick leave (64.6%) and 68.0% to medical or dental insurance (65.6%). Since 2022 their access to paid sick leave, medical or dental insurance and paid vacation leave has trended up.

**Part-time work.** In 2024, 21.3% of workers with disabilities held part-time jobs against 17.4% of those without, and 18.6% of part-time workers with disabilities worked part-time involuntarily, against 15.7% of those without disabilities (see [[DefinedTerm/involuntary-part-time-work]]). Full-time employment nevertheless grew faster among persons with disabilities (3.3% against 1.8%) from 2023 to 2024, driven by core-aged workers.

**Outside the labour force.** Half (50.4%) of persons with disabilities participated in the labour force in 2024, against 70.2% of those without. The participation gap between persons with and without disabilities was larger for men (51.6% against 74.6%) than for women (49.5% against 65.4%), which is why the gender gap in participation is smaller among persons with disabilities. Persons with disabilities made up 39.0% of people outside the labour force but 21.7% of those in it; among core-aged men, 50.7% of those not in the labour force had disabilities. Close to one in four (24.4%) youth with disabilities were not in education, employment or training ([[DefinedTerm/neet]]), twice the share among youth without disabilities (12.3%), and fewer students with disabilities combined education and employment (34.6% against 40.1%). Under a broad definition of unemployment that adds people outside the labour force who want to work, the unemployment rate of persons with disabilities would rise by 3.8 points to 11.9%, against 1.5 points to 7.1% for persons without disabilities.

**Data.** Persons with disabilities are identified with the Disability Screening Questions, which the Labour Force Survey and the Canadian Survey on Disability both use to identify 11 disability types and their severity. From 2014 to 2021 the questions were asked in the Canadian Income Survey for part of each year; 2022 was the first year in which the Labour Force Survey program collected 12 months of data with them, through the Canadian Income Survey and the Labour Market and Socio-economic Indicators supplement. Estimates exclude people living on Indigenous reserves, full-time members of the regular armed forces and people in institutions, and the analysis focuses on differences that are statistically significant at the 68% confidence level.
