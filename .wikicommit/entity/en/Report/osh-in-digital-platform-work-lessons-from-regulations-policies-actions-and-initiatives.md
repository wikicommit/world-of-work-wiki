This EU-OSHA policy brief examines how regulations, policies, strategies, initiatives and actions in the EU have dealt with the occupational safety and health (OSH) of digital platform workers. It builds on four in-depth case studies, a review of academic and grey literature, and a survey of EU-OSHA's national focal points. Its starting point is that early responses to platform work were limited, ad hoc and rarely concerned with working conditions or OSH, and that the debate has only more recently shifted towards the working conditions of [[DefinedTerm/digital-platform-worker]]s.

The brief defines digital platform work as all paid labour provided through, on or mediated by a [[DefinedTerm/digital-labour-platform]], characterised by the organisation of paid labour through a platform, the performance of specific tasks, the use of [[DefinedTerm/algorithmic-management]] to allocate, monitor and evaluate work, a three-party relationship (platform, client, worker), and a prevalence of non-standard arrangements with a tendency to classify workers as self-employed — which, it argues, shifts risks, liabilities and responsibilities, including for OSH, onto workers.

## Details

**OSH challenges.** According to the brief, the task-related risks of platform work resemble those of the same tasks performed outside the platform economy, but the nature of platform work may exacerbate them and complicate their prevention. It identifies four aggravating factors: the determination of employment status (platforms typically classify workers as self-employed, while the EU and many national OSH frameworks apply only to employees); algorithmic management, which undermines autonomy and job control and can lead to stress, anxiety, exhaustion and depression; professional isolation, poor work–life balance and lack of social support; and work transience, with chronic job and income insecurity.

**Mapping of responses.** The brief reports that in October 2021 Eurofound's platform economy repository held 170 initiatives and 44 policy documents, and that a European Commission study based on an expert survey identified 177 responses across the EU-27, Iceland, Norway and the United Kingdom, mostly concerning employment status, representation, earnings and social protection. With the exception of France and Italy, no country then had legislation directly targeting the working conditions or social protection of platform workers. The consultation of national focal points found that awareness of platform work and its OSH implications differed widely between Member States, and that platform workers are usually not considered in national OSH measures. Overall, few measures relate directly to OSH; more address it indirectly, for instance by clarifying the employment relationship or fostering data collection.

**Case studies.** The four case studies cover:

- the Spanish [[Legislation/riders-law]], which creates a rebuttable [[DefinedTerm/presumption-of-employment-relationship]] for delivery platform workers and a right to algorithmic transparency;
- the [[Legislation/bologna-charter]], set in the wider Italian context of contradictory court rulings, 2019 national legislation, regional initiatives in Lazio and Piedmont, and a Milan investigation that ordered four food delivery platforms to hire over 60,000 couriers as "employer-organised" workers and pay €733 million in fines;
- the French legislative framework — the [[Legislation/el-khomri-law]], the law on the fight against fraud, the mobility orientation law (LOM) and two 2021 ordinances — which the brief finds limited in material and personal scope and only indirectly linked to OSH, while noting that the LOM introduced a right to refuse tasks and a [[DefinedTerm/right-to-disconnect]] for some transport and delivery platform workers;
- actions by labour and social security inspectorates, where it contrasts disparate actions in Poland and joint inspections targeting a delivery platform in Belgium with the coordinated approach of the [[Organization/spanish-labour-and-social-security-inspectorate]].

**Takeaways.** The brief closes with four takeaways for policymakers: OSH risks in platform work remain largely overlooked, and binding, enforceable and comprehensive measures are needed that go beyond insurance, basic protective equipment and basic training, without employment status being an obstacle; awareness of OSH should be raised among platform workers and platforms, covering both physical and psychological risks; good practice should be shared more proactively at national and international level; and knowledge and data on OSH in platform work should be reinforced.
