This executive summary, published in 2021, summarises a report commissioned by EU-OSHA on prolonged static sitting at work (see [[DefinedTerm/sedentary-work]]). The report was written by researchers from vhp human performance (the Netherlands) in cooperation with the Nofer Institute of Occupational Medicine (Poland), and its contents are the authors' own rather than necessarily EU-OSHA's views. It explores the extent of sitting at work, its health effects, recommendations for time limits on sitting, and practical advice for avoiding and reducing prolonged sitting, and closes with conclusions and pointers for policy-makers. It aims to take into account the needs of micro and small enterprises, and looks at both office and non-office work, gender and ageing issues, and prolonged sitting in schools. A companion report covers prolonged standing.

The report defines prolonged sitting as being sedentary for more than 2 hours at a time, with three main characteristics: low energy expenditure, a seated body posture and static loading. It notes that there is no specific EU occupational safety and health policy on sitting at work, although work-related MSDs are prioritised in the EU OSH strategy and several EU directives are relevant to preventing risks from prolonged sitting.

## Details

**Extent of sitting.** The summary cites Eurostat data from 2017 showing that 39 % of EU workers carry out their work while sitting down, and the 2015 [[Dataset/european-working-conditions-survey]], in which 28 % of workers reported sitting almost all the time (31 % of women against 25 % of men) and the share working with computers, laptops or keyboards all or almost all of the time rose from 17.6 % in 2000 to 30.3 % in 2015. In the 2019 [[Dataset/esener]], prolonged sitting was the second most frequently reported risk factor in the EU-27, reported by 61 % of establishments.

**Workers at risk.** Office workers are most at risk, but drivers, pilots, crane operators, sewing machine operators, assembly line workers and those working at service desks, in laboratories, call centres and control rooms also sit for long periods; people working from home may be tempted to work longer without a break in poorer ergonomic conditions. Women are over-represented in predominantly seated, low-grade jobs such as office work and micro-assembly, which may leave them with little control over when they can take breaks. Prolonged sitting is a particular issue for older workers and those with chronic conditions such as back pain, and because work has become more sedentary and retirement ages have risen, workers today may be exposed to sedentary work over a longer part of their working lives.

**Health effects.** Based on a short literature review, the report links prolonged sitting to low back pain, neck and shoulder complaints, type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease, obesity, certain cancers (in particular breast and colon cancer), mental health issues and premature death. Whole-body vibration while sitting, for example in vehicles, increases the risk of low back problems and other [[DefinedTerm/work-related-musculoskeletal-disorders]], and seated work combined with repetitive work, static muscle load, awkward postures, force or reaching can cause upper limb disorders.

**Guidelines.** From the guidelines it reviewed, the report recommends spending 50 % or less of the working day sitting, getting up at least every 20–30 minutes, always getting up for at least 10 minutes after 2 hours of sitting, not exceeding 5 hours of sitting at work a day, and alternating between sitting, standing and walking. It treats 2 hours as the maximum for continuous sitting. It replaces the ideal of an "as upright as possible" posture with "dynamic sitting", in which sitting positions are continuously altered, and stresses that the opposite of sitting is moving rather than standing — so sit-stand desks alone are not enough, and prolonged sitting should not simply be replaced with prolonged standing.

**Prevention.** The report places action on prolonged sitting within a systematic prevention strategy based on risk assessment and a hierarchy of prevention measures: a suitable, adjustable ergonomic workstation; organising work to limit sitting through active work, task rotation, job enrichment, minibreaks and worker control over breaks; a work environment and culture that encourages movement; worker consultation and involvement; and health promotion, which it says will be ineffective on its own. Practical examples include letting supermarket checkout and petrol station staff alternate between sitting and standing, limiting casino croupiers to 15 minutes sitting and 30 minutes standing with a 15-minute rest after 45 minutes at the table, and microbreaks and task rotation on factory production lines. Its summary message is "sit when you need to, stand when you want to and move when you can".

**Pointers for policy-makers.** The report calls for sector-specific guidelines and simple resources for micro and small enterprises, more attention to prolonged sitting in non-office work, better research tools, and inclusion of prolonged sitting in OSH risk assessments and workplace health promotion campaigns. It asks for more research on prolonged sitting in jobs done by women and on older workers' extended exposure, a combined OSH and public health approach to reducing seated time at work and at home, and education on prolonged sitting and better classroom ergonomics in schools, where primary pupils spend 30 hours a week sitting at their desks.
