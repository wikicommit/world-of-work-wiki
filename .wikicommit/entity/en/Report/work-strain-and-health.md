*Arbeitsbelastungen und Gesundheit* ("Work strain and health") is issue 5/2011 (2nd year) of *GBE kompakt*, a series of the Robert Koch Institute's federal health reporting (*Gesundheitsberichterstattung des Bundes*) presenting figures and trends. It was written by Lars Eric Kroll and Stephan Müters of the Robert Koch Institute's Department of Epidemiology and Health Monitoring and Nico Dragano of the University Hospital Essen, and is published under ISSN 2191-4974.

The report sets out from the role of paid work: for many people of working age it is the main source of their livelihood, it finances the social security systems, and it brings meaning, social recognition, fixed time structures and social networks outside family and friends. With demographic ageing and a forecast shortage of skilled workers, it argues that it matters that qualified workers can work as long as possible, and that work-related health risks can stand in the way of this by forcing people to give up their occupation early. Strains at work arise from physical and mental activities, work organisation, the working environment and social relations at work; where they exceed a person's capacity they can lead to health impairments, absence, occupational diseases, incapacity for work or early retirement.

The analysis uses the [[Dataset/geda-study]] 2010, a telephone interview survey conducted by the Robert Koch Institute between September 2009 and July 2010, restricted to 13,767 women and men aged 18 to 64 who were employed at the time of the interview. A nine-question module covered environmental, physical and psychosocial strains with two indicators each, asking whether respondents were exposed to each condition "often", "sometimes", "rarely" or "never", plus a question on whether they believed their health was endangered by their work.

## Details

**Perceived health risk.** Most employed women and men said their work endangered their health "not at all" or at most "moderately". However, only about one in seven employed women (11.6% "strongly" and 2.3% "very strongly") but one in five employed men (16.1% and 4.0%) saw their own health as strongly or even very strongly endangered by their work. The share was particularly high among workers aged 30 to 44 (for men, 18.6% and 4.9%); these age differences were more marked for men than for women.

**Most common strains.** Working under time or performance pressure was the most frequent strain (often reported by 35.9% of women and 44.1% of men). It was followed by overtime or long working hours and commuting (27.5% and 40.5%) and adverse environmental conditions such as noise, cold or heat (25.2% and 41.7%), which men reported more often than women. Heavy lifting and carrying, awkward postures and [[DefinedTerm/shift-work]] (20.1% and 21.5%) were also common, while impairments in the working climate (5.3% and 5.8%) and strain from job insecurity (6.8% and 7.2%) were relatively rare. The authors conclude that despite the economy's structural shift from industry to services, a considerable share of employees in Germany are still affected by physical and environmental strains.

**Association with perceived risk.** In logistic regression models controlling for age, weekly working hours, length of service and the other strains, every form of strain was associated with a higher risk of perceiving one's work as health-endangering, regardless of sex. For women, impairments in the working climate (odds ratio 2.51) and [[DefinedTerm/time-pressure-at-work]] (2.32) were most closely associated with this perception; for men, impairments in the working climate (2.91) came first, followed by adverse environmental conditions (2.63).

**Differences between groups.** Full-time workers, who spend more time at their workplaces, perceived their work as burdening their health considerably more often than other groups: 17% of full-time women and 21% of full-time men reported strong or very strong strain, against 5% and 12% of those in marginal employment. By sector, women in the health sector reported strong strain particularly often, reflecting physical and emotional strain in nursing and elderly care, while for men it was mainly those in construction and crafts and in goods and passenger transport. After controlling for age, scope of employment and length of service, manual workers (*Arbeiter*) reported a strong health risk from their work 1.7 times (women) and 2.0 times (men) as often as salaried employees, and 2.0 and 1.4 times as often as civil servants.

**Context and conclusions.** The report links the rising relative importance of psychosocial strain at work with the growing role of mental disorders in early retirement: according to the German statutory pension insurance, 80,702 women and 90,427 men retired early in 2009 because of reduced earning capacity due to illness or accident, about 14% of all new pensions, and the share of mental disorders among the diagnoses behind them rose from 33% to 38% between 2006 and 2009. In 2009 there were also 16,657 recognised cases of [[DefinedTerm/occupational-disease]] and about 975,000 notifiable accidents at work. The authors conclude that most employees in Germany do not perceive pronounced health risks at their workplaces, but that a not inconsiderable share do, indicating substantial potential for prevention and workplace health promotion. They point to the Joint German Occupational Safety and Health Strategy of the federal government, the Länder and the accident insurance institutions, whose 2008–2012 objectives covered reducing accidents at work, musculoskeletal disorders and skin diseases, and secondarily mental strain.
