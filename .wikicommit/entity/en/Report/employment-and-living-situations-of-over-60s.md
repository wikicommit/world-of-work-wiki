"Erwerbs- und Lebenslagen von Über-60-Jährigen" (Employment and living situations of people over 60) is an IAB-Kurzbericht, a short research report published on 4 April 2023 by the Institute for Employment Research (IAB) of Germany's Federal Employment Agency. Its headline finding is that, with the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, older employees too worked from home more than before. The report starts from the premise that employment of people over 60 is one of the levers for meeting future demand for skilled labour, and asks whether the health risk of the pandemic and the temporary fall in labour demand pushed this age group out of the labour market, as had initially been suspected.

It compares three age groups meant to represent the phases of the transition to retirement: 60- to 62-year-olds (employment or early retirement), 63- to 65-year-olds (imminent entry into the old-age pension) and 66- to 70-year-olds (working while drawing a pension). It distinguishes employees subject to social insurance contributions from those in marginal employment ([[DefinedTerm/minijob]]s), either exclusively or as a second job.

## Details

**Data.** Employment trends come from a two per cent sample of the IAB's Integrated Employment Biographies (IEB), administrative data based on employer notifications, comparing May 2019 with May 2020 for about 163,300 people aged 60 to 70. Working from home and worries are measured with the first wave (May 2020) of the IAB high-frequency online personal panel (HOPP) "Leben und Erwerbstätigkeit in Zeiten von Corona", whose responses were linked to the IEB for respondents who consented.

**Employment.** At the end of the first lockdown in May 2020, all three age groups showed a slight increase in employment compared with May 2019, and the mix of employment types barely changed. In both years more than 80% of employed 60- to 62-year-olds had only employment subject to social insurance; this share falls with age to about 5–6% after entry into retirement, while minijobs follow the reverse pattern. The authors see no serious fall in employment among over-60s in the first months of the pandemic, while noting that early exits needing a longer lead time might only show up later.

**Working from home.** More than one in four employees aged 60–62 and 63–65 said in May 2020 that they had the possibility to work from home, compared with 16% of 66- to 70-year-olds; in all three groups it was least often possible for those in minijobs, which the authors relate to the kind of work minijobs involve. Among those who had the possibility, use rose sharply: from 46% before the pandemic to 82% in May 2020 among 60- to 62-year-olds, with weekly hours worked at home rising from 15 to 26, and from 45% to 75% among 63- to 65-year-olds, with hours rising from 14 to 26. Working pensioners aged 66–70 had already worked from home more before the pandemic, so their increase was smaller (64% to 69%, and 10 to 11 hours a week). In regression analyses, gender was hardly related to having the possibility, while a university degree was positively related in all groups; minijobs were negatively related for the two older groups; and employment in information and communication or in administration raised the likelihood compared with trade, transport and hospitality, whereas health and social work reduced it for 60- to 65-year-olds.

**Worries.** Employees subject to social insurance worried least about their own health in all three groups. Among 60- to 62-year-olds, 69% of those not in employment had great or some health worries; in the two older groups those in minijobs worried to a similar extent (68% and 65%). Working from home showed no relationship with health worries, although it was meant primarily as infection protection, while higher satisfaction with one's own health was associated with fewer worries. Financial worries were lowest among 66- to 70-year-olds, most of whom were covered by an old-age pension, but were reported by 59% of minijobbers aged 63–65 and by half of non-employed 60- to 62-year-olds; across groups they were concentrated among people with low household income, whatever their employment type.

**Conclusions.** The authors conclude that the labour-market effects of the infection-control measures do not appear to have hit older employees harder than the rest of the workforce, but that the pandemic acted like a magnifying glass on existing social inequalities. They caution that working pensioners are a selective group, often in good health or with higher education, and that the self-employed could not be analysed. They suggest that firms could examine ways — such as more working from home, flexible working hours and tailored training — to keep people over 60 in employment for longer.
