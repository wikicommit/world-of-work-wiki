This report, published under the series title *Protagonistas del crecimiento* ("protagonists of growth"), is the first of a quarterly series by Argentina's [[Organization/national-directorate-of-economy-equality-and-gender]] (DNEIyG), part of the Ministry of Economy. Each issue is meant to present a basic set of gender-gap indicators — on time and care, on the labour market and horizontal segregation, and on income — together with a section on a specific population or topic; the topic of this first issue is paid domestic work in private households. Most labour and income indicators are the directorate's own calculations from INDEC's Permanent Household Survey (EPH) for the fourth quarter of 2021.

The report frames gender inequality in Argentina as structural: women participate less in the labour market, have higher [[DefinedTerm/informal-employment]] and unemployment, and receive lower incomes, which it links to the time they spend on unpaid domestic and care work. It argues that these inequalities deepened with the economic and care crisis of COVID-19, and presents the 2021 recovery — when women's activity and employment rates reached their highest levels since 2003 — as the result of gender-sensitive policies. It notes that official statistics still record sex as a binary, so the report uses the categories women and men, and that the 2022 national census asked about gender identity for the first time.

## Details

**Labour market.** In the fourth quarter of 2021, women's activity rate was 50.3% against 69.7% for men, a gap of 19.4 percentage points; counting people who are homemakers as active roughly halves the gap (60.5% against 71.0%). Women's employment rate was 46.4% against 65.3% for men (a gap of 18.9 points), and their unemployment rate was 7.7% against 6.4%. Among people under 30 the unemployment gap was 2.7 points (16.2% against 13.5%), after young women's unemployment fell from 26% to 16.2% within a year. Time-related underemployment — working fewer than 35 hours a week involuntarily while wanting more hours — affected 14.6% of employed women and 10.1% of men, which the report attributes to unpaid care work pushing women towards jobs with shorter hours. Among wage earners, 35.7% of women and 31.3% of men had no pension contributions deducted.

**Segregation.** Almost four in ten employed women work in care-related activities: 14.7% in teaching, 12.2% in domestic service and 10.2% in social and health services, while men are concentrated in industry, transport, primary activities, energy and construction (see [[DefinedTerm/occupational-segregation]]). Domestic service, women's main branch of employment at the start of 2020, had fallen to third place behind commerce and teaching by the end of 2021.

**Pensions.** Only 12.1% of women aged 55–59 had more than 20 years of pension contributions, and more than 85.7% of women who retire do so through a pension moratorium, which the report describes as women's main route to a pension and a form of monetary recognition of their unpaid work. A measure recognising pension contributions for time spent raising children (one year per child, two per adopted child, with additional years for a child with a disability or one who received the Universal Child Allowance) had allowed more than 140,000 women to start a retirement claim.

**Income.** The gap between women's and men's income from their main occupation was 28.3% ($45,359 against $63,297), and among informal wage earners 33.6%. The report notes that emergency income measures during the pandemic went mainly to women, who receive a larger share of non-labour income, and that women make up seven in ten people in the lowest income decile while six in ten people in the highest decile are men (see [[DefinedTerm/gender-pay-gap]]).

**Time and care.** Citing INDEC's 2013 survey on unpaid work and time use, women perform 76.4% of unpaid domestic and care work ([[DefinedTerm/unpaid-work]]), spending on average 6.4 hours a day on it against 3.4 hours for men. Preliminary results of INDEC's 2021 National Time Use Survey put women's participation in this work at 91.6% and men's at 73.9%. The report also points to the leave regime — two days for non-birthing parents and 90 days for birthing parents — as one of the factors sustaining the unequal distribution of care, and describes the "Cuidar en Igualdad" bill sent to Congress in May 2022 to create an integrated national care system and reform leave.

**Special section: paid domestic work.** Before the pandemic, paid domestic work in private households employed 1.2 million women (16.7% of employed women). More than 400,000 workers lost their jobs in the second quarter of 2020, and 280,000 had not recovered them by the end of 2021. The sector is 98.4% female, has the highest informality in the economy (78.1% of its workers had no pension deductions) and the lowest average income ($16,936 in the fourth quarter of 2021, below the poverty-line basic basket of $23,995). The report presents the [[GovernmentService/registradas]] programme as the state's response to this situation.
