*Work-related musculoskeletal disorders statistics in Great Britain, 2025* is an annual statistical report published by the Health and Safety Executive (HSE) on 20 November 2025, covering data up to March 2025. It forms part of HSE's suite of Accredited Official Statistics. Its main data source is workers' own reports in the Labour Force Survey (LFS), a national household survey run by the Office for National Statistics in which HSE commissions annual questions on work-related illness; historic data from THOR-GP, a scheme in which general practitioners reported newly diagnosed cases of work-related ill health, provide a medically assessed perspective.

The report describes [[DefinedTerm/work-related-musculoskeletal-disorders]] as disorders of muscles, joints and tendons that develop in an occupational setting from the physical tasks of normal work, associated with fixed or constrained body positions, continual repetition of movements, force concentrated on small parts of the body such as the hand or wrist, and a pace of work that does not allow sufficient recovery between movements; workplace psychosocial factors such as organisational culture, the health and safety climate and human factors may also create the conditions for them, and generally none of these factors acts on its own. It estimates that 511,000 workers were suffering from a work-related musculoskeletal disorder (new or long-standing) in 2024/25, a prevalence of 1,470 per 100,000 workers, and that 7.1 million working days were lost as a result.

## Details

**Scale and trend.** There were 173,000 new cases in 2024/25, an incidence of 500 per 100,000 workers, and an average of 14 days lost per case. Musculoskeletal disorders accounted for 27% of all work-related ill health cases and 20% of all working days lost to work-related ill health. Of the cases, 43% mainly affected the back, 41% the upper limbs or neck and 15% the lower limbs; back conditions accounted for 41% of the days lost (13.2 days per case), upper limb and neck conditions for 34% (11.4 days per case) and lower limb conditions for 25% (23 days per case). Before the coronavirus pandemic both the rate of self-reported work-related musculoskeletal disorders and the working days lost per worker showed a generally downward trend; the rates in each of the latest three years are similar to the 2018/19 pre-pandemic level.

**Industry and occupation.** Averaged over 2022/23–2024/25, construction (2,000 per 100,000 workers), transportation and storage (1,800) and administrative and support service activities (1,700) had significantly higher rates than the all-industry average of 1,180. Skilled trades occupations (2,630), process, plant and machine operatives (2,470), elementary occupations (1,730) and caring, leisure and other service occupations (1,640) had significantly higher rates than all occupations, as did smaller groups including skilled agricultural and construction trades and transport and mobile machine drivers.

**Age, gender and workplace size.** Neither women nor men differed significantly from the rate for all workers. Men and women aged 16–34 had significantly lower rates, while men aged 45 and over and women aged 55 and over had significantly higher rates. Small workplaces had a significantly higher rate than workplaces of all sizes, and medium and large workplaces significantly lower rates.

**Causes.** In LFS self-reports (averaged over 2009/10–2011/12), the main causes cited were manual handling, working in awkward or tiring positions, and keyboard or repetitive work, with workplace accidents and stress at work among other factors. Cases reported by general practitioners to THOR-GP (2013–2015) showed a similar pattern, with heavy lifting the predominant factor and material manipulation and keyboard work also significant.
