*Managing the risks of shift work* is guidance published by WorkSafe New Zealand, available as a web page and a PDF, whose web version was last updated in May 2021. It is written for persons conducting a business or undertaking (PCBUs), though it notes that workers, supervisors, managers, health and safety representatives (HSRs) and unions may also find it useful. Its starting point is the PCBU's duty under the [[Legislation/health-and-safety-at-work-act-2015]] to ensure, so far as is [[DefinedTerm/reasonably-practicable]], the health and safety of its workers, together with workers' own duty to take reasonable care of their health and safety — so that businesses and workers should deal with the risks of shift work, including mental and physical fatigue, together.

The guidance defines [[DefinedTerm/shift-work]] broadly, as any work that requires a worker to be awake when they would normally be asleep, and treats [[DefinedTerm/work-related-fatigue]] as its central consequence. Fatigue, in its account, is a physiological state in which someone cannot function mentally and physically at their best, caused by four main things: missing out on sleep, being awake for too long, working and sleeping in the wrong parts of the circadian body clock cycle ([[DefinedTerm/circadian-disruption]]), and physical and mental workload.

## Details

**Why shift work needs managing.** Together, shift work and fatigue are said to contribute to poorer mental and physical health, a lower level of functioning, a greater likelihood of incidents and injuries — including while driving for work and commuting — and higher turnover and sick leave. The guidance notes that incident rates are higher on evening and night shifts than on morning shifts and rise as hours at work increase, and that consistently working more than 40 hours a week, regularly having short recovery periods between shifts, or regularly working more than two consecutive night shifts can lead to more sick leave. A diagram credited to the Sleep/Wake Research Centre at Massey University shows the links between shift work, sleep, long periods of wake, the circadian body clock and fatigue.

**A continuous risk management loop.** The core recommendation is to put robust fatigue risk management processes in place that identify, assess and minimise the risks of shift work and monitor whether control measures are working, as a continuous loop. Two foundations come first: engagement with workers and their representatives — for example through a working group of union members and delegates, HSRs, supervisors, managers and shift-work experts — and management commitment, so that resourcing, chains of responsibility and support for initiatives are in place. Risks are identified using data sources such as actual versus rostered hours, fatigue and incident reports, overtime records, sick leave and turnover, performance trends and validated questionnaires, and by working out who is most vulnerable: younger or older workers, pregnant workers and new parents, workers with chronic illnesses or time-dependent medication, casual workers unused to the business's schedules, workers who may already be fatigued, those doing safety-critical work, and those who stay on call after a standard day's work.

**Smart rostering and other controls.** The guidance gives most of its space to roster and shift-work design, stressing that "there is no perfect schedule" and that rostering alone will not prevent fatigue. It sets out key points and suggested actions on the physical and mental demands of the role, the variety of work activity, permanent versus rotating shifts, the timing of shifts (night, early-morning, afternoon and daytime), shift length (from 8-hour shifts to shifts longer than 12 hours, flexible and split shifts), [[DefinedTerm/rest-breaks-at-work]] within and between shifts, and rest days. Among its suggestions are avoiding permanent night shifts, rostering forward-rotating shifts, avoiding shift starts before 7 am where not essential, limiting 12-hour night shifts to two or three consecutive nights, avoiding shifts of more than 12 hours, setting a general limit of five to seven consecutive working days for standard shifts, and allowing at least two nights of full unrestricted sleep when switching between day and night shifts ([[DefinedTerm/night-work]], [[DefinedTerm/sleep-debt]]). It also addresses [[DefinedTerm/on-call-and-stand-by-time]], for which enough rest and recovery time should be scheduled, and [[DefinedTerm/workplace-napping]], drawing on the Safer Nursing 24/7 Code of Practice.

Beyond the roster, the guidance recommends a shift-work and fatigue policy and supporting procedures, clear routes for workers to raise concerns within a "no-blame" culture, including fatigue as an option on near-miss and incident report forms, planned communication (particularly at shift handover), and training tailored to every level of the business. Control measures are to be reviewed when tasks, equipment, procedures or schedules change or when there is any sign they are not working, using information from workers, incident and absence records and, where used, Employee Assistance Programme records. Appendices cover HSWA duties, the meaning of "reasonably practicable", overlapping duties, worker engagement, general risk management steps, a fatigue identification tool, background on fatigue, sleep and circadian rhythms, and a glossary.
