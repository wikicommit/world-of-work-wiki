*Realizing decent work in the platform economy* (ILC.113/Report V(1)) is a report of the International Labour Office, first published in 2024, prepared for the fifth item on the agenda of the 113th Session (2025) of the International Labour Conference. It opens the standard-setting process on decent work in the platform economy. The ILO Governing Body had requested a tripartite Meeting of Experts on Decent Work in the Platform Economy, held in Geneva from 10 to 14 October 2022, and then asked the Office for a normative gap analysis. Having taken note of that analysis at its 347th Session in March 2023, the Governing Body placed a standard-setting item on the agenda of the 113th Session under a double-discussion procedure. In line with the Conference's Standing Orders for that procedure, the report gives up-to-date information on platform work and on the regulations and practices related to it around the world, to inform the Conference's discussions and help Member States answer the questionnaire appended to it.

The report concentrates on platforms that connect businesses and clients to workers, either as intermediaries or by engaging workers directly. It describes the growth of the platform economy and the characteristics of work and workers on these platforms, examines how existing international labour standards apply to platform work, and reviews national legislation, case law and collective agreements dimension by dimension: employment classification, fundamental principles and rights at work, employment promotion, remuneration, working time, contract termination and deactivation, workers' personal data, dispute resolution, social security, representation and social dialogue, access to information, and compliance. It ends with the Office's case for a standard and a questionnaire to governments.

## Details

**Platforms and platform work.** Drawing on the Crunchbase database, the report finds that the number of platforms connecting businesses and clients to workers rose from 193 in 2010 to 1,070 in 2023, most of them online platforms or platforms in delivery, passenger transport, care and domestic work. Official surveys put the prevalence of such work in the low single digits of employment in most countries, from 0.5 per cent in Chile to 4 per cent in the Philippines, while the World Bank estimates that between 154 million and 435 million active workers are on online platforms, depending on the method. The report describes platform work as offering flexibility and low entry barriers, but also points to long hours in the transport and delivery sectors, unpaid time, studies estimating that a significant proportion of platform workers' hourly earnings are below the local minimum wage, the role of algorithms in allocating, monitoring and paying for work, and a potential for greater [[DefinedTerm/informal-employment]]. Most platforms characterize themselves as technological intermediaries and classify most workers as self-employed, which the report treats as a central issue for access to labour and social protection (see [[DefinedTerm/digital-platform-worker]]).

**International labour standards.** There are no international instruments of comprehensive application to digital labour platforms. Summarizing the Office's normative gap analysis, the report finds that standards on fundamental principles and rights at work, violence and harassment, employment promotion and some key principles on migrant workers apply to platform workers whether or not they are in wage employment. Others — including the Occupational Safety and Health Convention, 1981 (No. 155), the Minimum Wage Fixing Convention, 1970 (No. 131), the Termination of Employment Convention, 1982 (No. 158) and the Maternity Protection Convention, 2000 (No. 183) — do not extend to self-employed workers. It also identifies thematic gaps: no standard addresses the use of algorithms (see [[DefinedTerm/algorithmic-management]]) or comprehensively regulates the protection of workers' personal data; dispute resolution, penalties such as account suspension or deactivation, and the cross-border nature of online platform work are not fully covered; and on working time, the remuneration of waiting periods (see [[DefinedTerm/on-call-and-stand-by-time]]), limits on overtime, total hours across multiple jobs and workers' ability to disconnect from a platform without consequences are gaps.

**National law and practice.** The review covers 41 legislative instruments dealing explicitly with platform work, which the report regards as indicative rather than exhaustive. Countries regulate by amending existing labour legislation (for example Belgium, Chile, Croatia, France, Italy and Portugal), adopting standalone laws (China, the Canadian Province of Ontario), adopting sector-based legislation (the City of Seattle and Washington State in the United States) or extending labour or social protections through specialized laws (the social security laws of India and the Republic of Korea). Twelve of the laws contain statutory presumptions of an employment relationship (see [[DefinedTerm/presumption-of-employment-relationship]]). The report finds a trend towards extending labour protections and social security to platform workers irrespective of employment status, through legislation and collective agreements, and it lists developments such as rules on [[DefinedTerm/account-deactivation]], a [[DefinedTerm/right-to-disconnect]] from a platform, remuneration for waiting time, algorithmic transparency and human oversight, platform contributions to social security, and registration, licensing and reporting obligations. In France it describes the [[Organization/authority-for-social-relations-of-employment-platforms]], an administrative body established in 2021 to support social dialogue between platforms and the workers who use them.

**The case for a standard.** The Office concludes that normative action is required to close the gaps and considers that the new instrument or instruments should recall that international labour standards apply to all workers, including digital platform workers, unless otherwise provided, while supplementing them with standards specific to platform work. It recommends that they apply to both location-based and online platforms and to both employees and self-employed workers, who make up the larger share of platform workers; that they address algorithms, deactivation, personal data and the cross-border nature of platform work and disputes; that they leave Member States some flexibility; and that they could include a simplified and accelerated procedure for amending specific provisions in light of technological change. It suggests that a single instrument could combine mandatory provisions with provisions providing guidance, as the Maritime Labour Convention, 2006 does.

**Questionnaire.** The appended questionnaire asks governments, after consulting the most representative organizations of employers and workers, whether the instrument should be a Convention, a Recommendation, a Convention supplemented by a Recommendation, or a Convention combining mandatory and guidance provisions, and puts proposed definitions and thematic provisions to them, each marked as possibly mandatory or as guidance. Replies were requested by 31 August 2024, to inform a second report that, together with this one, was to be the basis for the first discussion in June 2025 and the second discussion in June 2026.
