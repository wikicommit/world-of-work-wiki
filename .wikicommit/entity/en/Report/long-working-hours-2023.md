*Long working hours, 2023* is an article in Statistics Canada's *Quality of Employment in Canada* series (catalogue no. 14-28-0001-X), released on 25 July 2024. It presents the long working hours indicator: the number of employed persons who usually work 49 hours or more per week at their main job and second job (if applicable), as a percentage of all employed persons ([[DefinedTerm/long-working-hours]]). Unless otherwise noted, the analysis is based on annual averages from the [[Dataset/canadian-labour-force-survey]] for workers aged 15 and older.

The article says the 49-hour threshold is based on International Labour Organization conventions — it cites the Hours of Work (Industry) Convention, 1919 (No. 1) and the Hours of Work (Commerce and Offices) Convention, 1930 (No. 30) — and notes that in Canada workers under federal jurisdiction cannot work more than 48 hours under normal circumstances. It relates long hours to several dimensions of quality of employment: they could lead to difficulties managing [[DefinedTerm/work-life-balance]] and negatively affect physical and mental well-being.

## Details

**Long-run trend.** 11.6% of workers worked long hours in 1976, rising to a peak of 14.8% in 1994 and 1995. Apart from 2003 to 2007 and 2020 to 2022, the share followed a downward trend from 1997 to 2023, falling from 13.0% to 8.2%, and has remained relatively stable since the COVID-19 pandemic. The decline from 1997 to 2023 was driven by a 7.4 percentage point fall in the share of employed men working long hours. Fewer women than men have worked long hours since 1976, but the gap has narrowed: from 16.2% of men and 4.0% of women in 1976 (a gap of 12.2 points) to 11.3% of men and 4.8% of women in 2023 (6.5 points).

**Who works long hours (2023).** Workers aged 55 and older had the highest rate among the main age groups (9.5%), followed by those aged 25 to 54 (8.8%), while workers aged 15 to 24 were least likely to work long hours (3.5%). West Asian workers had a slightly higher rate (9.9%) than workers who were not racialized or Indigenous (8.3%); rates were 9.0% for South Asian and 8.3% for Black workers, and 5.7% for Chinese workers.

**Self-employment.** The self-employed made up 13.2% of all employed persons in 2023 but 41.5% of those working long hours. Over a quarter of the self-employed (26.0%) worked long hours, against 5.5% of paid employees, and self-employed men (31.5%) did so more often than self-employed women (16.7%) ([[DefinedTerm/self-employment]]).

**Provinces and industries.** Rates ranged from 6.0% in Quebec to 13.7% in Newfoundland and Labrador, where the higher rate was partly related to the larger share of workers in the natural resources industry (7.0%, against 0.8% in Quebec). Counting usual hours in the main job only, long hours were most common in agriculture (33.0%), natural resources (25.3%), transportation and warehousing (17.7%) and construction (14.2%) — industries that may partly reflect above-average rates of self-employment — and least common in public administration (3.4%), retail trade (4.7%) and educational services (4.8%).

**Measurement.** Data collected before 1997 are not directly comparable with later data. Before January 1997, usual hours were the hours usually worked in a typical week, whether paid or not; from January 1997, usual hours for employees refer to their normal paid or contract hours, not counting [[DefinedTerm/overtime]], while the definition is unchanged for the self-employed and unpaid family workers ([[DefinedTerm/usual-hours-of-work]]). For people who changed jobs during the reference week, total usual hours were the sum of main and second job hours before 1997 and the hours at the new or main job afterwards. Industry coding follows NAICS 2017.
