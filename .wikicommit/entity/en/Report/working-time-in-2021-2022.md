Working time in 2021–2022 is a research report published in 2023 by Eurofound, the European Foundation for the Improvement of Living and Working Conditions, as part of a review it publishes every second year. It covers the most important developments in working time resulting from legislative reforms and collective bargaining at national or sectoral level in the EU Member States and Norway in 2021 and 2022, and updates earlier editions and Eurofound's database of wages, working time and collective disputes. The report relies mainly on information from the Network of Eurofound Correspondents and warns that figures from different countries are not strictly comparable, because of differing reference periods, extra days off, averaging schemes, the treatment of part-time workers and the differing roles of legislation and collective bargaining.

The report concludes that the most important regulatory changes in the period were linked to the transposition of two EU directives — the Work–life Balance Directive and the Transparent and Predictable Working Conditions Directive — and that the reduction of working time, and in particular the [[DefinedTerm/four-day-week]], was increasingly debated in many Member States.

## Details

**Regulatory developments.** Transposition of the two directives brought changes such as rights to request flexible working, remote work or a move from part-time to full-time work, new or extended paternity, parental and carers' leave, and more detailed written information on working hours and schedules. Belgium's Labour Deal of October 2022 and Greek legislation allowed full-time employees to work a four-day week — in Greece explicitly without a reduction in working time — and Belgium also introduced a right to request a working time cycle over two consecutive weeks. Germany, Greece and Slovenia saw important developments on [[DefinedTerm/working-time-recording]]: a German Federal Labour Court ruling requiring all employers to record working time, a Greek digital job card linked in real time to the Ministry of Labour's ERGANI II system, and Slovenian rules requiring detailed electronic evidence of working time. Other changes covered on-call workers in Estonia, telework in Croatia, Norway, Poland and Portugal, and Spanish preparatory work on legislation against "time poverty".

**Collective bargaining and debates.** Some agreements reduced working hours — for example from 38 to 37 hours a week in Austria's private social care sector — while others made working hours more flexible or, as in Finnish paper production, extended them. The four-day week was the most debated issue of 2022, discussed in Austria, Ireland, Latvia, Lithuania, Portugal and Spain; other debates concerned general reductions in working time, the regulation of and payment for overtime, Sunday work in Croatian retail and bonuses for full-time work in the Netherlands.

**Weekly working time.** Under the Working Time Directive, average weekly working time may not exceed 48 hours over a period of up to four months, with a minimum daily rest of 11 hours and a minimum uninterrupted weekly rest of 24 hours. Most countries set a statutory maximum week of either 48 or 40 hours, with Belgium (38) and France (35) the main exceptions. In 2022 the average collectively agreed normal working week for full-time workers was 38.1 hours in the EU27, 37.6 hours in the 14 pre-2004 Member States (EU14) and 39.7 hours in the 13 later ones (EU13), ranging from 35.7 hours in France to 40 hours in several countries. Among the sectors examined, agreed hours were shortest in public administration (37.7) and longest in retail (38.5). [[DefinedTerm/usual-hours-of-work]] of full-time employees, measured by the EU Labour Force Survey, averaged 39.7 hours in the EU27 in 2022 — 1.6 hours longer than agreed hours — ranging from 37.9 hours in Finland to 41.4 hours in Cyprus.

**Annual working time.** The Working Time Directive requires at least four weeks of paid annual leave. Most countries have a 20-day statutory minimum; the average collectively agreed paid annual leave was 24.3 days in the EU27 (25.3 in the EU14 and 20.9 in the EU13). Combining agreed weekly hours, leave and public holidays, the report estimates average collectively agreed normal annual working time in 2022 at about 1,726 hours in the EU27, 1,698 hours in the EU14 and 1,822 hours in the EU13, from 1,608 hours in France to 1,856 hours in Estonia and Hungary. These figures exclude overtime, other forms of leave and short-time working.
