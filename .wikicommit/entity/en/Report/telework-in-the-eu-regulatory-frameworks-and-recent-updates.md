Telework in the EU: Regulatory frameworks and recent updates is a research report published in 2022 by Eurofound, the European Foundation for the Improvement of Living and Working Conditions. It starts from the observation that telework, which before the pandemic was mostly an occasional or partial pattern among a small share of highly skilled professionals and managers, became the norm during the COVID-19 pandemic for all jobs in which it was technically feasible, and that a considerable proportion of employees are expected to go on teleworking afterwards. It analyses how telework is regulated in the 27 EU Member States and Norway, what changed in legislation and collective agreements since the pandemic began, and to what extent the known drawbacks of telework — extended availability and workload, blurred boundaries between work and private life, isolation and informal overtime — have been addressed.

The report is based mainly on contributions from the Network of Eurofound Correspondents, who gathered information on telework legislation and collective agreements in every country covered, complemented by desk research framing the analysis against EU regulation, chiefly the [[Legislation/eu-framework-agreement-on-telework]]. Provisions of a purely temporary, pandemic-related scope are excluded, and the account of pending legislation reflects the situation in the second quarter of 2022.

## Details

**Regulatory clusters.** Most countries have a statutory definition and specific legislation on telework; the others (Cyprus, Denmark, Finland, Ireland, Norway and Sweden) deal with it through collective bargaining or through general laws on data protection, safety and health, working time and labour. Combining the role of legislation with that of social dialogue, the report identifies six clusters: corporatist-framed governance (Belgium, France and Luxembourg, where the EU agreement was implemented through national cross-sectoral agreements extended to all employees by decree); multi-employer-framed governance (Austria and the Netherlands, with broad legislation and a prominent role for sectoral bargaining); a southern European cluster (Italy and Spain, and Greece, Portugal and Slovenia); voluntary associational governance (the Nordic countries, where collective bargaining and informal agreements based on trust matter most); market-oriented governance (Cyprus and Ireland, where telework is left mainly to employers' decisions and individual agreements); and state-centred governance (the central and eastern European countries, where legislation dominates and collective bargaining plays only a marginal role). Germany does not fit the typology: its telework legislation covers only home-based telework and few sectoral agreements deal with it, but works councils' co-determination rights — strengthened for mobile work by the Works Council Modernisation Act in June 2021 — compensate at company level.

**Changes during the pandemic.** Permanent legislative reforms were passed in Austria, Greece, Latvia, Portugal, Romania, Slovakia and Spain. The most comprehensive, measured against ten regulatory dimensions, were Spain's (all ten), Portugal's (nine) and Slovakia's (seven); the narrowest were those of Austria and Greece (four each), Romania (three) and Latvia (one). Social partners were involved through tripartite agreement or consultation in Austria, Portugal, Slovakia and Spain, while the reforms in Greece, Latvia and Romania were designed unilaterally. Bills were under discussion in Germany, Ireland and Luxembourg in the second quarter of 2022. National-level cross-industry agreements on telework exist in seven of the 28 countries — Belgium, Estonia, France, Greece, Italy, Luxembourg and Spain — and binding agreements were newly negotiated or updated during the pandemic in Belgium (Collective Labour Agreement No. 149), France (the cross-industry agreement of 26 November 2020) and Luxembourg (made binding on all private-sector companies by Grand-Ducal regulation in January 2021).

**Definitions and the telework regime.** Of the 22 countries with a statutory definition, 11 cover only regular or predominant telework, and only Belgium, France, Italy and Luxembourg explicitly distinguish and regulate occasional telework; how regularity is measured differs, by a number of days in some countries and a share of working time in others (see [[DefinedTerm/working-from-home]]). In 17 of the 22, the telework arrangement must be set out in the employment contract or a written agreement. Access rests everywhere on agreement between employer and employee, and only France, Lithuania and Portugal require an employer to justify a decision on a telework request, which the report treats as recognising a right to request (see [[DefinedTerm/right-to-request-flexible-working-arrangements]]). Bulgaria, Malta and Romania protect the employee's right to refuse telework, and a right to return to the previous arrangement is explicit in the legislation of ten countries. Only Hungary and Portugal set any limit on the frequency or duration of telework.

**Working time and the right to disconnect.** The report classifies countries by how their regulation handles flexible working time, work–life balance, the monitoring of working time and the [[DefinedTerm/right-to-disconnect]]: an only-promoting approach (Hungary, the Netherlands, Poland and Romania), which stresses flexibility without dealing with its negative effects; an only-partial-protection approach (Austria, Croatia, Finland, Malta and Slovenia), which regulates the recording and monitoring of working time but has no right to disconnect; a balanced promote–protect approach with light (Bulgaria, Czechia and Lithuania) or strong protection (Belgium, France, Italy, Luxembourg, Portugal, Slovakia and Spain); general legislation only (Estonia, Germany and Latvia); and no specific legislation (Cyprus, Denmark, Ireland, Norway and Sweden). Only some countries regulate [[DefinedTerm/working-time-recording]] for teleworkers, and the number of countries with a right to disconnect in national legislation doubled during the pandemic.

**Occupational safety and health.** General OSH rules apply to teleworkers, so the employer remains responsible for risk assessment, but only a few countries make a risk assessment a pre-condition for telework, and access to a teleworker's home by employers, workers' representatives or labour inspectors is generally subject to the worker's consent; Bulgaria and Portugal are exceptions, Portugal allowing the employer access between 9:00 and 19:00 in the presence of the worker or someone they designate. Citing EU-OSHA's ESENER survey, the report notes that only about 30% of European establishments carry out risk assessments of teleworkers' homes. Specific provisions on musculoskeletal disorders exist mainly in western Member States, while more countries regulate [[DefinedTerm/psychosocial-risks]], mostly by targeting isolation — for instance through periodic meetings at the employer's premises — rather than work intensification, overtime or irregular schedules.

**Compensation for expenses.** Following the EU agreement, 16 of the 22 countries with specific legislation make the employer responsible for providing, installing and maintaining equipment or for compensating the related expenses; in Austria, for example, an employee who uses their own equipment is entitled to a flat-rate reimbursement of up to €300, provided they work in their home office for at least 26 days a year. Czechia and the Netherlands have no specific rules, and countries differ on whether energy and communication costs are covered and whether compensation is paid by the employer or through the tax and social security systems.

**Collective agreements.** A sample of 99 sectoral agreements from 18 Member States shows telework provisions concentrated in manufacturing, financial and insurance activities, and information and communications, with new agreements during the pandemic in public administration, education, and health and social work. Most deal with the formal procedure for introducing telework and with access to it; an explicit right to disconnect was found at sectoral level only in France, and some German framework agreements allow the statutory 11-hour rest period to be shortened to 9 hours in mobile work. Company-level agreements, from a sample of 39, most often set the frequency of telework, access criteria and monthly compensation, which ranged from €10 to €129.

**Impact and social partners' views.** Apart from Sweden, no detailed impact assessments of telework regulation were found. Survey evidence points to the link between collective bargaining and coverage: French surveys show that 57% of workers in companies with more than 500 employees are covered by a collective regulation on telework, against about 5% in companies with 10–49 employees. Social partners are most satisfied with the content of telework regulation where they are involved both in legislation and in national-level collective bargaining — Austria, Belgium, Denmark, Finland, France, Spain and Sweden — and trade unions are, as a rule, less satisfied than employer organisations.

**Conclusions and policy pointers.** The report concludes that the pandemic accelerated the regulation of telework, that one size might not fit all given the diversity of industrial relations systems, and that the EU Framework Agreement's definition of telework could be considered outdated because nearly all remote work now uses ICT and regular telework is not the only form. Its policy pointers call for EU-level social dialogue to assess whether the 2002 agreement still meets the needs of businesses and workers, shared standards to protect teleworkers equally across the EU, capacity building for social dialogue, continued monitoring of developments such as psychosocial risks and the relationship between telework and gender equality, and solutions to the tax and social security issues raised by [[DefinedTerm/cross-border-telework]].
