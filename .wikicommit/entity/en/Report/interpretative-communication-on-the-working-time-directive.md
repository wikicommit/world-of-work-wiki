This Interpretative Communication is the European Commission's guide to how the [[Legislation/working-time-directive]] (Directive 2003/88/EC) is to be read in the light of the case-law of the Court of Justice of the European Union. It was published in the Official Journal of the European Union (C 143) on 26 April 2023 as a corrected text that annuls and replaces the version published in Official Journal C 109 of 24 March 2023. It updates the Commission's 2017 Interpretative Communication to take account of the more than 30 judgments and orders the Court had issued on the Directive since then, and consolidates into one document all the relevant case-law from before and after 2017 — more than 80 judgments and orders since 1993.

The Commission addresses the Communication to national authorities, citizens and businesses, legal practitioners and social partners, with the stated aims of offering legal certainty and clarity on the obligations and flexibilities in the Directive, helping to apply it to new and flexible work arrangements, and supporting effective enforcement of its minimum standards for health and safety. It stresses that the Communication is not binding and does not create new rules: only the Court is competent to interpret EU law authoritatively. Where case-law is limited or absent, the Commission sets out its own position, basing itself on the wording, context and objectives of the Directive and, where these do not point to a specific outcome, on the in dubio pro libertate principle; these passages are identified in the text as sidelined paragraphs. The Commission presented it alongside the implementation report on the Directive's transposition required by Article 24.

## Details

The Communication frames the update against changes in the world of work since the first working time directive was adopted in 1993, when employment relationships were more homogeneous and the "9 to 5" working day more standard. It points to remote, mobile, on-demand and platform work and multiple employment; to digitalisation, which it says blurs the distinction between work and rest and fragments work in place and time while creating new ways to monitor working time; and to the COVID-19 pandemic, which, citing Eurofound, it describes as sharply increasing home-based telework and possibly marking the start of a lasting trend towards [[DefinedTerm/hybrid-work]].

It singles out as the most significant judgments since 2017 those on whether stand-by duty counts as working time or rest ([[DefinedTerm/on-call-and-stand-by-time]]); a 2019 ruling requiring Member States to make employers set up a system measuring each worker's daily working time ([[DefinedTerm/working-time-recording]]); a 2021 ruling that the minimum daily rest period applies to several employment contracts with the same employer taken as a whole; and a 2021 ruling confirming that the Directive in principle applies to members of the armed forces, while certain military activities can be excluded.

The body follows the order of the Directive's chapters and articles. After an introduction and a chapter on the Directive's legal basis and health-and-safety purpose, it covers the Directive's personal scope (the EU-law concept of [[DefinedTerm/worker-eu-law]], and concurrent contracts), its material scope across sectors and the limited exclusions for certain public service and military activities, and what the Directive does not govern, such as pay. It then covers the definitions of [[DefinedTerm/working-time-and-rest-period]], night time and night work, [[DefinedTerm/shift-work]], mobile workers, offshore work and adequate rest; minimum daily rest, breaks and weekly rest ([[DefinedTerm/rest-breaks-at-work]]); the 48-hour limit on average weekly working time ([[DefinedTerm/maximum-weekly-working-hours]]); paid annual leave ([[DefinedTerm/statutory-annual-leave]]); [[DefinedTerm/night-work]], shift work and patterns of work; and the Directive's derogations, including the one for autonomous workers, those requiring equivalent compensatory rest, the individual [[DefinedTerm/working-time-opt-out]] and the special regimes for mobile, offshore and sea-fishing workers. It does not deal with the expired derogations for doctors in training or with the Directive's procedural articles.

In its conclusion the Commission describes the Directive as a complex but flexible instrument whose aim is to protect workers' health and safety through minimum requirements that should not be subordinated to purely economic considerations. Because the Directive must be transposed into national law, it is first and foremost for the Member States to apply its protective rules, use the flexibility it offers or adopt more favourable provisions; the guidance in the Communication remains subject to further development by the Court.
