*Distribution of weekly working hours, 1976 to 2022* is an article in Statistics Canada's *Quality of Employment in Canada* series (catalogue no. 14-28-0001-X), released on 13 June 2023. It presents the distribution of weekly working hours indicator: the number of employed persons whose [[DefinedTerm/usual-hours-of-work]] in all jobs fall into standardized bands, expressed as a percentage of all employed persons. The analysis uses annual averages from the [[Dataset/canadian-labour-force-survey]].

Statistics Canada presents the bands as a complement to the basic split between part-time (less than 30 hours a week) and full-time (30 hours or more) work, giving more insight into the schedules found in the labour market, particularly for people working very short or very long hours. It notes that the UNECE Expert Group on Quality of Employment recommends bands of 4 or 5 hours, but uses a smaller number of bands to ease interpretation in the Canadian context: less than 15 hours, 15 to less than 30, 30 to less than 35, 35 to less than 40, 40 to less than 45, 45 to less than 50, and 50 or more. It links the breakdown to work-life balance: short hours may indicate underemployment or a choice to devote time to other activities, while very long hours may make it difficult to balance work and family life, lowering overall well-being.

## Details

**Historical trends.** In 1976 nearly half (48.1%) of workers usually worked 40 to less than 45 hours a week. That share fell to 38.5% in 1994 and has been relatively stable since, while the share working part-time rose from 12.3% in 1976 to 18.2% in 1996. From 1997 to 2022 the share working 35 to less than 40 hours increased from 19.8% to 23.0%, and the share working long hours declined. The share of women working part-time fell from 27.8% in 1997 to 23.2% in 2022 — and among non-student women aged 15 to 64 it fell by 6.8 percentage points to 16.7% — while the share of men working less than 30 hours rose by 1.8 points to 11.8%. Statistics Canada suggests the change in [[DefinedTerm/part-time-employment]] may be related, at least in part, to the share of students among the employed.

**2022 snapshot.** Working 40 to less than 45 hours remained the most common schedule (41.0%), followed by 35 to less than 40 hours (23.0%) and 15 to less than 30 hours (11.7%); less than 15 hours (5.6%) and 45 to less than 50 hours (3.4%) were the least common. The shares working less than 15 hours and 15 to less than 30 hours were highest among youth aged 15 to 24 (19.1% and 28.4% of their employment), and employed youth who were not students were also more likely to work such short hours than workers aged 25 to 54. Hours of 35 to less than 40 were most common among core-aged women and women aged 55 and older, while core-aged men were the group most likely to work 40 to less than 45 hours (54.1%), showing that even within full-time employment a higher proportion of women worked shorter hours than men.

**Self-employment.** Hours were more polarized among the self-employed. Looking at the main job only, a third (33.4%) of self-employed workers usually worked 35 to less than 45 hours a week, against over two-thirds (70.4%) of employees; the self-employed were over six times more likely to work 50 hours or more (24.4% against 3.7%) and nearly twice as likely to work less than 15 hours (10.1% against 5.2%) ([[DefinedTerm/self-employment]]).

**Measurement.** Data before 1997 are not directly comparable with later data. From January 1997 usual hours for employees refer to normal paid or contract hours, not counting [[DefinedTerm/overtime]] (the definition is unchanged for the self-employed and unpaid family workers), the upper limit for responses was raised from 99 to 168 hours a week, and for people who changed jobs during the reference week, total usual hours refer to the new or main job rather than the sum of the main and second jobs.
