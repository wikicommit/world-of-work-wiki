Langfristige Folgen der Covid-19-Pandemie für Wirtschaft, Branchen und Berufe ("Long-term consequences of the COVID-19 pandemic for the economy, sectors and occupations") is a research report published on 1 March 2021 by the Institut für Arbeitsmarkt- und Berufsforschung (IAB) of Germany's Federal Employment Agency, written by researchers from the IAB, the Bundesinstitut für Berufsbildung (BIBB) and the Gesellschaft für Wirtschaftliche Strukturforschung (GWS). It belongs to the BIBB-IAB Qualification and Occupational Projections (the QuBe project), which project the German labour market to 2040 in a baseline projection that already includes the short-term effects of the pandemic but not any lasting changes in behaviour.

The report asks what would happen if ways of working, producing and consuming that were adopted, often by force, during the pandemic were kept after it. It builds a "post-coronavirus scenario" from a set of assumptions, feeds them into the QuBe project's QINFORGE model and compares the result with the baseline. The authors stress that the scenario describes "one" possible space of outcomes rather than "the" space, and that the quantitative size of several assumptions is very uncertain.

## Details

**Assumptions.** The largest group concerns [[DefinedTerm/working-from-home]] and video conferencing, which the report sees as advantageous to both workers and companies because they save travel time and costs. It assumes that about 45% of all workers would work from home, on average one day a week more than before, cutting their commuting trips by 20% and, since commuting accounts for about half of travel, reducing household spending on vehicles, fuel, repairs and local public transport by 4.5% by 2025. Labour productivity is assumed unchanged, because possible gains from less commuting and the doubts companies voice about performance at home could not be weighed against each other. Companies in the sectors concerned are assumed to cut their office space by ten percentage points by 2030, and one in five business trips — with the associated business meals and hotel stays — to disappear, with investment shifted from company cars to information and communication technology. Further assumptions cover a one-off jump in online retail in 2020, a 10% wage rise by 2023 in food manufacturing and agriculture as subcontracted meat-processing workers catch up with other employees, a 16% long-term reduction in air traffic and aircraft construction, more stockholding and more diversified (and so more expensive) supply chains in manufacturing, more domestic investment in medicine production, and better labour-market matching because home working widens the search radius of employers and job seekers. The rest of the world is assumed to behave like Germany.

**Results.** GDP is lower than in the baseline in the medium term, with the largest gap in 2025, but slightly higher by 2040. Labour demand is about 200,000 employed persons below the baseline in 2025 and 210,000 below it in 2040, while labour supply falls most in 2026, by about 110,000 people, and is 30,000 lower in 2040. Comparing labour demand across 9,072 combinations of 63 sectors and 144 occupations in 2040, about 320,000 jobs that exist in the baseline do not exist in the scenario and about 100,000 new ones do; counted cumulatively across the sub-scenarios, job turnover reaches about 800,000. Retail, other business services — where the report points to temporary agency work, which is classified there — and hospitality lose most jobs — the last mainly through fewer business trips — while public administration, IT and information services and the manufacture of computers, electronic and optical products gain. Among occupations, sales, cleaning and catering jobs decline, with cleaning hit by smaller office space and fewer business trips, while administrative, IT and software development occupations gain. Of the sub-scenarios, the diversification of supply chains has the strongest negative effect on employment and the trend towards home working and video conferencing the most positive.

The report concludes that home working and the wider search radius could bring major changes for particular sectors and occupations and calls for further research, including a pandemic-informed look at individual [[DefinedTerm/work-life-balance]], whose individual opportunities and risks it did not examine.
