*Health and safety at work: Summary statistics for Great Britain 2025* is a twelve-page summary published by the Health and Safety Executive (HSE) in November 2025, with the next update due in November 2026. It brings together HSE's key figures on work-related ill health, workplace injury, occupational lung disease and the costs of these to Britain, and compares industries and large European economies. With the exception of the international comparisons, all figures in it are Accredited Official Statistics, a subset of official statistics independently reviewed by the Office for Statistics Regulation.

Its headline figures for 2024/25 are 1.9 million workers suffering from work-related ill health (new or long-standing), of whom 1.0 million had work-related stress, depression or anxiety and 0.5 million a work-related musculoskeletal disorder; 124 workers killed in work-related accidents; 0.7 million workers sustaining a non-fatal workplace injury according to self-reports; 59,219 employee non-fatal injuries reported by employers under RIDDOR; and 40.1 million working days lost to work-related ill health and non-fatal injury.

## Details

**Work-related ill health.** Of the 1.9 million cases, stress, depression or anxiety accounted for 52% (964,000), musculoskeletal disorders for 27% (511,000) and other illnesses for 21% (392,000); 730,000 workers had a new case in 2024/25, and 35.7 million working days were lost. Before the coronavirus pandemic the rate of self-reported work-related ill health had been broadly flat, and the rates in each of the latest three years are higher than the 2018/19 pre-pandemic level (see [[DefinedTerm/work-related-stress]] and [[DefinedTerm/work-related-musculoskeletal-disorders]]).

**Occupational lung disease.** An estimated 13,000 deaths each year are linked to past exposure at work, primarily to chemicals or dust, and occupational lung diseases account for around 11,000 of them; there were 2,218 mesothelioma deaths in 2023, with a similar number of lung cancer deaths linked to past exposure to asbestos.

**Workplace injury.** Of the estimated 680,000 self-reported non-fatal injuries, 18% involved over 7 days' absence and 82% up to 7 days. Among employee injuries reported by employers, the most common kinds were slips, trips or falls on the same level (30%) and handling, lifting or carrying (17%). Over the long term the rate of fatal injury to workers has shown a downward trend, though it has been broadly flat over the last decade or so, and 4.4 million working days were lost to non-fatal injuries.

**Costs.** Workplace injury and new cases of work-related ill health, excluding long-latency illness such as cancer, cost Britain an estimated £22.9 billion in 2023/24 — £16.4 billion from ill health and £6.5 billion from injury — including both financial costs and a monetary valuation of human costs. Individuals bore £13.4 billion of the total, government £5.2 billion and employers £4.3 billion.

**Industries and international comparisons.** Using averages over 2022/23–2024/25, public administration and defence, human health and social work, and education had statistically significantly higher rates of work-related ill health than the all-industry average, while accommodation and food service activities, construction, transportation and storage, and wholesale and retail trade had significantly higher non-fatal injury rates. HSE reports that the UK consistently has one of the lowest rates of fatal injury across Europe: in 2018 its standardised rate was of a similar order to Germany's and lower than those of France, Spain, Italy, Poland and the EU-27 average.

**Sources.** Ill-health and self-reported injury estimates come from questions HSE commissions in the Office for National Statistics' Labour Force Survey, which surveys around 31,000 households each quarter; employer-reported injuries come from the Reporting of Injuries, Diseases and Dangerous Occurrences Regulations (RIDDOR); deaths from death certificates and epidemiological estimates; and costs from HSE's Costs to Britain Model. Data for 2019/20 to 2021/22 are affected by the coronavirus pandemic.
