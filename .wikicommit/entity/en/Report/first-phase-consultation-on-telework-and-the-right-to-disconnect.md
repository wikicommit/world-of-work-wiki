This consultation document, issued by the European Commission in Brussels on 30 April 2024, opened the first phase of consultation of the EU social partners under Article 154 TFEU on possible EU action on telework and workers' [[DefinedTerm/right-to-disconnect]]. It follows up the European Parliament's resolution of 21 January 2021 under Article 225 TFEU, which called for a directive on minimum standards for the right to disconnect and a legislative framework for remote work. The Commission first invited the social partners to find agreed solutions: the EU cross-industry social partners began negotiations on 4 October 2022 to update their 2002 Framework Agreement on Telework into a legally binding agreement, but informed the Commission on 17 November 2023 that the talks had ended without agreement.

The document sets out the background, opportunities and challenges of telework and of an "always-on" work culture, the existing EU and national rules, and possible areas and instruments for EU action. It asks the social partners three questions: whether the opportunities and challenges have been correctly identified, whether EU action is needed and with what scope, and whether the potential areas of action are comprehensive.

## Details

**Background.** The Commission links digitalisation and ICT-based working to "extended availability" — receiving and acting on work requests during rest time — and so to an always-on culture. It cites EU Labour Force Survey data that in 2019, 14% of workers (other than the self-employed) were contacted several times for work purposes outside working hours and 22% occasionally, and that the share of employees working from home usually or sometimes rose from 11.1% in 2019 to 20% in 2022, mainly because those usually working from home rose from 3.2% to 8.7%. It expects telework to stay above pre-pandemic levels, often in hybrid form, and describes [[DefinedTerm/teleworkability]], noting that actual telework in the EU is well below the roughly 37% of jobs estimated to be teleworkable.

**Opportunities and challenges.** Telework and a right to disconnect are presented as offering flexibility, autonomy, better work–life balance and more inclusive labour markets, including for carers and people with disabilities. The challenges are grouped under employment and working conditions (overtime, the "autonomy paradox", training, costs and equipment), occupational safety and health (psychosocial risks such as isolation and anxiety; ergonomic risks, musculoskeletal disorders, headaches and eyestrain), performance and digital monitoring, equal treatment and gender, and geographical mobility and [[DefinedTerm/cross-border-telework]].

**Existing law.** No EU legislation specifically regulates telework or the right to disconnect, but relevant rules include the Working Time Directive, whose case law on on-call and standby time would bear on any right to disconnect; the Directive on Transparent and Predictable Working Conditions; the OSH Framework Directive; the Display Screen Equipment and Workplace Directives, whose application to laptops, tablets, smartphones and home workplaces is unclear; the GDPR; and the Work–life Balance Directive's right to request flexible working. Nationally, the right to disconnect was regulated in 11 Member States with differing definitions, scopes, implementation methods and sanctions, and telework is regulated in all Member States by statute or, in Denmark, Finland and Sweden, by collective agreement.

**Possible EU action.** The Commission considers that an EU initiative introducing a right to disconnect and ensuring adequate working conditions in telework could be appropriate. Possible areas include establishing the right to disconnect with due regard to on-call and standby time; ensuring teleworkers are not treated less favourably, with rules on monitoring, equipment and compensation of costs; assessing psychosocial, ergonomic and physical risks; collective information and consultation rights; information to workers; the role of social partners; and enforcement. Instruments could be non-binding (a communication or recommendation), a directive based on Article 153 TFEU, or a combination.
