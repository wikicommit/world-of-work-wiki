Older Women and Unpaid Caregiving in the U.S. is an issue brief published in November 2023 by the U.S. Department of Labor. It describes older women as essential pillars of the U.S. care infrastructure: they are concentrated in many paid caregiving occupations and also provide much of the country's unpaid care. Its figures on unpaid care come from the [[Dataset/american-time-use-survey]] for 2017–2019 and 2021–2022, pooled to give sufficient sample sizes. Its childcare and adult-care figures count only care provided as a primary activity, while its eldercare figures also count secondary care given while doing something else; a spotlight on grandmothers raising grandchildren uses 2021 American Community Survey data instead.

Across a year, the average U.S. woman aged 55 or older provides 182 hours of unpaid care to family and friends — more than five weeks of full-time (35 hours a week) work. These women account for 35.3% of everyone providing unpaid [[DefinedTerm/eldercare]] on a given day, 25.6% of those providing any unpaid adult care, and about 8% of those providing unpaid childcare.

## Details

**How many provide care.** On an average day 23.5% of women aged 55 or older provide some form of unpaid care, and those who do spend on average 2 hours 11 minutes on it; with 52 million women in this age group, the brief puts the total at 26.6 million hours of unpaid care a day. Some 9.5% care for a minor child and 15.6% for an adult, such as a family member with disabilities, a spouse recovering from surgery or parents with aging-related illnesses. The comparable figure for men aged 55 or older is 17.6%.

**Differences among older women.** Employed and non-employed women are about equally likely to provide care on an average day (23.2% and 23.7%); employed caregivers spend 1 hour 59 minutes on it in addition to their paid work, against 2 hours 18 minutes for those not employed. Women living with a spouse or partner provide care more often than those without one (26.2% against 20.2%), mostly because many care for that spouse or partner. Caregiving is markedly less common at 75 or older (17.5%) than at 55 to 74 (about one in four), largely because fewer of the oldest women care for children, and less common among women with a disability (17.3%) than among those without (25.1%). Among the roughly 2% of women aged 55 or older living with their own minor child, 55.3% provide care on an average day, and 6.8% care for both a child and an adult.

**Eldercare and grandchildren.** On an average day 8.8% of women aged 55 or older support someone with a chronic, age-related condition, and for many who do this is frequent: 28.3% do so daily and 38.6% at least weekly. Separately, 1.32 million women were raising at least one grandchild in 2021; see [[DefinedTerm/grandparents-raising-grandchildren]].

**Costs to the caregiver.** The brief argues that care underpins the rest of the economy by allowing people with family responsibilities to work, citing Women's Bureau analyses that up to 13% of women aged 55 or older who left the workforce in the first year of the pandemic did so to provide care. It also stresses the cost to caregivers of reduced hours, earnings and retirement income, and says caregiving is a significant contributing factor in older women's especially high likelihood of living in poverty; see [[DefinedTerm/caregiving-penalty]]. The brief closes by describing the Biden-Harris Administration's efforts to expand access to affordable care, improve job quality among paid caregivers and support family caregivers, including a proposal for national paid leave.
