*Panorama Laboral 2024: América Latina y el Caribe* ("Labour Overview 2024: Latin America and the Caribbean") is an International Labour Organization review of the region's labour market; this page covers its executive summary (*Resumen ejecutivo*), published in Spanish under the ILO Regional Office for Latin America and the Caribbean. Five years after the COVID-19 pandemic began, it finds relative stability in the region's main labour indicators in 2024: the employment rate rose slightly, labour force participation held steady though still below 2019 levels, and the regional [[DefinedTerm/unemployment-rate]] kept falling.

Over the longer term, however, the summary stresses that the region has gone more than a decade without significant labour progress, since neither participation nor employment has substantially exceeded 2012 levels. On job quality, [[DefinedTerm/informal-employment]] declined and real wages improved, but only slightly and not enough to close historic decent work gaps; marked differences between countries persist, and gaps between men and women and between young people and adults remain a persistent challenge.

## Details

**Economic context.** The region kept to its recovery path at an ever slower pace. With world growth estimated at 3.2 per cent in 2024, the region is put at 2.1 per cent according to the IMF and 2.2 per cent according to ECLAC; if confirmed, average annual growth of 1 per cent between 2015 and 2024 would mean stagnant GDP per capita over the period, well below the roughly 3 per cent a year observed between 1990 and 2010. Inflation, though above pre-pandemic levels, continued to fall.

**Labour market.** Indicators stabilised in 2024, consolidating the full recovery from the pandemic reached in 2023. The employment rate rose by 0.5 percentage points to 58.9 per cent, participation was 62.7 per cent and unemployment fell to 6.1 per cent. Recovery was uneven: in eight of the countries considered the employment rate in the second quarter of 2024 was still below 2019, and only six had reached or exceeded 2019 participation levels, although unemployment fell in most. Urban employment exceeded 2019 levels while rural recovery remained insufficient; rural participation was 3.2 percentage points below urban, urban unemployment fell from 9.6 per cent in 2019 to 6.4 per cent, and rural unemployment from 6.2 to 4.4 per cent. Wage employment grew faster (2.9 per cent on average between the first quarters of 2024) than non-wage employment (0.7 per cent). Employment grew or held steady in every sector except agriculture, with notable rises in transport, financial services and health services, while paid domestic work still lagged its level of five years earlier.

**Informality and gaps.** The labour informality rate stood at 47.6 per cent in mid-2024, down slightly from 48 per cent in 2023 and 48.8 per cent in 2019. As in every year since the pandemic, job recovery in several countries was driven by informal employment: outside Argentina, Brazil and Mexico, informal jobs accounted for between 48 and 70 per cent of the net increase in employment over the past year. In the second quarter of 2024 women's participation rate was 52.1 per cent against 74.3 per cent for men, their employment rate 48.4 per cent against 70.4 per cent, and their unemployment rate 7.2 per cent against 5.2 per cent; the gaps have narrowed, but slowly. Youth unemployment fell from 14.5 per cent in 2023 to 13.8 per cent in 2024 but remains almost three times the adult rate.

**Wages.** Inflation continued to erode average real labour incomes. In more than half of the countries analysed, real hourly wages in 2024 were below or similar to their pre-pandemic level — lower in Argentina and Costa Rica, similar in Brazil, Chile and the Dominican Republic. Only 5 of 17 countries had a real [[DefinedTerm/minimum-wage]] in 2024 more than 10 per cent above 2019; Mexico, which has pursued a policy of raising the minimum wage's purchasing power, also saw average real wages rise.

**Outlook and special topics.** Unemployment is projected at between 5.8 and 6.2 per cent in 2025, and the summary warns that moderate growth and a lack of structural progress in job creation may prolong recent dynamics. It calls for policies that foster formalisation and [[DefinedTerm/decent-work]], supported by effective social dialogue. Its two special topics are the gender wage gap, which it presents as requiring concrete measures to close structural gaps in pay and working conditions between women and men (see [[DefinedTerm/gender-pay-gap]]), and youth employment, for which it calls for strategies easing young people's transition from education to work and from the informal economy to formal jobs, including training in digital skills.
