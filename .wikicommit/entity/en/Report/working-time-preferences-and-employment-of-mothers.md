Arbeitszeitwünsche und Erwerbstätigkeit von Müttern ("Working-time preferences and the employment of mothers") is a short report by two researchers at the Institut für Arbeitsmarkt- und Berufsforschung (IAB), published on 11 April 2017 as IAB-Kurzbericht 10/2017. It starts from the observation that paid work and housework and childcare are still divided unequally between the sexes in Germany, and that raising the employment of women and mothers is a political aim — to improve families' finances, strengthen women's financial independence and help meet labour demand. Whether that aim can be reached, it argues, depends not only on structural conditions such as childcare but also on people's attitudes to family and work.

The report therefore asks how the working hours of men and women vary with family context, what hours they would ideally work, and — to judge whether there is unused employment potential among mothers — at what age of the child women think a mother can return to work and a child can be cared for outside the family. It uses wave 5 (2011) of the [[Dataset/labour-market-and-social-security-panel]] (PASS), the only wave that asked about desired hours and these attitudes alongside actual hours, for 6,521 women and 5,620 men aged 18 to 60.

## Details

**Employment and desired hours.** In 2011 the employment rate was 68% for women and 79% for men aged 18 to 60. Women's employment varies strongly with household situation — lowest among women with children under 3, rising with the child's age to the level of women without children — while men's hardly does; employed mothers work reduced hours more often than women without children, while fathers often keep working full-time. Women would on average like to work 28 hours a week and men 35. Among employed women, desired hours range from 22 hours (women with a partner and children aged 3 to under 6) to 32 hours (single women); single mothers would like about 30 hours and mothers with a partner just under 25. Women thus tend to want extended part-time rather than full-time work, whereas men's ideal is a 35-hour week whatever their household situation. No differences in desired hours were found between employed and non-employed people.

**Gaps between wishes and reality.** Treating desired and actual hours within plus or minus 4 hours as a match, almost two-thirds of employed men would like to cut their hours by more than 4 hours, 26% have a match and only 9% want more hours. Among employed women, 45% would like to work more than 4 hours less (just under 50% of those without children) and 26% would like to work more. The wish to work more is clearly stronger among mothers: 36% of employed mothers in couples with children under 3 and 44% of single parents with children under 6 would like to increase their hours by more than 4, which the report reads as part-time work that is rather involuntary and as working-time potential that better reconciliation of work and family could tap — a [[DefinedTerm/working-time-mismatch]] in both directions. That potential is limited, it notes, because women's ideal is not full-time work and because wishes to reduce hours offset wishes to increase them. Except for mothers with a partner and children aged 3 to under 6, at least 90% of non-employed women in every household type would like to be employed.

**Attitudes to mothers' employment.** Women on average think a mother can return to work for at least 15 hours a week when her child is 3 years and 2 months old, and full-time when the child is 7. Views on full-time work differ more by household situation than views on part-time work: 29% of women in couples with children under 3, 21% of those with children aged 3 to under 6 and 22% of single parents with preschool children can imagine a mother working full-time before the child is 3, while most single women and women with a partner and older children put the ideal age at 6 or older. The pattern holds in a regression controlling for employment status, receipt of unemployment benefit II, education, migration background, age and region.

**Attitudes to childcare outside the family.** The report notes that in 2011 25% of children under 3 in Germany were in formal childcare (50% in the East, 20% in the West), and that over 92% of children aged 3 to 6 were. Women on average consider care outside the family for a few hours a day appropriate from 2 years and 7 months — slightly before kindergarten age — with little variation between household types, but full-day care (at least 35 hours a week) only from 5 years and 4 months, with more variation: mothers of children over 6 put the age at around 6, and they also include the largest shares saying a child should not be cared for full-day outside the family at all. Almost half of women can imagine a few hours of care for a child under 3, well above the actual rate for under-3s.

**Who favours an earlier return.** Attitudes to mothers' employment and to outside care go together. Besides household situation, region is a significant factor: women in East Germany favour both a clearly earlier return to work after a birth and clearly earlier use of outside childcare than women in West Germany. Younger women (up to 40), women in employment subject to social insurance contributions (especially compared with those in marginal employment) and women with a higher level of education also favour an earlier return to full-time work and more extensive care outside the family. In every group the acceptable age for full-day care is clearly lower than the age for mothers' full-time work, which the report takes to mean that outside childcare is seen as serving other aims too, such as early-childhood education.

**Conclusions.** The authors see unused working-time potential among mothers of young children, set against a relevant group who would rather work less. Gaps may persist because mothers consider their child still too young, or because there is not enough childcare or suitable jobs; for many non-employed mothers who want work, the wish matches their own attitudes, so further expansion of public childcare could improve their chances. Long career breaks and subsequent part-time phases, they note, risk losing touch with the labour market and often bring disadvantages in career, income and old-age provision. Given the uneven supply of childcare and the larger gap between need and provision in the West, they see further expansion as particularly sensible there. They also see first pointers for the debate on a "family working time" (Familienarbeitszeit) that would let both parents work reduced full-time hours, since women aspire to extended part-time and men to a 35-hour week.
