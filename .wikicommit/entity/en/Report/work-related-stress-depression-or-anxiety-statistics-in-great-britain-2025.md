*Work-related stress, depression or anxiety statistics in Great Britain, 2025* is an annual statistical report published by the Health and Safety Executive (HSE) on 20 November 2025, covering data up to March 2025. It forms part of HSE's suite of Accredited Official Statistics. It defines [[DefinedTerm/work-related-stress]] as a harmful reaction that people have to undue pressures and demands placed on them at work, and notes that stress is by its very nature difficult to measure. Its preferred data source is workers' own reports in the Labour Force Survey (LFS), a national household survey run by the Office for National Statistics in which HSE commissions annual questions on work-related illness; historic data from THOR-GP, a scheme in which general practitioners reported newly diagnosed cases of work-related ill health, add a general practitioner's perspective on causes, although the two sources may reflect different perceptions of whether a case is work-related.

The report estimates that 964,000 workers were suffering from work-related stress, depression or anxiety (new or long-standing) in 2024/25, a prevalence of 2,770 per 100,000 workers and a statistically significant increase on the previous year, resulting in 22.1 million lost working days. Stress, depression or anxiety accounted for 52% of all work-related ill health and 62% of all working days lost to work-related ill health.

## Details

**Scale and trend.** There were 409,000 new cases in 2024/25, an incidence of 1,180 per 100,000 workers, and an average of 22.9 days lost per case. In the years just before the coronavirus pandemic the rate of self-reported work-related stress, depression or anxiety had shown signs of increasing, and the rates in each of the latest three years are higher than the 2018/19 pre-pandemic level. Working days lost per worker showed no clear trend before the pandemic; the current rate is higher than the 2018/19 level.

**Industry and occupation.** Averaged over 2022/23–2024/25, public administration and defence; compulsory social security (3,470 cases per 100,000 workers), human health and social work activities (2,830) and education (2,620) had significantly higher rates than the all-industry average of 2,040. Associate professional occupations (3,020) and professional occupations (2,730) had significantly higher rates than all occupations, as did smaller groups including health professionals, teaching and other educational professionals, health and social care associate professionals, protective service occupations, and business and public service associate professionals. HSE notes that these occupations often involve high levels of public contact or interaction and that many are largely within the public sector.

**Age, gender and workplace size.** Women overall had a significantly higher rate than all workers (3,220 against 2,580 per 100,000), and men a significantly lower one (1,980). Women aged 25–54 had significantly higher rates, and men aged 16–24 and 35 and over significantly lower ones. Small workplaces had a significantly lower rate than workplaces of all sizes, and medium and large workplaces significantly higher rates.

**Causes.** In LFS self-reports (averaged over 2009/10–2011/12), the main cause cited was workload, particularly tight deadlines, too much work or too much pressure or responsibility; other factors included a lack of managerial support, violence and bullying, organisational changes at work and role uncertainty. Mental ill health cases reported by general practitioners to THOR-GP (2013–2015) showed a similar pattern, with workload pressures the predominant precipitating factor and interpersonal relationships at work and changes at work also significant.
