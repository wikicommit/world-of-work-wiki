The *WHO guidelines on mental health at work* were published by the World Health Organization in 2022 as evidence-based global public health guidance on interventions delivered in, or related to, work. They were prepared by WHO's Department of Mental Health and Substance Use and its Department of Health, Environment and Climate Change, with technical contributions from the International Labour Organization, and were developed under the WHO handbook for guideline development, with systematic evidence reviews graded using the GRADE approach. WHO states that, to date, global evidence-based guidelines on promotion, prevention and support for mental health related to work had been lacking.

The guidelines cover civilian adults aged 18 and over in paid formal or informal work, including workers with or without known mental health conditions and people with mental health conditions or psychosocial disabilities seeking to gain or return to work. Health, humanitarian and emergency workers are treated as at-risk occupations with separate recommendations. The guidelines do not address military personnel, forced labour, trafficking, modern slavery or child labour. Their target audience is primarily those responsible for workers' health, safety and well-being, including occupational health and safety and mental health providers, employers, workers and their unions, and human resource services.

## Details

**Context.** The guidelines describe work as a social determinant of mental health: meaningful work is protective, while poor working conditions, poor working relationships or unemployment can worsen mental health. They estimate that 15% of working-age adults have a mental disorder at any point in time, and that depression and anxiety cost the global economy US$ 1 trillion each year, driven predominantly by lost productivity. They list ten categories of [[DefinedTerm/psychosocial-risks]] to mental health at work, and note that one third of the global workforce is estimated to work more than 48 hours per week.

**Recommendations.** Each recommendation is rated strong or conditional, with a certainty of evidence from very low to high:

- Organizational interventions that address psychosocial risk factors, including participatory approaches, may be considered for workers generally, and — for example reductions to workload, schedule changes, or better communication and teamwork — for health, humanitarian and emergency workers (conditional, very low certainty).
- [[DefinedTerm/reasonable-work-accommodation]] should be implemented for workers with mental health conditions, including psychosocial disabilities, in line with international human rights principles (strong, very low certainty).
- Training managers to support their workers' mental health should be delivered, both generally and for managers of health, humanitarian and emergency workers (strong, moderate certainty).
- Training workers in mental health literacy and awareness may be delivered to improve knowledge and attitudes, including stigmatizing attitudes (conditional, very low certainty).
- Individual interventions — universally delivered stress-management interventions such as those based on mindfulness or cognitive behavioural approaches, leisure-based physical activity, and psychosocial interventions or physical exercise for workers with emotional distress — may be considered (conditional, low to very low certainty).
- For people on absence associated with mental health conditions, work-directed care plus evidence-based clinical care, or clinical care alone, should be considered to reduce symptoms and days of absence (conditional, low certainty).
- Recovery-oriented strategies such as (augmented) supported employment should be made available for people with severe mental health conditions to obtain and maintain employment (strong, low certainty).

No recommendation was made for or against screening programmes during employment, because it was unclear whether their potential benefits outweigh potential harms; where screening does take place at work, the guidelines list minimum conditions such as ensured follow-up care, impartial health providers, privacy and confidentiality, and adherence to human rights principles.

**Overarching principles.** The guidelines prefer that interventions be delivered comprehensively, embedded in work health policies rather than independently of each other, and rest on the protection of people with mental health conditions from discrimination, the protection of mental health at work, and the right to decent working conditions. Workers surveyed during development were less likely to value individual interventions offered without organizational or managerial ones, out of concern that this implies they are to blame for their own mental health.

**Research gaps and updating.** The guidelines call for more and better evidence from the informal sector, small and medium enterprises and low- and middle-income countries, where little of the reviewed research was conducted, and on cost-effectiveness. They are expected to be valid for five years, and WHO and the ILO planned a joint policy brief to support implementation.
