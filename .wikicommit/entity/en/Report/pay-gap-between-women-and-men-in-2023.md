*Écart de salaire entre femmes et hommes en 2023* ("The pay gap between women and men in 2023"), subtitled "Between 1995 and 2023, the salary income gap fell by a third", is a study published by the French national statistics institute Insee on 4 March 2025 as *Insee Focus* No. 349. It measures the [[DefinedTerm/gender-pay-gap]] among employees whose main job is in France's private sector, using Insee's annual employee databases built from employers' administrative declarations and a panel that follows a sample of employees since 1976.

In 2023 women's average annual salary income (*revenu salarial*, the sum of net salaries received over the year) was 22.2% below men's: 21,340 euros against 27,430 euros. Part of the gap reflects working time: women are less often in employment over the year and more often work part-time — situations that may be chosen or not — so their average annual volume of work was 9.3% lower than men's. At equal working time, women's average net salary in full-time equivalent (*EQTP*) was still 14.2% lower. For the same job in the same establishment, the full-time-equivalent gap narrows to 3.8%.

## Details

**Trend since 1995.** The full-time-equivalent salary gap fell from 22.1% in 1995 to 14.2% in 2023, partly because of changes in the composition of private-sector jobs: women's share of managers (*cadres*), who are on average better paid, rose from 23% to 38%. The gap in annual volume of work narrowed from 14.9% to 9.3%: it rose in 1995–1999 with the spread of part-time work, mostly among women, then narrowed as women's working time rose and, from 2009, men's fell, before stabilising around 11% in 2015–2021 and falling again in 2022 and 2023. Combining both components, the salary income gap stood at about 34% between 1995 and 2001 and then declined by 0.6 points a year on average, close to 1 point a year since 2019 — a reduction of a third over 1995–2023.

**Age, occupation, firm size and sector.** On a slightly narrower field, the full-time-equivalent gap was 13.5% in 2023. It rises with age, from 4.3% among employees under 25 to 24.9% among those aged 60 or over, whereas the working-time gap is largest among the under-25s (20.6%), partly because women, who are on average more highly qualified, enter the labour market a little later; after 25 it lies between 8.5% and 9.5%. By socio-professional category, gaps are largest among manual workers for both working time (21.0%) and salary (12.9%) and smallest among clerical and service employees (0.7% and 3.6%). The salary gap grows with firm size, from 7.2% in firms with fewer than 10 employees to 17.3% in those with 5,000 or more, and is highest in information-communication, financial and real-estate services (23.3%); in construction, women's average salaries are slightly higher than men's (by 1.6%) because women are rare there except among managers, who are better paid on average.

**Occupational segregation and top pay.** Women and men do not work in the same occupations, firms or sectors — [[DefinedTerm/occupational-segregation]]. Only four occupations are common to the twenty most frequent for each sex, which account for about 38% of women's and 30% of men's employment. Women hold 42% of private-sector jobs in full-time equivalent, up to 54% around a monthly net salary of 1,410 euros, but 34% at the ninth decile and only 24% among the best-paid 1% (above 10,222 euros a month); excluding that top 1% reduces the full-time-equivalent gap from 13.5% to 9.7%.

**Parenthood.** In 2022 the gap in working time between women and men was larger among parents (11.9%) than among all private-sector employees (9.9%), and larger still among parents of a child aged 3 or under (23.1%) and of three or more children (17.7%). The full-time-equivalent salary gap rises with the number of children, from 5.8% among employees without children to 28.2% between mothers and fathers of three or more, reflecting both the drop in pay after a birth and mothers' durably slowed careers. Combined, mothers' salary income was 29.9% below fathers', and 40.9% below among parents of three or more children.
