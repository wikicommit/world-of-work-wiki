Le télétravail permet-il d’améliorer les conditions de travail des cadres ? ("Does telework improve managers' working conditions?") is a dossier published by Insee on 7 November 2019 in the Insee Références volume *L'économie et la société à l'ère du numérique*, written by two researchers from Dares, the research and statistics directorate of the French Ministry of Labour. It examines how telework relates to the working conditions of managers (*cadres*) and, more broadly, to teleworkers' social relations within their company.

Following the 2002 European framework agreement on telework ([[Legislation/eu-framework-agreement-on-telework]]), the dossier defines telework by the use of information and communication technologies and by work being done regularly away from the employer's premises when it could have been done at the usual workstation. It therefore distinguishes telework from home-based work, mobile work, work while travelling and overflow work taken home in the evening or at weekends. It defines *regular* telework as at least one day a week and *intensive* telework as two days a week or more.

The study uses the [[Dataset/sumer-survey]] and the [[Dataset/reponse-survey]], both from 2017, and covers managers in private non-agricultural establishments with more than 10 employees in metropolitan France, who made up 63% of regular teleworkers in 2017 and have near-universal access to ICT. Relationships between telework and working conditions are estimated with logit models controlling for individual and employer characteristics.

## Details

In 2017, 3.0% of all employees teleworked regularly and 4.3% a few days or half-days a month; among the managers studied, 11.1% teleworked regularly and 5.2% at least two days a week. In private establishments with more than 10 employees, a quarter of teleworkers were covered by a collective agreement, more than one in five by an individual agreement with their line management, and more than half teleworked without any formal arrangement.

The dossier's main findings are:

- **Working time.** Intensive teleworkers worked 43.0 hours a week on average against 42.4 hours for non-teleworkers, were twice as likely to work more than 50 hours a week, and had more atypical (evenings, Saturdays) and less predictable hours. They did not report a better work–life balance. Where telework was covered by an agreement — especially a collective one — long and evening hours were less frequent and satisfaction with working hours higher, although this may reflect establishments that sign such agreements offering better conditions generally.
- **Autonomy and intensity.** Intensive teleworkers more often said they could interrupt their work when they wished and that their work required a high level of skill, but they did not have more operational autonomy. Their work intensity and time pressure were not lower, and they were 1.6 times as likely to have to interrupt a task frequently for an unplanned one.
- **Cooperation.** Intensive teleworkers received less help from their superiors (67% against 77%) and colleagues (80% against 88%) and less often had sufficient information to do their job, but the friendliness of relations with colleagues, recognition, exposure to hostile behaviour and involvement in staff representation and collective action were not affected.
- **Training.** They more often felt their training was insufficient and expressed a need for more, but accessed training as much as other managers.
- **Instability.** Intensive teleworkers more often worked in establishments that had recently undergone a redundancy plan (21% against 4%), a restructuring or relocation (37% against 22%) or a takeover or change of management (37% against 18%), and 47% felt their job security was threatened against 12% of non-teleworkers.
- **Health.** Regular teleworkers more often had a disability (one in ten against 5%) and rated their health as poor; intensive teleworkers were twice as likely to show moderate or severe depressive risk (17% against 8%) and more often had at least one sick leave in the past year. The surveys cannot say whether poorer health preceded telework — telework may serve as a workplace adjustment — or followed from it.
- **Satisfaction.** Teleworkers were neither more nor less satisfied with their work than other managers: the authors suggest the advantages of telework are offset by poorer health and working conditions, factors that may partly predate telework.
