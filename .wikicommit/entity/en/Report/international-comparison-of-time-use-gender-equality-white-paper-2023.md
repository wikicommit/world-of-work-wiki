*生活時間の国際比較* ("An international comparison of time use") is Column 1 of the Reiwa 5 edition of Japan's White Paper on Gender Equality (男女共同参画白書), published by the Gender Equality Bureau of the Cabinet Office. It starts from the white paper's own finding that in Japan [[DefinedTerm/unpaid-work]] falls disproportionately on women and paid work on men, which the white paper says may be one factor holding back women's participation in society and men's involvement in housework and childcare. Using the OECD's international time-use data, the column compares 11 countries in four regions — the United States and Canada (North America); the United Kingdom, France, Germany and Italy (Europe outside the Nordic countries); Norway, Sweden and Finland (the Nordic countries); and Japan and South Korea (Asia) — to bring out what is distinctive about how Japanese women and men spend their time.

The column concludes that each country's division of time between women and men is closely bound up with its culture and people's attitudes, so there is no single right answer, but that a fair sharing of paid and unpaid work between women and men is important for promoting a gender-equal society. In Japan, it argues, men's paid working hours are remarkably long by international standards, and unless that changes it will be difficult for men to do more unpaid work; reducing men's long working hours would both make room for them to do more unpaid work and raise women's share of paid work.

## Details

**Combined working time.** The column defines working time (労働時間) as the sum of paid and unpaid working time (see [[DefinedTerm/total-work-time]]). Japan's was the second longest of the 11 countries after the United States, and this held for women and for men separately.

**Paid and unpaid work.** Japan's paid working time was the longest of the 11 countries, more than twice that of Italy, which had the shortest. Japanese men's paid working time was remarkably long compared with other countries, and Japanese women's was the second longest. Japan's unpaid working time, by contrast, was the second shortest after South Korea's, mainly because Japanese men's was exceptionally short; Japanese women's unpaid working time was not necessarily long by international standards. The column therefore attributes the length of Japanese women's and men's working time to long paid work.

**Women's share.** Measuring women's share of each type of work as women's time divided by the combined time of women and men, the column finds that paid work leans towards men and unpaid work towards women in the other countries too. Japan's imbalance was the most extreme: women accounted for 37.5% of paid working time, the smallest share of the 11 countries, and 84.6% of unpaid working time, the largest. In Sweden, Finland and Norway women's shares of both paid and unpaid work were close to half, while in Japan, South Korea and Italy paid work leaned especially strongly towards men and unpaid work towards women. The column sets this beside the Gender Gap Index published by the World Economic Forum, on which the Nordic countries ranked high and Japan, South Korea and Italy low among the 11.

**Sleep and eating.** Japanese women and men had the shortest sleep of the 11 countries, which the column infers is a consequence of their long working time. Americans of both sexes slept the longest, and also had the longest working time and the shortest time spent eating and drinking. The French spent the longest time eating and drinking and Italians the second longest, both more than twice as long as Americans. The column suggests these differences are not unrelated to food culture: the United States as the birthplace of global fast-food culture, and France's gastronomic meal and the Mediterranean diet, which includes Italian cuisine and eating habits, both inscribed on UNESCO's intangible cultural heritage lists.
