*Average hours worked and economic growth, UK: 1998 to 2022* is an article by the UK Office for National Statistics (ONS), released on 22 January 2024. It analyses the longer-term trends in average weekly hours worked in the UK, the more recent changes since the coronavirus (COVID-19) pandemic, and the effect of those changes on economic growth.

For all those in employment, average weekly hours trended down from 33.1 in 1998 to 31.8 in 2022, a fall of 1.3 hours. They dropped sharply to 29.0 in 2020 from 32.1 the year before, and by 2022 had recovered to 31.8 — still 0.3 hours lower than in 2019. The ONS notes that although the fall is less than 1% of average weekly hours, with total UK employment of 32.7 million in 2022 it is equivalent to approximately 310,000 fewer people in employment.

## Details

**Men and women.** The trends differ sharply by sex. Men's average weekly hours fell from 38.6 in 1998 to 35.3 in 2022 (3.3 hours), and were 0.9 hours lower in 2022 than in 2019. Women's rose from 26.5 to 27.9 (1.4 hours), and were 0.5 hours higher in 2022 than in 2019. The article concludes that although women still work fewer hours on average, their relative contribution to labour supply is increasing.

**Accounting for the fall.** Using [[DefinedTerm/shift-share-analysis]] of Labour Force Survey data, the article attributes almost all of the 1.3-hour fall between 1998 and 2022 to shorter hours among men working full-time (negative 1.2 hours). Men and women working part-time increased their hours slightly, and second jobs had a marginal effect. Share effects — changes in the composition of employment — contributed negative 0.3 hours by employment status. By age and sex they contributed negative 0.7 hours, over half the total, reflecting both the increasing participation of women and the ageing of the UK population, since workers aged 50 and over tend to work fewer hours than those aged 25 to 49. Between 2019 and 2022, men's full-time hours again accounted for most of the 0.3-hour fall, but the share effect by employment status was positive (0.2 hours), mainly because a larger share of women worked full-time rather than part-time — which the ONS suggests may reflect more widespread flexible working since the pandemic.

**Individual characteristics.** A regression on nearly 74,000 respondents to the [[Dataset/annual-population-survey]] in 2022 identified age, sex, marital status, nationality, housing tenure, [[DefinedTerm/self-employment]], graduate status and occupation as determinants of individual hours worked. Relative to a baseline worker, being female lowered weekly hours by six hours, being aged 65 or over by 9.3 hours, and owning a home outright by 2.4 hours, while managers and directors worked 2.8 hours more and elementary occupations 7.4 hours fewer. Comparing 2019 with 2022, those aged 65 and over and women increased their hours relative to the baseline, while the self-employed and outright homeowners reduced theirs further.

**Economic growth.** Treating GDP as output per hour multiplied by total hours worked, the article finds that changes in average hours had a large effect during the pandemic: of the approximately 10.9% contraction in GDP in 2020, 10.3 percentage points were accounted for by the drop in average hours, which the ONS links to national lockdowns and the furlough scheme. Over 1998 to 2019, however, average hours contributed only negative 0.16 percentage points a year to average annual GDP growth of 1.7%. Between 2019 and 2022 GDP grew by 1.64% despite negative contributions from average hours (0.97 percentage points) and the participation rate (1.34 percentage points), so the relative contribution of productivity increased; the article suggests a continuing recovery in average hours could support growth.
