Être parent : des cadres aux ouvrières, plus de conséquences sur l’emploi des femmes ("Being a parent: from managers to blue-collar women, greater consequences for women's employment") is an issue of the Insee Première series (No 1795), published on 6 March 2020 by two authors from the employment division of Insee, the French national statistics institute. Drawing on the 2018 Labour Force Survey and its complementary module on reconciling working and family life — a module of European initiative — it looks at people aged 25 to 49 who are employees or former employees in France excluding Mayotte, about 20,100 respondents.

The study defines family responsibilities as living with one or more children under 15 (one's own or a partner's), or regularly looking after children under 15 who do not live with one. It finds that in 2018 women with family responsibilities were less often in employment than women without them, the gap being widest among blue-collar women, while men with family responsibilities were more often employed than other men. When in work, women said about twice as often as men that being a parent had consequences for their working life — mostly on the amount or organisation of their working time — and female managers reduced their working time to look after children more often than other women employees. Women and men, by contrast, reported similar levels of difficulty reconciling work and family, and these difficulties were greatest for managers of both sexes because of their long working days.

## Details

**Employment.** Among women aged 25 to 49, 76% of those with family responsibilities were in employment against 84% of those without; for men the figures were 91% and 82%. The lower employment rate of mothers shows up mainly as inactivity (17% against 9%), while for men with family responsibilities both unemployment and inactivity are lower. The study notes that this pattern, reversed between women and men, holds after accounting for age, socio-occupational group, the partner's employment situation, and the age and number of children. The gap is widest among blue-collar women: 54% of those with family responsibilities were in employment, against 74% of those without, and 32% of them were inactive. For female managers the employment rates were 90% and 94%.

**Consequences for working life.** Among employed people with family responsibilities, 45% of women said being a parent had consequences for their working situation, nearly twice as many as men (23%). Women reported much more often that they had reduced their working time (16% against 3%), and part-time work was the main means of doing so: 72% of those who reported reducing their working time were working part-time. Overall, 31% of women employees with family responsibilities worked part-time, against 4% of men. Differences were smaller for changes to working hours (14% of women, 11% of men), which was the consequence men cited most. Consequences were reported more often when children were young: 55% of women and 27% of men with at least one child under three reported them.

The more qualified a woman's job, the more often she reported consequences: 56% of female managers against 27% of blue-collar women, with 41% of female managers having reduced or rearranged their working time against 18% of blue-collar women. The study links these differences to the characteristics of occupations. Female managers worked part-time less often (21%), but when they did it was mostly for family reasons (83%), and 44% said they could generally start later or finish earlier for family reasons. Female clerical and service employees worked part-time more often (38%), less often for family reasons (56%), and nearly a third would have liked to work more; opening-hour constraints left only 28% able to adjust their start or finish times. Blue-collar women worked part-time relatively little (25%) and had little room to adjust their hours, which the study connects to their marked withdrawal from employment when they have children. Among men, changing working hours was likewise more common among managers (20%) than blue-collar workers (5%).

**Reconciling work and family.** In work, 63% of women and 59% of men reported difficulties reconciling work and family responsibilities, and the kinds of difficulty differed little by sex. Time-related difficulties — long working days, unpredictable, fluctuating or staggered hours, and commuting — were the most cited (41% overall), work intensity and the fatigue it causes were cited by 13%, and lack of understanding from the employer or colleagues by 3%. Difficulties were more frequent with young children: 66% of employees with at least one child under three, against 54% when all children were over ten.

Managers reported difficulties most often (73%, against 63% for intermediate occupations, 58% for clerical and service employees and 53% for blue-collar workers), with 29% naming long working days as the main source; nearly half of them usually worked more than 40 hours a week. Working at home, done by 40% of managers, can increase time present with the family but also blurs the boundary between private and working life. Clerical and service employees and blue-collar workers more often cited unpredictable, fluctuating or staggered hours, in line with more frequent weekend work among employees and more night work and alternating shifts among blue-collar workers ([[DefinedTerm/atypical-working-hours]]); 22% of blue-collar women named intense or tiring work as their main difficulty. Employees who had difficulties reconciling work and family were twice as likely to want to change jobs (14% against 7%), and nearly a quarter of blue-collar women with such difficulties wanted to.
