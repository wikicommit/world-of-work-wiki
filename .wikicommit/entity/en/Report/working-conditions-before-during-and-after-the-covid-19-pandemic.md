"Arbeitsbedingungen vor, während und nach der Covid-19-Pandemie" (Working conditions before, during and after the COVID-19 pandemic) is an IAB-Kurzbericht, a short research report published on 19 March 2026 by the Institute for Employment Research (IAB) of Germany's Federal Employment Agency. It asks whether the pandemic permanently changed the world of work, as debates about accelerated digitalisation, changing places of work and new forms of work organisation had suggested. Its headline answer is that employment quality has remained largely stable.

The report follows five dimensions of employment quality — satisfaction with pay, work autonomy, career prospects, workload (time pressure and interruptions) and [[DefinedTerm/work-life-balance]] — over the whole period from before the pandemic to after it, which the authors note few earlier studies had done. It pays particular attention to employees in [[DefinedTerm/systemically-relevant-occupations]], whose working conditions were often at the centre of public debate during the pandemic.

## Details

**Data and method.** The analysis uses the IAB's Panel Labour Market and Social Security (PASS), an annual survey since 2006 drawn from population registers and from people receiving basic income support under SGB II. It covers dependent employees aged over 18, earning above the minijob threshold and not in training, observed from 2018 to 2024 — 24,040 observations in all — and divides the period into a pre-pandemic phase, the first wave, summer 2020, the Alpha wave, the Omicron wave and a post-pandemic phase from week 22 of 2022. Employees are classified as systemically relevant using the Berlin Senate's list of "first hour" systemically relevant occupations. Fixed-effects regressions compare each person with themselves before the pandemic, so that stable differences between people do not bias the estimates, with controls for interview month, interview mode and age.

**Before the pandemic.** Employees in systemically relevant occupations already reported significantly lower pay satisfaction and work autonomy than other employees; their career prospects and work–life balance were only slightly lower, and their workload slightly higher, a difference that was only weakly significant.

**Findings.** Pay satisfaction fell for both groups during the pandemic and stayed below its pre-crisis level afterwards; a comparison of nominal and real net wages suggests that real income losses had not been fully compensated, which the authors link also to the energy-price crisis triggered by Russia's war against Ukraine. Work autonomy showed no substantial change, and career prospects stayed largely stable, with only a slight, statistically insignificant decline after the pandemic. Workload diverged: other employees temporarily reported relief, whereas perceived excessive demands rose clearly for systemically relevant employees and remained significantly above the pre-crisis level after the pandemic. For this group the probability of working overtime fell at the start of the pandemic and stayed lower, while the overtime hours actually worked did not fall and later rose slightly, suggesting that overtime became concentrated on a smaller group. Work–life balance improved slightly for both groups, though only partly significantly. Among systemically relevant employees, the improvement came mainly from smaller occupational segments and from a small subgroup who could work from home — who, if they had children, could choose between emergency childcare and working at home — while the three largest segments (logistics, health, and social and cultural services) saw only temporary, mostly insignificant gains, and employees with a migration background had significantly lower work–life balance in most phases than before the pandemic.

**Conclusions.** The authors conclude that the widely discussed lasting changes to work have, as far as employment quality is concerned, largely not materialised, but that employees in systemically relevant occupations remain under greater strain. They argue for targeted measures to improve the attractiveness and working conditions of these occupations, particularly in areas with persistent shortages such as health, logistics and services.
