This Options Assessment (OA), dated 12 August 2026, accompanies the UK government's consultation on the secondary legislation that will implement the [[DefinedTerm/right-to-guaranteed-hours]] created by the [[Legislation/employment-rights-act-2025]]. The right is one of three measures — with a right to reasonable notice of shifts and a right to payment for shifts cancelled, moved or curtailed at short notice — through which the government intends to tackle [[DefinedTerm/one-sided-flexibility]] and end exploitative [[DefinedTerm/zero-hours-contract]]s. Because the primary legislation has already been passed, the OA assesses only regulatory options, and it builds on the impact assessment published when the Bill was introduced in 2024.

The OA does not set out a preferred option for most of the parameters under consultation. The government states preferences on two: an initial reference period of 12 weeks, and an hours threshold within the range of 8 to 20 hours a week. For the rest it presents the range of options and models the costs at the ends of that range, treating the main effect of the policy as a transfer of risk from workers on variable-hours contracts to employers and hirers. For the purposes of the appraisal it assumes the measure will be implemented in 2027.

## Details

**Options under consultation.**

- *Hours threshold* — the contractually guaranteed hours at or below which a worker is in scope: options from 8 to 48 hours a week in 4-hour steps, with a preference for 8 to 20. Using the Annual Survey of Hours and Earnings (ASHE) for 2025, the OA estimates that 3.3 million employee jobs would be potentially in scope at 8 hours, 5.9 million at 20 hours and 28.6 million at 48 hours. It notes that basic hours in ASHE are not necessarily guaranteed hours, so these figures are likely to be underestimates.
- *Regularity requirements* — the hours worked during a reference period must be spread over a minimum number of weeks (6, 8, 10 or all 12 weeks of a 12-week period), possibly with a second test of a minimum total of hours worked above contracted hours (fewer than 48, 48, 72 or 96 hours over 12 weeks).
- *Calculating the offer* — the mean or the median of hours worked over the reference period; whether employers may use a small adjustment margin (a fixed figure or a percentage); and whether the guaranteed hours are allocated per week, per month or over another period, set by government or chosen by employers.
- *Reference periods* — an initial period of 12, 26 or 52 weeks, and subsequent periods of 12, 26 or 52 weeks, either starting as soon as the previous one ends or with a gap between them.
- *Exemptions* — whether particular workers should be excluded, whether employers or hirers should be exempt in some circumstances, and whether the duty to make offers to agency workers should in some cases fall on the agency rather than the hirer.

Enforcement is through the individual system — Acas early conciliation and employment tribunals — rather than the [[Organization/fair-work-agency]], whose civil penalty regime covers non-payment or underpayment and so, of the zero-hours measures, reaches only payment for cancelled or curtailed shifts.

**Costs.** Over a 10-year appraisal period, the equivalent annual net direct cost to business is estimated at between £100 million and £450 million a year, and the net present social value at between −£2.3 billion and −£7.5 billion, depending on the options chosen. The monetised costs are one-off familiarisation (£60–110 million) and implementation (£260–320 million), the ongoing administration of tracking hours and making offers, and an indirect workforce-planning cost of £170–420 million a year. The OA stresses that these figures are a partial assessment: the benefits to workers — greater income security, predictability and wellbeing — and any productivity gains could not be monetised, and the loss of flexibility for employers, which it expects to be significant, is also left unmonetised.

**Who is affected.** At thresholds within the preferred range, in-scope jobs are concentrated in hospitality (54% of employee jobs at a 20-hour threshold), arts, entertainment and recreation (42%) and administrative and support services (38%), followed by retail, health and social care and education. Among jobs that are zero-hours or have 20 basic hours or fewer, 65% are held by women (against 47% of other jobs) and 23% by people aged 16–24 (against 7%). Micro businesses have the highest share of potentially in-scope jobs (42% at a 20-hour threshold), but the OA concludes that exempting small and micro businesses would significantly undermine the policy and risk creating a two-tier labour market. It also estimates an additional 500 to 900 employment tribunal cases and 1,500 to 2,700 early conciliation notifications to Acas between the 8-hour and 20-hour threshold scenarios.
