This EU-OSHA report, published in 2024, analyses the implications of digitalisation for occupational safety and health (OSH) with respect to [[DefinedTerm/psychosocial-risks]]. It draws on over one hundred EU-OSHA documents — reports, policy briefs, discussion papers and case studies — and on headline findings from EU-OSHA's OSH Pulse survey of workers (2022) and the 2019 [[Dataset/esener]]. Its contents are the author's own and do not necessarily reflect EU-OSHA's views. The analysis follows the five areas of EU-OSHA's research programme on OSH and digitalisation (2020-2023): advanced robotics and artificial intelligence (AI), smart digital systems, digital platform work, remote working, and AI for worker management.

Following EU-OSHA, the report defines psychosocial risk factors as the aspects of the design and management of work, and its social and organisational contexts, that have the potential to cause psychological or physical harm, and lists factors such as job insecurity, long or irregular hours, time pressure, excessive workload, cognitive overload, lack of autonomy, poor communication, third-party violence, harassment, a sense of unfairness, lack of training, lack of trust and changes of job role. It stresses that digitalisation can also improve working conditions — for instance robots taking over dangerous tasks or wearables giving real-time data on hazards — and that existing law, starting with Framework Directive 89/391/EEC, already requires employers to assess all risks and consult workers on new technologies.

## Details

**Survey evidence.** In OSH Pulse 2022, 37 % of respondents reported overall fatigue and 27 % stress, anxiety or depression; 46 % reported severe time pressure or work overload. Only 12 % used no digital device for work. Digital devices were used to allocate tasks, working time or shifts for 30 % of respondents, to have their performance rated by others for 27 % and to monitor their work or behaviour for a quarter, and 52 % said digital technologies determined the speed or pace of their work. A third said these technologies increased their workload, 44 % that they resulted in working alone, 37 % that they increased surveillance and 19 % that they reduced autonomy. In ESENER 2019, establishments most often reported difficult customers, patients or pupils (about 59 %) and time pressure (about 45 %) as psychosocial risk factors.

**Advanced robotics and AI.** About 5 % of OSH Pulse respondents used AI-based machines and 3 % cobots. In 16 EU-OSHA workplace case studies the most frequently reported risk was cognitive overload, followed by fear of job loss, lack of trust (too much trust can lead to "automation complacency"), deskilling and the need to reskill, and changes in job content. Risks differed by the type of task automated — cognitive or physical, routine or complex — but the solutions converged on training, worker involvement and clear communication.

**Smart digital systems.** Sensors, wearables, the internet of things, AR/VR and drones used to monitor OSH risks raise similar risks, with a stronger focus on privacy: lack of trust arising from surveillance, a sense of unfairness, higher workload and time pressure, reduced autonomy, poorer social relationships and inadequate training.

**Digital platform work.** Defined as all paid labour mediated through online platforms, platform work combines non-standard arrangements with [[DefinedTerm/algorithmic-management]] (see also [[DefinedTerm/digital-labour-platform]]). Case studies of parcel delivery, handiwork, content moderation and programming found risks common to all — professional isolation, time pressure, job and income insecurity, lack of autonomy and a sense of unfairness — and task-specific ones such as exposure to distressing content in content moderation and cognitive overload in programming. Because most platform workers are classified as self-employed, the OSH Framework Directive does not cover them, shifting risk management onto the workers.

**Remote working.** OSH Pulse found almost 30 % of people employed in the EU-27 working away from the employer's or client's premises and about 17 % working from home. The main risks were blurred work-life boundaries, extended availability and workload, working while unwell, isolation and intrusive monitoring; women who telework face more work-life conflict, time pressure and worse mental health (see [[DefinedTerm/working-from-home]] and [[DefinedTerm/hybrid-work]]).

**AI for worker management.** [[DefinedTerm/ai-based-worker-management]] (AIWM) systems were associated with time pressure, poor communication, fear of job loss — reported by 18.9 % of workplaces that use technology to monitor workers against 10.6 % of others — work intensification, cognitive overload, poor work-life balance, lack of trust and autonomy, and lack of training.

**Legislation and good practice.** The report finds no legislation specifically addressing the OSH impact of digitalisation, although existing OSH law applies to employees. It reviews the GDPR's rules on automated decision-making, the AI Act (which classes AI used in employment as high-risk but has no OSH focus), national AI strategies in Germany, France, Sweden and Norway, the 2002 Framework Agreement on Telework and post-pandemic telework reforms, the European Parliament's 2021 resolution on the [[DefinedTerm/right-to-disconnect]], and the EU directive on platform work approved in 2024 with its rebuttable [[DefinedTerm/presumption-of-employment-relationship]]. National examples include Spain's Riders' Law, Italy's Legislative Decree No. 101/2019 and the Bologna Charter, and French laws giving platform workers rights to unionise and to disconnect.

**Policy pointers.** The report calls for a proactive, human-centred ("human-in-command") prevention-through-design approach; job design that avoids constant high mental workload and builds in rest breaks; worker participation from the design stage; training and reskilling; risk assessments that cover digitalisation and regular workload assessments; flexible arrangements and the right to disconnect; promoting social interaction; transparency and fairness in digital decision-making; and filling regulatory gaps by embedding OSH in legislation and agreements on digitalisation.
