The *WHO/ILO Joint Estimates of the Work-related Burden of Disease and Injury, 2000–2016: Global Monitoring Report* was published by the World Health Organization and the International Labour Organization in 2021. It presents the first set of joint estimates the two agencies agreed in 2016 to produce with a single unified methodology — previously they had produced separate estimates with different methods and different results — and in 2019 they signed a Collaboration Agreement to produce them regularly. The report describes itself as the most comprehensive set of official estimates of the work-related burden of disease produced to date, intended for global monitoring and for identifying, planning, costing, implementing and evaluating actions to prevent exposure to occupational risk factors.

The estimates are built within the global Comparative Risk Assessment framework, linking exposure to a specific occupational risk factor to the burden of one specific disease or injury. They cover 41 pairs of occupational risk factor and health outcome: 39 established pairs, estimated from population attributable fractions derived from recent Global Burden of Disease estimates, and two newly added pairs — exposure to [[DefinedTerm/long-working-hours]] and the outcomes of ischaemic heart disease and stroke — estimated from new WHO/ILO exposure databases and systematic reviews and meta-analyses. Estimates are given for 2000, 2010 and 2016 at country, regional and global levels, disaggregated by sex and age group.

## Details

**Headline findings.** In 2016, 1.88 million deaths and 89.72 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) were attributable to the 41 pairs. Diseases accounted for 80.7% of the deaths and injuries for 19.3%; all the diseases covered are non-communicable. The risk factor with the most attributable deaths was exposure to long working hours (744 924 deaths), followed by occupational particulate matter, gases and fumes (450 381) and occupational injuries (363 283). By health outcome, the largest work-related death tolls were from chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, stroke and ischaemic heart disease. Occupational injuries caused the most DALYs, followed by long working hours and occupational ergonomic factors, the last of which accounted for 12.27 million DALYs through back and neck pain.

**Trends and inequalities.** Between 2000 and 2016 the number of work-related deaths rose by 177 914, driven mainly by long working hours, while occupational injuries contributed the largest decrease. Rates per head nevertheless fell: attributable deaths declined from 39.9 to 34.3 per 100 000 working-age population (14.2%), and DALYs by 12.9%. The burden was disproportionately large in the WHO African, South-East Asia and Western Pacific regions, among males — whose death rate was 51.4 per 100 000 working-age males against 17.2 for females — and in older age groups.

**Prevention.** For each risk factor the report outlines primary prevention, generally following the hierarchy of controls (elimination and substitution, then engineering controls, administrative controls and, as a last resort, personal protective equipment), together with ratification and implementation of ILO standards, occupational health services for all workers, and involvement of employers and workers or their representatives.

**Limitations and scope.** The estimates are not available subnationally or by occupation, industrial sector or migration status, and some rest on limited exposure data; more data from low- and middle-income countries are called for. Not all occupational risk factors have been quantified: pairs such as occupational exposure to psychosocial risk factors and mental health outcomes, biological risk factors and infectious diseases, and ambient air pollution could not be estimated in this cycle. The report proposes that the estimates could support a complementary indicator under Sustainable Development Goal target 8.8, since injuries account for only a minority of work-related deaths.
