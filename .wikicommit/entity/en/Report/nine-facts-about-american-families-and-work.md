*Nine Facts About American Families and Work* is a report by the U.S. Council of Economic Advisers (CEA), published in June 2014. It lays out nine facts which, in the CEA's words, together illustrate the changes needed to ensure long-term economic growth, maintain economic competitiveness, improve Americans' well-being and make full use of America's talent. The report starts from the observation that most workers have caregiving responsibilities for others — children, elderly parents, other relatives, spouses, or adult children with disabilities, including returning veterans — and argues that balancing breadwinning and caregiving without supportive work–family policies leaves too many families stressed, exhausted and burdened by work–family conflict.

The report rests mainly on CEA analysis of the Current Population Survey, the [[Dataset/american-time-use-survey]] and the General Social Survey, together with studies by other researchers. Its central economic argument is that the growth in women's labour force participation and earnings has been essential to family incomes and the economy: according to the CEA, without women's gains since 1970 median family income would be about $13,000 lower and the economy $2 trillion smaller.

## Details

**Breadwinning and caregiving.** More than 40 percent of mothers are the sole or primary source of household income, reflecting both the rise of single mothers and more married women out-earning their husbands (24 percent, against 7 percent in 1970); in 2013 employed married women's income made up 44 percent of their family's income, up from 37 percent in 1970. Women were still paid on average 77 cents for every dollar a man makes. Fathers are increasingly caregivers: 1 in 5 fathers is the primary caregiver of preschool-age children when the mother is employed, and according to the report fathers spent 49 hours a week on paid work and family care in 1965 and 54 hours in 2012, doing more childcare and housework without a commensurate cut in paid work.

**Women in the labour force.** Women made up 47 percent of the labour force, compared with 38 percent in 1970, and the CEA estimates the economy is 13.5 percent larger than it would be had women's employment and hours stayed at 1970 levels. Women's participation had stagnated in recent decades, and the U.S. fell from 7th of 24 developed countries in women's labour force participation in 1990 to 16th in 2012, which the report links to its lag in flexible work and paid leave arrangements. Women attain the majority of college degrees, and the gap in median job tenure between women and men closed in 2012 for the first time since the Bureau of Labor Statistics began measuring it in 1983.

**Working parents and eldercare.** All parents work in more than 6 out of 10 households with children, up from 4 out of 10 in 1965, while childcare costs have risen; almost one-quarter of adults report having lost a job or been threatened with job loss for an absence due to illness or caring for a sick child or relative, a problem the report describes as most acute for low-wage workers with unpredictable schedules. About 40 million Americans (16 percent of the population aged 15 and over) provide unpaid [[DefinedTerm/eldercare]] each year; 63 percent of them have jobs, and one-fifth also care for young children — the [[DefinedTerm/sandwich-generation]].

**Work–family conflict and workplaces.** In 2008, 60 percent of fathers in dual-earner couples reported work–family conflict, up from 35 percent in 1977, surpassing the share of mothers in such couples; in 2010, 46 percent of working men and women said their job demands interfered with family life sometimes or often, while fewer than 30 percent said family interfered with work. A third of workers have passed up a job because it conflicted with family obligations. The report notes that the United States is one of three countries in the world, and the only developed country, without mandatory paid parental leave, that only 11 percent of private-sector employers offer formal paid leave specifically for family reasons, and that the Family and Medical Leave Act guarantees unpaid leave but covers only about 60 percent of workers. Only half of workers earning less than $540 a week have access to any form of paid leave, against more than 80 percent of those earning more than $1,230 a week.

**Returns to flexibility and paid leave.** The report argues that workplace flexibility policies such as job sharing, phased retirement, [[DefinedTerm/flexible-working-hours]] and telecommuting generate a positive return for employers by helping them attract and retain talent, and it cites studies associating such policies with rising share prices, good management and higher performance among call-centre workers allowed to work from home. It concludes that because employers may lack accurate information about the costs and benefits of these practices, and because some benefits extend beyond the individual employer, wider adoption likely offers more benefits to firms, workers and the economy than employers may realise, and that today's working families need a modern workplace with workplace flexibility, paid leave and quality child and eldercare. According to a CEA calculation reported in the document, closing the male–female employment gap would raise GDP by 9 percent.
