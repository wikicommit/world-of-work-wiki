*Time use: Total work burden, unpaid work, and leisure* is a chapter of *Women in Canada: A Gender-based Statistical Report*, released by Statistics Canada on 30 July 2018 (catalogue no. 89-503-X) and written by Melissa Moyser and Amanda Burlock. Using the 1986, 2010 and 2015 cycles of the General Social Survey on Time Use (the [[Dataset/canadian-time-use-survey]]) and the 2012 [[Dataset/general-social-survey-on-caregiving-and-care-receiving]], it examines gender differences in time spent on [[DefinedTerm/housework]], caregiving and leisure over 30 years, and the total work burden of women and men — the time spent on paid and [[DefinedTerm/unpaid-work]] in combination (see [[DefinedTerm/total-work-time]]). Unless stated otherwise its estimates cover people aged 25 to 54 in the 10 provinces, an age group chosen because it spans the years when earning and caring roles are most onerous.

The chapter sets out from the changing economic structure of families: since the mid-1970s the share of dual-earner families rose from 39.2% to 58.8% and the share of lone-parent families nearly doubled, from 8.4% to 14.2%. It weighs two competing claims from earlier research — that the total work burden of women and men is now equal, and that men have not taken on unpaid work to the extent women have taken on paid work, leaving women with a "second shift" of unpaid work after their paid work and a "stalled" gender revolution. The authors argue that gender differences in multitasking may reconcile the two: because housework and child care are often done alongside other activities, research that counts only primary activities may systematically underestimate women's unpaid work. The chapter therefore presents both primary and [[DefinedTerm/simultaneous-activity]] data. It also notes that the mental and emotional work of coordinating children's lives and the household, "emotion work" and "kin keeping" goes unmeasured in time-use surveys.

## Details

**Total work burden.** In 2015 women spent an average of 3.9 hours a day on unpaid work as a primary activity and men 2.4 hours, while women spent 3.9 hours on paid work and men 5.2. Their total work burden was therefore equivalent (7.8 and 7.6 hours). Counting unpaid work done as a simultaneous activity, women's total work burden in 2010 was 1.2 hours a day greater than men's (9.1 versus 7.9 hours); 60.8% of women did unpaid work as a simultaneous activity in 2015, against 40.2% of men. From the Labour Force Survey, 82.0% of women and 90.9% of men participated in the labour market in 2015, and employed women usually worked 35.5 hours a week on all jobs against men's 41.1. The chapter stresses that the unpaid work women disproportionately do for their families facilitates men's higher labour force participation and longer hours. In an international comparison of people aged 15 to 64, Canadians had the highest total work burden among the G7 countries and Sweden, and Italian men, followed by French men and women, the lowest.

**Housework.** Women spent 2.8 hours a day on housework as a primary activity in 2015 and men 1.9. Between 1986 and 2015 women's time fell by 42 minutes a day and men's rose by 24 minutes, so men made up a little more than half of women's reduction. Women's housework time fell with longer paid-work hours, higher education and higher personal income, while men's did not vary with paid-work hours or income.

**Caregiving.** Women spent an average of one hour a day caring for children in 2015 and men 30 minutes; both had increased their time by 12 minutes since 1986, leaving a stable 30-minute gap. Including child care done alongside other activities, women spent 1.9 hours a day on it in 2010 and men 54 minutes. On a given day in 2015, 3.3% of women and 1.2% of men cared for an adult family member or friend. Because the time diary misses intermittent care, the chapter turns to the 2012 caregiving survey, in which female caregivers spent 11.8 hours in a typical week helping with care tasks and male caregivers 7.4.

**Leisure and time pressure.** Women spent 3.6 hours a day on leisure in 2015, 30 minutes less than men (4.1 hours) and than women in 1986. Nearly 20% of women did housework or caregiving at the same time as leisure, compared with 6.0% of men, and the chapter cites research finding that such leisure is more fragmented and less restorative. Women aged 25 to 54 also reported more [[DefinedTerm/time-stress]]: 68.6% felt under stress when they did not have enough time, against 60.0% of men.

**Other age groups.** Textboxes extend the analysis to young Canadians aged 15 to 24, whose total work burden — including studying — was lower than that of people aged 25 to 54, and to Canadians aged 55 or older, whose time use still paralleled traditional gender roles: employed older women did less paid work than older men (4.7 versus 5.6 hours a day), and older women who did unpaid household work spent more time on it (4.1 versus 3.4 hours).
