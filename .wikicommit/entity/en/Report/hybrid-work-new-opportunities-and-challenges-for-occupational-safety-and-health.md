This discussion paper was commissioned by the European Agency for Safety and Health at Work (EU-OSHA) and published in 2023, in the context of EU-OSHA's overview on digitalisation and occupational safety and health (OSH) and its Healthy Workplaces Campaign 2023–2025 on "Safe and Healthy Work in the Digital Age". It was written by Yves Roquelaure, who directs the Epidemiology in Occupational Health and Ergonomics team at Inserm's Research Institute for Environmental and Occupational Health (Irset) and the Department of Occupational Diseases and Medicine at the University Hospital of Angers, and EU-OSHA states that its contents are the author's alone and do not necessarily reflect the agency's views. It synthesises knowledge on the impact of [[DefinedTerm/hybrid-work]] on working conditions and management practices, and on wellbeing, OSH outcomes and health behaviours compared with full-time telework or in-person office work.

The paper rests on a narrative literature review carried out in February 2023 across scientific databases from several disciplines, together with reports from international agencies, statistical and labour bodies, universities and consulting firms, and on discussions of the review's results with eight hybrid-work and telework experts from institutions in France and Belgium. It notes that evidence on hybrid work as practised after the pandemic is still scarce and methodologically weak, so much of its analysis extrapolates from research on telework.

## Details

Following the classification of the European Working Conditions Telephone Survey, the paper treats hybrid work as a combination of telework and on-site work in which telework accounts for 10% to 90% of working time, distinguishing it from full-time telework (more than 90%) and work at the employer's premises (less than 10% telework). It describes hybrid work in terms of physical, temporal, technical, organisational, social, managerial, ergonomic and individual dimensions, and notes that, as with telework, it mainly involves highly qualified white-collar workers in teleworkable office jobs, so that its spread may reinforce social inequalities between workers who can and cannot work remotely, and could reinforce gender stereotypes and the unequal sharing of household tasks and unpaid care.

On physical health, the paper links the telework periods of hybrid work to longer computer time and more [[DefinedTerm/sedentary-work]], with an increased risk of neck and low back pain and other [[DefinedTerm/work-related-musculoskeletal-disorders]], as well as digital eye strain, sleep disorders, poor eating habits, noise and poor air quality at home, and accident risks in home offices that often do not meet the OSH standards of the employer's premises. On the psychosocial side, it describes a spatiotemporal dispersion of work teams, work intensification and [[DefinedTerm/technostress]] from intensive use of digital tools, reduced informal interaction and feelings of isolation even on site, weakened teamwork, heavier demands on line managers, more surveillance and [[DefinedTerm/algorithmic-management]], and exposure to digital incivilities and cyberbullying. Effects on job satisfaction, [[DefinedTerm/work-life-balance]] and mental health are mixed and depend on the work situation, with the voluntary nature and predictability of hybrid arrangements highlighted as moderating factors.

For prevention, the paper stresses that employers have equivalent OSH responsibilities wherever employees work and that the risk assessment required by the OSH Framework Directive must cover both home and on-site work, including new recruits and non-teleworking colleagues to whom work may be shifted. It recommends ergonomic workspaces and equipment at home and in the office, measures against prolonged sitting, supportive management with clear objectives rather than strict monitoring, training for managers, technical support, and clear hybrid-work schedules. On regulation, it notes that hybrid work, like telework, is governed at EU level by the [[Legislation/eu-framework-agreement-on-telework]] rather than hard law, that the European social partners agreed in June 2022 to revise that agreement, and that promoting the [[DefinedTerm/right-to-disconnect]] would help counter blurred work–life boundaries. It argues that occupational disease compensation criteria need adapting, since the non-specific musculoskeletal pain typical of hybrid workers does not match the biomechanical-overload criteria of current lists, and it notes the risk of [[DefinedTerm/virtual-presenteeism]] alongside hybrid work's potential to support return to work after chronic disease.

The paper concludes that well-designed hybrid work is likely to retain many advantages of full-time telework, such as autonomy and work–life balance, while reducing some of its disadvantages, such as technostress and social isolation, but that knowledge of its medium- and long-term effects lags behind its spread. It calls for longitudinal and multidisciplinary research, including on favourable thresholds of weekly telework days.
