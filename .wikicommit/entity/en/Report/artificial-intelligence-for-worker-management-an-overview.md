*Artificial intelligence for worker management: an overview* presents the findings of an EU-OSHA project on new forms of worker management through AI-based systems — [[DefinedTerm/ai-based-worker-management]] (AIWM) — and occupational safety and health (OSH). The project formed part of a four-year research programme on digitalisation and OSH that EU-OSHA began in 2020, and aimed to identify gaps, needs and priorities for OSH and to make recommendations for policy, research and practice, discussed at a concluding high-level workshop. The research combined a literature review, in-depth interviews with 22 experts, consultation with EU-OSHA's national focal points, and statistical analysis including data from the third [[Dataset/esener]] (ESENER-3). It was commissioned by EU-OSHA and written by researchers from Visionary Analytics and Tallinn University of Technology; the summary notes that its contents are those of the authors and do not necessarily reflect EU-OSHA's views.

## Details

**Definition and uptake.** The report adopts the definition of an AI system from the European Commission's 2021 proposal for an AI regulation and describes AIWM as an umbrella term that also covers [[DefinedTerm/algorithmic-management]]. Because there is no single database measuring AIWM, its uptake can mainly be inferred from the technologies organisations use. Interviewed experts considered that many organisations use simple algorithms and mistake them for AI. AIWM is described as used more in manual sectors with routine tasks in controlled environments — experts named logistics, manufacturing, transportation and healthcare — and on blue-collar and lower-skilled white-collar workers such as call-centre staff. ESENER-3 data show that around 23% of manufacturing businesses use machines, systems or computers to monitor workers' performance, against about 14% in information and communication and 11% in financial and insurance activities; around 6% of organisations with 5–9 employees use systems to monitor worker performance, compared with 19% of those with 250 or more; organisations with worker representation use AIWM-enabling technologies more often; and private and public organisations use them to a broadly similar extent.

**Goals and risks.** Organisations are described as implementing AIWM to increase efficiency and productivity (for example by automating scheduling and task allocation, gamification, or real-time direction of workers), to improve decision-making (through people analytics and prediction models), and to improve workers' health, safety and well-being (for example by monitoring fatigue or offering mental health chatbots). The risks identified include dehumanisation and the "datafication" of workers, manipulative nudging, a lack of transparency — workers are often unaware that they are monitored or that an algorithm is evaluating them — invasions of privacy that blur work–life balance, increased pace of work and performance pressure leading to [[DefinedTerm/work-related-stress]] and accidents, and the amplification of existing biases.

**Regulatory context.** At EU level, the report points to the OSH acquis, Directive 2002/14/EC on informing and consulting employees, the GDPR (including Article 22 on decisions based solely on automated processing), and EU non-discrimination and fundamental-rights law, as well as the Commission's April 2021 proposal for an AI regulation, which would classify AI systems used for recruitment, promotion and termination decisions, task allocation and monitoring and evaluating performance and behaviour as high-risk; it reports academic criticism that the proposal lacks social dialogue and workers' voice and clear provisions on liability. At national level, at least 20 of 27 Member States plus Norway and Switzerland had AI strategies, most of them general; exceptions highlighted include the German, French and Czech strategies, a German Trade Union Confederation concept paper on deploying trustworthy AI, Italian National Labour Inspectorate guidance on tools used in call centres, and Spain's [[Legislation/riders-law]].

**Recommendations.** The report makes eleven recommendations: human-centred design that leaves humans in control; workers' participation, consultation and social dialogue, including co-governance of AIWM systems; holistic and dynamic evaluation of AIWM; transparent, explainable systems backed by binding requirements on providers and strong enforcement; a clear line of responsibility, including named responsible managers; better privacy and data protection, with rights to contest automated decisions, refuse consent without reprisal and obtain an explanation; the [[DefinedTerm/right-to-disconnect]], also as a safeguard against disproportionate monitoring; knowledge exchange and awareness building; stronger labour inspectorate capacity and cooperation with data protection authorities; AI literacy for workers and employers; and transparency between system developers and deploying organisations.
