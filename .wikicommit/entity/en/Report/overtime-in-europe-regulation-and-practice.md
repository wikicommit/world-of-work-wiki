Overtime in Europe: Regulation and practice is a research report published in 2022 by Eurofound, the European Foundation for the Improvement of Living and Working Conditions. It notes that, despite the well-known adverse effects of regular long working hours, many EU workers work beyond the hours set by their contracts, regulations or legislation, and that there are no publicly available harmonised regular data quantifying [[DefinedTerm/overtime]] in the EU. Based mainly on national information supplied by the Network of Eurofound Correspondents in response to a standard questionnaire, it compares how overtime is regulated in the EU Member States, Norway and the United Kingdom, assesses its prevalence and extent, explores its drivers and consequences, and summarises national debates.

For its purposes the report treats overtime as working time beyond normal working hours, and it points out that the Working Time Directive limits overtime only indirectly — through the 48-hour maximum average working week including overtime and the 11-hour minimum daily rest — without defining overtime or requiring Member States to regulate it explicitly.

## Details

**Regulation.** Most Member States regulate overtime through legislation supplemented by collective bargaining, with Denmark the main exception, where collective bargaining is the primary means. Countries differ in whether overtime is treated as a normal obligation or an exceptional measure, and workers' right to refuse it is rarely unconditional. Default annual limits range from 80 hours in Spain to 250 hours in Hungary and can often be extended by agreement, in Hungary and Slovakia up to 400 hours a year. Overtime is usually compensated at a premium, most commonly 50% of regular pay, with a range from 10% to 100% or even 150% in specific circumstances; in Germany, Ireland, the Netherlands and the UK there is no statutory entitlement to a premium. Some groups — pregnant workers, parents of young children and young workers — may be precluded from overtime, while senior managers are often exempt from the rules. Enforcement relies on employers recording working hours, labour inspectorates and mostly financial sanctions.

**Prevalence.** National statistics are disparate and not strictly comparable, but in most countries with data a two-digit percentage of employees work overtime with some regularity. Austria, Ireland, Malta, Portugal and the UK show the highest average overtime hours, with those working overtime doing roughly an extra day a week. No clear European trend emerged over the past decade. Men, prime-age workers and those with higher education are more likely to report overtime, and patterns by occupation, sector and public or private employment vary between countries.

**Drivers and consequences.** For employers, overtime is a tool of internal flexibility, and the legal framework influences how much it is used; for workers, it can reflect extra income, high work intensity, organisational pressure or strong motivation to work. The report summarises research showing that overtime and long working days harm physical and mental health with a dose–response effect, and that regular overtime is associated with poorer work–life balance. It finds unpaid overtime pervasive where data exist and concludes that it generally results from pressure on workers, which is most effective when they are vulnerable.

**Debates.** National debates in 2018–2020 concerned unpaid working hours, structural overtime (continuous reliance on extra hours instead of recruitment), the monitoring and [[DefinedTerm/working-time-recording]] of hours, vulnerable groups such as platform workers, domestic workers and healthcare staff, and overtime during remote working and the COVID-19 pandemic, including "grey overtime" that goes unrecorded and the growing debate on the [[DefinedTerm/right-to-disconnect]]. The report concludes that overtime remains sometimes abused or misused, that better recording and compensation mechanisms could help, and that compensation does not undo harm to workers' health.
