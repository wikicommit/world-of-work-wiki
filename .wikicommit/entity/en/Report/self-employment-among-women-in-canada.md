*Self-employment among women in Canada* is an article in Statistics Canada's *Insights on Canadian Society* series, released on 4 December 2023. Written by a senior researcher at Statistics Canada's Centre for Social Data Insights and Innovation and funded by Women and Gender Equality Canada, it examines how [[DefinedTerm/self-employment]] among women has changed over time in rate, type and occupation, and how it differs among women with different characteristics, setting their experience against that of men.

The study combines two data sources. Annual data from the [[Dataset/canadian-labour-force-survey]] for 1976 to 2022 are used for historical trends, and the 2001 and 2021 Censuses of Population for a detailed profile of population groups. The article notes that women's motivations for becoming self-employed often differ from men's, with women much more likely to cite flexible hours and the need for [[DefinedTerm/work-life-balance]], which it links to the gender imbalance in [[DefinedTerm/unpaid-work]].

## Details

**Long-run trends.** Nearly one million women were self-employed in 2022, 37% of all self-employed Canadians, up from 26% in 1976; most of that gain came by the late 1990s. Women's self-employment rate rose from 9% in 1976 to a high of 13% in the late 1990s and was 11% in 2022, while men's went from 14% to 20% and then 16%. The overall rate climbed from 12% in 1976 to 17% in 1998, fell to 15% by 2001, and stayed roughly stable afterwards, dipping during the COVID-19 pandemic without recovering. Self-employment rises with age and is trending older: in 2022, 2% of working women aged 15 to 24, 10% of those aged 25 to 54 and 18% of those 55 and older were self-employed, and the small overall gain since 1976 was largely driven by older workers. There is very little gender gap under age 25.

**Type of self-employment.** In 1976, 34% of self-employed women were unpaid family workers, mostly in agriculture; by 2022 the share was 1%. In 2022, 80% of self-employed women and 68% of self-employed men worked without employees, and the most common type for both was unincorporated without paid help (61% of women, 40% of men). Self-employed women were less likely than men to have employees (19% versus 32%) and to be incorporated (34% versus 54%), although the gap in having employees had narrowed from 29 percentage points in 1976 to 13 in 2022. Incorporation without paid help grew the most over the period, which the article partly attributes to a shift in industry mix from agriculture toward professional, scientific and technical services, and to the limited personal liability and tax advantages of incorporation.

**Industries and occupations.** In 2022, half of self-employed women worked in health care and social assistance (20%), professional, scientific and technical services (19%) and services other than public administration (11%). Their most common occupations were retail and wholesale trade managers, early childhood educators and assistants, real estate agents and salespersons, light duty cleaners, and hairstylists and barbers. The share of self-employed women in their five most common occupations fell from 43% in 1987 to 24% in 2022, so that they went from being more occupationally concentrated than self-employed men to slightly less.

**Population groups (2021 Census).** Racialized and non-racialized, non-Indigenous Canadians had similar self-employment rates overall (14% and 15%), but rates varied widely between racialized groups of women, from 20% among Korean Canadian women to 6% among Black and 5% among Filipino Canadian women. Immigrants were more likely to be self-employed than non-immigrants (18% versus 14%), and the gender gap among immigrants was smaller for recent arrivals. The Indigenous population living off reserve had a lower rate than the non-Indigenous population (10% versus 15%); among women, 10% of Métis, 7% of First Nations people living off reserve and 4% of Inuit were self-employed, against 12% of non-Indigenous women, and light duty cleaners was the most common occupation among self-employed First Nations and Métis women. The article suggests that lower Indigenous self-employment can result from complex factors including systemic barriers and the intergenerational effects of colonization and residential schools.

The article concludes that self-employment is fundamentally different from 40 years ago, reflecting a shift to a knowledge-based economy, the rise of the [[DefinedTerm/gig-economy]] and women's growing labour force participation. It calls for research into the reasons for the gender gaps, into whether women turn to gig work through online platforms as a main or supplementary source of income, and into the differences across population groups.
