*Horaires de travail atypiques : les femmes, les moins qualifiés et les personnes nées à l’étranger sont exposés aux formes les plus contraignantes* ("Atypical working hours: women, the less qualified and people born abroad are exposed to the most constraining forms") is a dossier published by Insee on 18 November 2025 in its Insee Références series, in the November 2025 edition of *France, portrait social*. It was written by Anne Lambert and Capucine Rauch of Ined and Laurent Lesnard of Sciences Po (Centre for Research on Social Inequalities, CNRS).

The study uses the [[Dataset/french-working-conditions-survey]] of 2019 and covers employees living in ordinary dwellings in metropolitan France. It counts as [[DefinedTerm/atypical-working-hours]] both atypical usual hours — long, shifted early in the morning, in the evening or at night, or [[DefinedTerm/split-working-hours]] — and regular [[DefinedTerm/weekend-work]] of at least 11 Saturdays or 11 Sundays a year, excluding work taken home. It frames these hours against the decline of the post-war norm of full-time, daytime, weekday, continuous work, and articulates occupational category with gender to reflect the differing time organisation of more or less female or male occupations.

## Details

**Prevalence.** In 2019, 48% of employees — about 11 million people — regularly worked at least one form of atypical hours in their main job: 36% had atypical usual hours and 30% regularly worked at weekends. Early-morning hours were the most common atypical hours (12% of employees) and night work the least common (3%). Women were slightly more exposed than men (49% against 46%), more often worked regularly at weekends (32% against 28%) and more often had split hours (9% against 5%), while men more often worked at night and long days. About a fifth of employees combined atypical hours with regular weekend work (20% of women, 18% of men); night workers were the most likely to do so, 72% of them also working regularly on Saturdays and 64% on both weekend days.

**Who works atypical hours.** Early-morning and evening hours mainly concerned employees with few or no qualifications, more than half of them without the baccalauréat. Among men, these hours were concentrated among manual workers, especially in industry; among women, they were spread across more occupations, including health and social care intermediate occupations, civil-service employees, retail employees and manual workers. Split hours concerned the least qualified most of all. Long hours were worked mainly by qualified men — 45% of the men working them were managers and professionals, though drivers were also over-represented — whereas among women 60% of long hours were worked by clerical and service employees, notably personal-services staff. Night work was strongly segregated by gender: manual workers, police and the military among men; civil-service employees such as care assistants, and health and social care intermediate occupations, among women.

**Determinants.** Logistic regression models estimating the probability of each kind of hours at otherwise comparable characteristics found that age and family situation mattered little, while occupational category was the main determinant, with effects that differ by gender. The more advantaged the occupational category, the higher the probability of standard hours, with exceptions such as business administrative employees, who were close to managers. Early-morning hours followed a marked social gradient almost the reverse of standard hours, with the highest probabilities among unskilled industrial women workers (37%) and, among men, skilled handling, warehousing and transport workers (32%). Retail employees had by far the highest probability of working on Saturdays only (51% for women, 55% for men). At comparable characteristics, people born abroad had a higher probability of atypical hours.

**Part-time work and gender.** People in constrained part-time work were nearly twice as likely as full-time workers to have early-morning or evening rather than standard hours, and far more likely to have split hours; part-time work chosen for family or personal reasons also raised the risk of split hours. Because part-time work, especially constrained part-time work, is more common among women, the authors conclude that women's greater exposure to atypical hours runs largely through occupational category — some feminised occupations being particularly exposed — and through the ways employment and family life are combined, part-time work among them.
