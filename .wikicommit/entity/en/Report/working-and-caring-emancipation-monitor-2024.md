*Werken en zorgen* ("Working and caring") is chapter 5 of the *Emancipatiemonitor 2024* (Emancipation Monitor 2024), published by Statistics Netherlands (CBS) in 2024. The chapter asks whether one parent works more while the other does more of the childcare or whether work and care are shared equally, whether the division matches what parents want, what it means financially for fathers and mothers, how many parents use arrangements for combining work and care such as childcare and birth leave, and whether women and men provide informal care to sick relatives equally often and at what cost to work and other activities.

The chapter finds that practice is "more traditional" than parents' wishes. Among parents with a partner and a child under 18, 50% would like work and childcare to be shared equally, but 9% say they actually share them equally, while 48% say the man works more and the woman does more of the care, a division 13% regard as desirable. The dominant working pattern among couples with minor children is the [[DefinedTerm/one-and-a-half-earner-model]], and women's income from work falls sharply after the birth of a first child — the [[DefinedTerm/child-penalty]] — while men's does not.

## Details

**Preferences and practice.** 57% of parents with a partner and a child under 18 would prefer the man and the woman to do an equal amount of paid work; a third would prefer the father to work more, most of whom still want childcare shared equally. Fathers and mothers did not demonstrably differ in their preferred division. Women report more often than men that the man works more and the woman does more of the care (54% against 42%). Even so, most people do not think women are better suited to raising young children, although men agree with that statement more often than women (36% against 16%). For housework, 51% of people aged 16 and over with a partner would prefer to share paid work and household tasks equally, while 10% say they do, and 46% say the woman does more of the housework while the man works more.

**Working patterns and hours.** In 2023, in 13% of couples (both partners aged 25 to 50) with children under 18 both partners worked full time, compared with 44% of couples without minor children. The combination of a full-time working man and a part-time working woman was the most common pattern among couples with minor children (55%), increasingly with the woman working 20 to 35 hours a week. Just over half (53%) of mothers with a partner and children under 18 worked 20 to 35 hours a week, and a minority worked full time (at least 35 hours), a share that rises with the age of the youngest child; the great majority of fathers worked full time. Women with a full-time job as an employee whose youngest child was 4 to 12 years old experienced a work–private life imbalance — neglecting family activities because of work — more often than full-time working fathers with a youngest child of that age (15% against 8% in 2023); among part-time working parents the imbalance was smaller.

**Attitudes to working hours.** Most people aged 16 and over think both fathers and mothers should work less than they do in practice. A minority (21%) think mothers of children under 4 should work at least four days a week, while 45% of these mothers do (at least 28 hours a week). Men more often than women think fathers of children under 4 should work full time (42% against 29%).

**Income after a first child.** Mothers with a partner and minor children worked on average just under 30 hours a week in 2023, against 35 hours for women up to 45 with a partner and no children. For women who had their first child in 2014–2016 and were working before the birth, income from work six years after the birth was 35% lower than in the year before; for men it was 1% higher. The fall was 57% for mothers whose first child was born in 1996–2001, and has shrunk mainly because women work longer hours. It was smallest for women with higher professional or university education (28%), against 47% for women with a vmbo or comparable diploma.

**Childcare and leave.** In 2023, 52% of working parents whose youngest child was under 4 used formal childcare (day care, out-of-school care or childminders), against 30% of those whose youngest child was 4 to 13; 40% of parents with a youngest child at primary school used no childcare at all. Over a third of people aged 16 and over think it is good for a baby to attend day care a few days a week, rising to 57% for a one-year-old and 75% for toddlers of 2 or 3. In 2023, 32% of female and 27% of male employees with children under 8 had taken parental leave in the past year; besides leave, 15% of the women and 11% of the men had started working fewer hours to care for a child, while men more often had started working at other times, working (more) from home or taking (other) leave. For 13% of men and 12% of women who took no parental leave, it was financially not possible. The chapter frames these arrangements against Dutch policy: the government's Emancipation Memorandum 2022–2025 holds that an equal division of paid work and care is crucial to gender equality on the labour market; birth leave for partners, introduced in 2019, has been extended step by step, and since August 2022 the first nine weeks of parental leave have been partly paid. In 2023, 89% of fathers took leave immediately after the birth and 53% used the full six weeks of birth leave.

**Care for sick relatives.** 12% of women say they (very) often help people in their surroundings who need help for more than a month because of health problems, twice the share of men. Among those who help, 66% say it leaves them with less free time, 10% that it comes at the expense of household or family care and 7% at the expense of working time. In 2023, 9% of female and 7% of male employees provided long-term care (more than two weeks), most often to a parent; most employees with a long-term or short-term care task took no leave for it, and long-term (unpaid) care leave was rarely used. The government of Prime Minister Schoof plans to merge short-term and long-term care leave into a single "informal carer's leave" and has asked the Social and Economic Council (SER) for advice on combining work and informal care.
