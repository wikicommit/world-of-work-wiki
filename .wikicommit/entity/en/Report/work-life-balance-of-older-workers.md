*Work-life balance of older workers* is an article in the October 2009 issue of Statistics Canada's *Perspectives on Labour and Income* (catalogue 75-001-X), by two researchers in the Policy Research Directorate of Human Resources and Skills Development Canada. It examines [[DefinedTerm/work-life-balance]] among workers aged 55 and over — a group the authors describe as largely understudied, since most work-life research has focused on younger workers with children — looking at how many are dissatisfied with their balance, the reasons they give, and the personal and job characteristics associated with work-life conflict.

The authors argue that the issue matters because work-life conflict has been associated with negative health and productivity outcomes and plays a role in retirement decisions. As people age, changes such as taking on care for elderly parents or grandchildren, the onset of disability, events such as divorce or the loss of a spouse, and shifting priorities may become new sources of work-life tension. The data come from the 2005 General Social Survey on time use, with a study sample of 1,832 respondents aged 55 and over who were working at the time of the survey.

## Details

In 2005 there were 2.3 million workers aged 55 and over in Canada, 14% of the workforce; about three-fifths were men. Compared with core-age workers (25 to 54), they were more likely to have a disability (38% vs. 26%), to be self-employed (31% vs. 18%), to work fewer than 30 hours a week (20% vs. 9%) and to be able to choose when their workday started and ended (48% vs. 41%). Only 3% lived with children under 15, but 26% provided unpaid child care, mostly outside their own household, and 20% provided care to seniors, compared with 16% of core-age workers.

Fourteen per cent of older workers were dissatisfied with their work-life balance, compared with 25% of core-age workers. Dissatisfaction was around 15% to 16% at ages 55 to 64 and dropped to 5% at 65 and over. The most common reason was spending too much time on the job (45% of the dissatisfied), followed by not having enough time for family (27%); older workers cited lack of family time much less often than core-age workers (27% vs. 46%). Only 27% of those citing too much time on the job would prefer to work fewer hours for less pay, which the authors read as financial considerations, rather than necessarily a lack of employer flexibility, making it hard to cut back.

A probit model with selection, which accounted for older people leaving employment, found that once this selection and other factors were controlled for, the risk of dissatisfaction did not vary with age. Dissatisfaction was more likely for women (11 percentage points more than men), for workers with a disability (over 7 points), for those providing four or more hours of [[DefinedTerm/eldercare]] a week (over 14 points), for managers (9 points more than sales, service and manufacturing workers) and for those working 50 hours or more a week (over 20 points more than those working under 30 hours). It was less likely for those with an employed spouse or partner (8 to 12 points less than those without a partner), the self-employed (6 points) and those very satisfied with their jobs (over 37 points). Providing child care made no discernible difference. The authors suggest that the growing share of women among older workers and the likely rise in elder care as the population ages could make work-life conflict among older workers more prevalent.
