*Harassment in Canadian workplaces* is an article in Statistics Canada's *Insights on Canadian Society* series, released on 17 December 2018 in partnership with the agency's Centre for Gender, Diversity and Inclusion Statistics. Written by Darcy Hango and Melissa Moyser, it uses the 2016 [[Dataset/general-social-survey-on-canadians-at-work-and-home]] to examine [[DefinedTerm/workplace-harassment]] experienced in the previous 12 months by people aged 15 to 64 who worked for pay; the self-employed were excluded to focus on the power dynamic between employers and employees. The survey covered five types of harassment: verbal abuse, humiliating behaviour, threats, physical violence, and unwanted sexual attention or sexual harassment.

The first part of the article measures how common harassment is, its type and source, and the personal and workplace characteristics associated with it; the second explores its relationship with workplace well-being (job satisfaction, motivation, intention to leave and sense of belonging) and personal well-being (stress, general and mental health, and outlook on the future). Because cause and effect cannot be disentangled, the article presents these as associations, and notes that the relationships may run in both directions.

## Details

**Prevalence and type.** In 2016, 19% of women and 13% of men reported having been harassed at work in the past year. Verbal abuse was the most common type (13% of women, 10% of men), followed by humiliating behaviour (6% and 5%); about 3% of each sex reported threats. Women were more likely than men to report physical violence (3% versus 1.5%) and sexual harassment or unwanted sexual attention (4% versus less than 1%).

**Source.** Among those harassed, 53% of women and 42% of men named a client or customer, 32% of women and 39% of men a supervisor or manager, and about a third of each a colleague or peer. Among women who were sexually harassed, 56% were targeted by clients or customers, 44% by colleagues or peers and 15% by supervisors or managers, and just over a quarter were targeted by more than one perpetrator.

**Who is harassed.** In models adjusting for other characteristics, women's higher probability of harassment (18% versus 14%) persisted when sexual harassment was included, but the smaller gap for non-sexual harassment disappeared once occupation and other workplace characteristics were taken into account. Workers in health occupations had the highest probability (23%; 27% of women and 21% of men in those occupations) and those in natural and applied sciences the lowest (9%). Workers in unionized jobs had a probability 6 percentage points higher than non-unionized workers (20% versus 14%), although harassed unionized workers were also more likely to say the harassment had been addressed. The probability was also higher among people with a bachelor's degree or higher and those with a mobility limitation, and lower among visible minority workers, married people and those earning $120,000 or more. Young, unmarried, lesbian or bisexual, and Aboriginal women were more likely to report sexual harassment at work.

**Associations with well-being.** Harassed workers were more likely to be dissatisfied with their job — 14% of harassed women against under 4% of those not harassed, and 14% against 5% for men — to have low motivation, to plan to leave their job and to have a weak sense of belonging to their organization. They also reported more stress and poorer mental health: 18% of harassed men and 16% of harassed women rated their mental health as poor, against 6% and 8% of those not harassed. The workplace associations were generally strongest when the harasser was a supervisor or manager: 47% of men and 34% of women harassed by a supervisor or manager had a weak sense of belonging, compared with about 16% of workers who had not been harassed.

**Work environment.** Among workers continuously employed in the same job over the year, harassment was more often reported by those with fewer opportunities for input into decisions, less support from their supervisor, unmanageable workloads, little choice in the order of their tasks, more competition with colleagues, more conflict with supervisors and fewer good friends at work — associations that tended to be stronger for women. The article concludes that workplace harassment has a considerable impact not only on people's lives but also on employers.
