*Job quality indicators in the UK – hours, pay and contracts: 2018* is an article by the UK Office for National Statistics (ONS), released on 4 December 2019. It presents what the ONS calls the first exploratory analysis of indicators of the quality of jobs in the UK, using newly reweighted earnings data from the [[Dataset/annual-population-survey]] for 2018. The data are experimental, the analysis was requested by representatives of city regions through the ONS Centre for Subnational Analysis, and the ONS states that it is not a government definition of quality jobs.

The article looks at employee jobs, on a resident basis, against three indicators of [[DefinedTerm/job-quality]]: **satisfactory hours** (not working more than 48 hours a week, and not underemployed — not wanting more hours in the current role or looking for an additional or replacement job with more hours); a **desired contract** (a permanent contract, or a non-permanent one held for a reason other than being unable to find a permanent job); and **not being in [[DefinedTerm/low-pay]]** (earning at least two-thirds of median hourly pay, measured against either the UK median or the median of the local area where the employee lives). Employees meeting all three are counted as being in "quality work".

## Details

**Main findings for 2018.** Four in five UK employees (80%) worked satisfactory hours; 7% were underemployed and 13% worked over 48 hours. Northern Ireland had the highest share working satisfactory hours (84%). Women were about 10 percentage points more likely than men to work satisfactory hours (86% against 75%), which the article relates to more women working part-time; managers, directors and senior officials were the least likely occupation group to do so (70%). Almost all employees (99%) had a desired contract, which the ONS takes to imply that part-time work is more often an active choice than the result of being unable to find full-time work. Using two-thirds of UK median hourly pay (£7.90 an hour in 2018), 7% of employees were in low-pay employment, ranging among city regions from 3% in the Greater London Authority to around one in ten in the Sheffield City Region. Measured against local median pay, employees in England were more likely to be in low pay (14%) than those in Scotland, Wales or Northern Ireland, and the Cambridgeshire and Peterborough Combined Authority, Aberdeen City Region and Greater London Authority stood out as having significantly more employees in low pay than other city regions.

**Composite measure.** Using the local measure of low pay, 69% of women and 65% of men were in quality work. The share was 22% among employees aged 16 to 20, rose to around 70% between ages 25 and 64, and reached 77% at 65 and over — differences the ONS attributes mainly to low pay among young workers. By industry it ranged from 39% in accommodation and food service activities to 83% in public administration and defence, and by occupation it was lowest in elementary occupations (44%) and sales and customer service occupations (53%), with pay and hours the biggest contributing factors.

**Context and limits.** The article sets its measures against the International Labour Organization's concept of decent work, the UK government's Good Work Plan of December 2018 and the recommendations of the Measuring Job Quality Working Group, which proposed 18 priority job quality measures in seven categories and cautioned against measuring job quality by a single composite figure. The ONS acknowledges that its three indicators are only some of the measures recommended, that its composite has no hierarchy and "is not a conclusive definition for quality work", and that the underlying components should be used to understand what is happening. The analysis covers employees only, not the self-employed; it relies on self-reported survey data; and results at local authority level rest on small samples and are indicative only.
