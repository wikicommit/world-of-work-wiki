Gender-Leadership-Gap in Deutschland: Frauen besetzen weniger als ein Drittel der obersten Führungspositionen ("Gender leadership gap in Germany: women hold less than a third of top management positions") is a short report by three researchers at the Institut für Arbeitsmarkt- und Berufsforschung (IAB), published on 4 November 2025 as IAB-Kurzbericht 24|2025 (DOI 10.48720/IAB.KB.2524) under a CC BY-SA 4.0 licence. It notes that the German Basic Law has, since 1994, committed the state to promoting the actual implementation of equal rights for women and men, yet that 30 years later equality has not been reached — visible among other things in lower pay and poorer career prospects. Although women today are on average better educated than men, the report says, family formation and childcare place the burden of care work mainly on them, and traditional role models, structural barriers and inadequate reconciliation of work and family limit their advancement.

The IAB has documented the under-representation of women in management — the [[DefinedTerm/gender-leadership-gap]] — in private-sector establishments for 20 years using the [[Dataset/iab-establishment-panel]]. This report gives particular attention to establishments' measures to reconcile work and family.

## Details

**Women in management, 2024.** Women held 29% of top-level management positions in the private sector, 1 percentage point more than in 2022 and 4 points more than 20 years earlier, against a 45% share of all employees. Because women's share of employment has also risen, the gap has hardly changed; the ratio of the two shares, the report's representation measure, was 0.64 at the top level. At the second management level women held 42% of positions, a measure of 0.93, almost in line with their share of employees.

**East and West.** Women's employment rate and weekly hours are higher in East Germany and they work part-time less often. Women's shares at both management levels are 5 percentage points higher in the East, and the representation measure is 0.72 at the top level (0.64 in the West) and 1.00 at the second level (0.93 in the West), so that at the second level there is no longer a gender leadership gap in the East.

**Establishment size.** In establishments with at most nine employees women held 29% of top and 56% of second-level positions; in those with at least 200 employees, 24% and 34%. Women's share of employment also falls with size, so the representation measure at the top level differs at most slightly by size, while at the second level women are over-represented in the smallest establishments (1.17) and the measure falls to 0.81 in those with 200 or more employees.

**Industries.** Health and social work and education has by far the highest shares of women in management — 55% at the top and 71% at the second level — but also the highest share of women among employees, 76%, so women remain clearly under-represented at the top. Shares at the top level are also above average in interest groups and associations (46%), retail (41%) and hospitality and other services (38%). Construction has the lowest shares, 11% and 15%, but since women make up only 16% of its employees its representation measure is slightly higher than in the private sector as a whole. Relative to their share of employees, women are best represented in transport and storage and in energy, water, waste and mining (measures of 0.86 and 0.77 at the top level and 1.33 and 1.18 at the second), which the report explains by the large share of non-managerial jobs in strongly male occupations such as lorry driving. The largest gap is in finance and insurance, where women are 52% of employees but hold 15% of top and 38% of second-level positions (measures of 0.29 and 0.73). Across industries the correlation between women's share of employees and of management positions is very high (0.89 at the top level, 0.97 at the second).

**Reconciliation measures.** The share of private-sector establishments offering at least one measure to reconcile family and work rose from 32% in 2016 to 59% in 2024, chiefly because the share allowing family-friendly working hours — for example flexible hours, working from home or family-friendly part-time models — rose from 28% to 56%. Other measures are much rarer: 17% keep in contact with employees on parental leave, 12% support childcare, for instance through workplace kindergartens, holiday care or contributions to childcare costs, and 8% specifically support employees caring for relatives. The share of employees in establishments with at least one measure rose from 63% to 81%, and 77% now work in establishments with family-friendly hours. Measures are similarly widespread in East and West, except childcare support (15% of East German and 11% of West German establishments); they are more common in larger establishments (50% of those with up to nine employees, 94% of those with 200 or more) and most widespread in information and communication (80%), health and social work and education, and interest groups and associations (78% each), and least in agriculture, construction and hospitality (just over 40%). A third of establishments offer only family-friendly hours, while 26% offer at least one other measure. The report notes that family-friendly hours can also serve establishments' own aims, such as organising flexible deployment of staff, while the other measures serve reconciliation directly.

**Leave for fathers.** The report notes that the EU directive on work-life balance for parents and carers (2019/1158) was adopted in 2019 and that some European countries such as Sweden, France and Spain already provide leave for fathers, but that this has not yet been transposed into German law. Only 4% of establishments, employing 9% of employees, offer paid leave for fathers beyond the statutory rules, mostly larger establishments.

**Measures and women's advancement.** In industries where reconciliation measures were widespread in 2016, the share of women in top management rose particularly strongly by 2024 — for example in health and social work and education and in interest groups and associations — though not in information and communication; the correlation coefficient is about 0.5. The data cannot show the underlying mechanisms, or whether such establishments employ more career-oriented women or help similar women advance further. Membership of a network of family-friendly companies remains rare, rising from 1% of establishments in 2016 to about 2% in 2024 and almost 15% of those with over 200 employees.

**Conclusions.** The authors argue that, given shortages of skilled workers and managers, the potential of well-educated women must be used, and that conditions making it easier to reconcile work and family can be an important competitive advantage in recruiting (female) skilled workers in a labour market increasingly tight because of demographic change. They note that, according to further results from the 2024 panel, almost half of all establishments already regarded better reconciliation of work and family as an important strategy for securing skilled workers in 2023.
