This consultation document, issued by the European Commission in Brussels on 20 July 2026 and accompanied by an analytical staff working document (SWD(2026) 239), opened the second phase of consultation of the EU social partners under Article 154(3) TFEU on a Quality Jobs Act. The initiative, announced in the 2025 State of the Union address, aims to modernise and simplify the EU framework in a targeted way, improve the enforcement of existing rules and promote social dialogue and collective bargaining, so that employment protections keep pace with digitalisation, decarbonisation and demographic change while limiting burdens on companies, especially small and medium-sized ones.

The first phase ran from 4 December 2025 to 29 January 2026, and 34 recognised EU-level social partners replied — 12 trade unions and 22 employer organisations. According to the document, the trade unions all called for EU legislation in every area consulted on, while employer organisations argued that the existing framework is extensive and that EU action should focus on implementation, enforcement and reducing administrative burdens. It does not re-examine issues already covered by the 2024–2025 consultation on the right to disconnect and telework (see [[Report/first-phase-consultation-on-telework-and-the-right-to-disconnect]]), except for occupational safety and health. It asks the social partners for their views on the objectives, avenues and instruments set out, and whether they are willing to negotiate an agreement under Article 155 TFEU.

## Details

**Algorithmic management and AI at work.** The document describes [[DefinedTerm/algorithmic-management]] as spreading beyond the platform economy and identifies challenges of transparency, human involvement, intrusive monitoring (including neurotechnology and predictive "people analytics"), collective governance and regulatory complexity. Avenues include guidance on how the GDPR, the AI Act and information and consultation rules apply, human review of significant automated decisions, safeguards against disproportionate practices, and a stronger role for workers' representatives.

**Occupational safety and health.** The Commission points out that the Workplace Directive's definition of workplace covers only the employer's premises, although about a third of EU workers (32%) work most of the time outside them, and that it excludes agricultural and forestry land away from the undertaking's buildings. The Display Screen Equipment Directive does not specifically cover laptops, tablets and smartphones, used by 78% of EU workers in their main job in 2025, and contains outdated references. Options include extending the Workplace Directive to off-premises work including telework, updating and extending the DSE Directive, addressing psychosocial (including sexual harassment), ergonomic and heat- and weather-related risks, removing outdated exemptions, and merging the two directives.

**Subcontracting, just transitions and enforcement.** For subcontracting chains, options include identifying high-risk sectors, targets for risk-based inspections, liability mechanisms along the chain and OSH coordination on shared sites. For restructuring, the Commission considers the existing legal framework fit for purpose and favours building on the 2013 EU Quality Framework for anticipation of change and restructuring and using EU funds. On enforcement it considers obligations on labour inspection, sanctions for new rights, AI-supported targeting of inspections and better data exchange. Possible instruments range from a directive based on Article 153 TFEU to recommendations, guidance, or a package combining them.
