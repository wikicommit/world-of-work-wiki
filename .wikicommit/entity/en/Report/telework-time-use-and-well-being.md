*Telework, time use, and well-being: Evidence from the 2022 Time Use Survey* is a study by Dana Wray published by Statistics Canada on 5 June 2024 (catalogue no. 89-652-X). It asks two questions: how teleworking from home is associated with how people use their time, and how it is associated with aspects of well-being such as [[DefinedTerm/work-life-balance]] and time pressure. The study presents itself as the first in Canada to use time use data to study telework since the start of the COVID-19 pandemic, noting that earlier Statistics Canada research on the topic dated from over 14 years before. It sets the question against the rise of working from home — the share of Canadians working most of their hours from home rose from 7% in May 2016 to about 24% in July 2022, then fell slightly to 21% in July 2023 — and against a "paradox" in the literature: telework may let workers reallocate commuting time and balance work and personal life, but may also lengthen work hours, deepen gender inequality in unpaid work and blur the boundary between work and home.

The study uses the 2022 [[Dataset/canadian-time-use-survey]], collected from July 2022 to July 2023, and compares three groups of employees (the self-employed are excluded) who did at least two hours of paid work on the diary day: non-teleworkers, who did not telework in the previous week and worked on-site on the diary day; on-site teleworkers, who teleworked in the previous week but worked on-site that day; and work-from-home (WFH) teleworkers, who teleworked in the previous week and worked at home that day. The survey defines telework as spending any time in the previous week working from home, a co-working space or elsewhere while using telecommunications tools; to exclude people who simply took work home, the study counts as teleworkers only those who teleworked for at least one regular working day. Because teleworkers differ from non-teleworkers in socioeconomic and job characteristics, the study mainly reports predicted daily minutes from regression models that hold diary, sociodemographic and job characteristics constant ([[DefinedTerm/working-from-home]]).

## Details

**Commuting and paid work.** Non-teleworkers were predicted to commute for about 63 minutes and on-site teleworkers for about 74 minutes, so WFH teleworkers redistributed over an hour a day to other activities; there was no significant difference in transportation for non-work purposes. Before adjustment, WFH teleworkers did 27 minutes less paid work than non-teleworkers and on-site teleworkers 16 minutes less, but once job characteristics were also accounted for there was no statistically significant difference in paid work time between the three groups.

**Housework and children.** WFH teleworkers did about 16 minutes more unpaid housework than non-teleworkers (82 against 66 predicted minutes); both men and women did more housework when working from home, but women did more housework than men whatever their telework status. Parents who teleworked from home spent 35 minutes more on childcare than non-teleworkers and 23 minutes more than on-site teleworkers, a difference driven primarily by fathers, and about 41 and 56 minutes more co-present time with children — in all, nearly an hour more time with children. Because both fathers and mothers working from home spent more time with children, the gender gap in total time with children did not change with telework status.

**Multitasking.** Those who worked at home were more likely to multitask during paid work than those who worked at the workplace (a predicted probability of 56% against 45%), and women were more likely than men to multitask wherever they worked ([[DefinedTerm/simultaneous-activity]]). Working at home was associated with a higher likelihood of doing unpaid housework (10% against 3%) and, for parents, unpaid care (13%) at the same time as paid work, and a lower likelihood of socializing during paid work.

**Sleep, personal care and leisure.** WFH teleworkers slept about 23 minutes more than non-teleworkers and 19 minutes more than on-site teleworkers, spent more time eating, and spent less time on personal care such as grooming and getting dressed (24 minutes less than non-teleworkers). They spent about half an hour more in leisure — about 23 minutes more passive leisure than non-teleworkers, mostly watching TV, and 10 to 11 minutes more active leisure such as exercise and sports.

**Well-being.** WFH teleworkers were 12 percentage points more likely than non-teleworkers and 14 points more likely than on-site teleworkers to be satisfied or very satisfied with their work-life balance. On-site teleworkers reported the most time pressure: they were more likely than both other groups to worry about not spending enough time with family and friends and to report constantly being under stress trying to accomplish more than they could handle, and 15 percentage points more likely than WFH teleworkers to cut back on sleep when they needed more time ([[DefinedTerm/time-stress]]). The study says the reasons are unclear and suggests that on-site teleworkers' slightly longer commutes, or the instability of alternating between work and home in [[DefinedTerm/hybrid-work]] schedules, may contribute.

**Limitations.** The survey cannot fully distinguish how often people work from home or precisely identify hybrid and fully remote workers; the sample of teleworkers is too small to analyse differences by gender, age, racialized group or disability status; diaries come from only one respondent per household; the study does not examine when activities took place; and the analysis of simultaneous activities does not estimate how long multitasking lasted.
