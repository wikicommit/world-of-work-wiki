Right to disconnect: Implementation and impact at company level is a research report published in 2023 by Eurofound, the European Foundation for the Improvement of Living and Working Conditions. It starts from the concern that the growth of telework and flexible working, and the use of mobile digital tools that make workers contactable at any time, have fostered an "always on" culture in which workers answer work communications and work additional, often unpaid, hours outside their contractual working time. Noting that, as of spring 2023, there was no specific EU legislation on the [[DefinedTerm/right-to-disconnect]], it sets out to add evidence on how the right is implemented in companies and what difference it makes to workers.

The report has two parts. The first reviews national legislation and collective agreements as of early 2023. The second presents a web survey of employees carried out in December 2022 in Belgium, France, Italy and Spain — the four countries with the longest-standing legislative provisions — comparing workers in companies with and without a right to disconnect policy; after data cleaning, 1,634 responses were used. A parallel questionnaire to human resources managers received only 13 responses and is summarised briefly. The authors caution that the survey used a non-probability, quota-based online panel limited to a subset of sectors, and that its findings must be interpreted in the light of those limitations.

## Details

**National frameworks.** In June 2023 nine Member States had legislation providing a right to disconnect: Belgium, Croatia, France, Greece, Italy, Luxembourg, Portugal, Slovakia and Spain; Ireland had adopted a code of practice in 2021. France was the first, through a 2016 provision of its Labour Code in force from 1 January 2017. The laws differ in coverage (some apply only to teleworkers or to workers using digital tools), in implementation (directly applicable, as in Portugal and Slovakia, or through negotiation at company or sectoral level with a company policy or charter as a fall-back, as in Belgium, France, Luxembourg and Spain) and in sanctions, which are rarely specific. Countries with legislation also tend to have more collective agreements addressing the issue.

**Out-of-hours contact.** Over 80% of respondents were contacted about work outside their contractual hours in a typical week, most often by colleagues, and almost 9 in 10 took some action in response, with 23% replying to all calls or messages. The most cited reasons for responding were feeling responsible for one's assignments (82%), wanting to stay on top of things (75%), because it was expected (75%), fear of a negative impact (61%) and hoped-for career progression (50%). Once other factors were controlled for, having a right to disconnect policy made little difference to how often workers were contacted or responded.

**Additional hours.** About 47% of respondents regularly worked beyond their contractual hours, most often to finish tasks they could not complete in working time (37%); only 17% did so mainly because they were contacted out of hours, and this was the type of additional work least likely to be compensated. In companies with a policy, fewer respondents worked additional hours because of out-of-hours contact (14% against 19%), and additional hours were more likely to be paid or compensated with time off. Workers covered by a policy also reported more autonomy over their working hours, suggesting the right does not limit desired flexibility.

**Well-being and satisfaction.** Around 45% of respondents said out-of-hours contact harmed their work–life balance and health, with women, 25- to 39-year-olds and remote workers more affected. Workers with a right to disconnect policy more often reported a good fit between working hours and private life (92% against 80%), reported fewer health problems such as headaches, backache and anxiety, and were more often very satisfied with their working conditions (29% against 15%); 72% of those with a policy rated its impact positively.

**Conclusions.** The report presents these as the first quantitative evidence of positive associations between company right to disconnect policies and work–life balance, health and job satisfaction, but concludes that a policy alone is insufficient: it must be combined with awareness raising, effective implementation measures, joint monitoring by management and employee representatives, and action on the causes of over-connection such as high workloads, with effective enforcement of existing rules, including those of the Working Time Directive.
