Arbeitszeit: Trends, Wunsch und Wirklichkeit ("Working time: trends, wishes and reality") is a research report by two researchers at the Institut für Arbeitsmarkt- und Berufsforschung (IAB), published on 6 November 2023 as IAB-Forschungsbericht 16|2023 under a CC BY-SA 4.0 licence (DOI 10.48720/IAB.FB.2316). Prompted by public talk of the four-day week, "quiet quitting", the pandemic, labour shortages and Generation Z, it asks whether there really is a "big trend towards leisure" in Germany, and whether the pandemic was a turning point.

To answer, it describes how the weekly hours employees would like to work and the hours they actually work have developed from 1985 to 2021, using the [[Dataset/socio-economic-panel]] (SOEP). It counts a gap of more than 2.5 hours a week between the two as a [[DefinedTerm/working-time-mismatch]] — underemployment when people want more hours, overemployment when they want fewer — and breaks the results down by sex, employment type (full-time, regular part-time and exclusively [[DefinedTerm/minijob]] employment), age group and band of hours. The self-employed, apprentices, people in military or civilian service and interns are excluded.

## Details

**Overall.** In 2021 the actual hours of two-thirds of employees did not match their wishes. Desired and actual hours have both drifted down since the early 2010s, and during the pandemic desired hours fell faster, so that employees would on average have liked to work 3.8 hours less in 2021 (3.1 hours in 2019). Throughout the period more men than women were overemployed and more women than men underemployed; in 2021, 49% of women and 58% of men wanted to reduce their hours — the highest shares observed — while about 15% of women and 9% of men wanted to work more. Women's desired and actual hours were 6 and almost 8 hours a week below men's.

**By employment type.** Full-time employees would like to work less. Full-time women worked 40.9 hours a week in 2021 and would have liked 34.7; full-time men worked about 42.3 and would have liked 36.8. Men's falling hours since the early 2010s reflect, according to the report, fewer paid and more time-off-in-lieu overtime hours and many men's wish for a better [[DefinedTerm/work-life-balance]]. Among regular part-time employees ([[DefinedTerm/part-time-employment]]), actual hours have risen for some time while desired hours stayed fairly stable: part-time women's desired hours, 25.3 a week, are almost 2 hours higher than 20 years earlier, but since 2019 their actual hours have been slightly above their wishes (by 0.8 hours in 2021), whereas part-time men wanted more than an hour more than they worked. Minijob holders still mostly want longer hours, by about 4 hours in 2021; women in minijobs would have liked 14.7 hours a week and men 17.9.

**By age.** The fall in desired hours among women up to 25 — seven hours since 2009 — is traced mainly to a rising share of students and minijob holders in that age group rather than to lower preferences as such. Excluding students, men up to 25 worked about 39 hours a week, almost as much as older men, and wanted slightly more hours than them. The share of men aged 26 to 40 who would prefer part-time work below 35 hours rose from almost 10% in 2000 to almost 30% in 2021, which the report links partly to fathers taking on more care work; among men over 40 it almost doubled from 14% to 27%, mostly in near-full-time hours of 26 to 34. Women's actual and desired hours fall with age once students are left out.

**Contractual hours and the four-day week.** Agreed hours have become more differentiated: 51% of women had contracts of under 35 hours in 2021 (45% in 2000), while among men contracts of 40 hours or more gained ground alongside more part-time. The share of employees wishing to work part-time rose from 55% to 62% among women and from 13% to 28% among men. Only a very small share want 32 to 34 hours, which would correspond to a [[DefinedTerm/four-day-week]] of a 40-hour week; for 28 to 34 hours the shares are a sixth of men and a quarter of women, rising by 5 percentage points among men since the pandemic.

**Conclusions.** The authors find desired hours of full-time and part-time employees remarkably stable over decades, with a moderate, pandemic-reinforced shift towards somewhat shorter hours among full-time employees rather than a strong decline, and no evidence of a "quiet quitting" trend. They argue that preferences point less towards general models such as a four-day week than towards further differentiation, since wishes change over the course of life — for example with care work and occupational autonomy — and that working time should be adjustable to individual needs and life phases. Because full-time and part-time work remain fairly separate in Germany and part-time workers rarely return to full-time, they see greater permeability between the two as a way to keep part-time work from becoming a trap.
