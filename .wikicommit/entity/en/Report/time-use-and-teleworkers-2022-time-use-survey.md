*Time use and teleworkers: Highlights from the 2022 Time Use Survey* is a release published by Statistics Canada in *The Daily* on 5 June 2024. It announced new data from the 2022 [[Dataset/canadian-time-use-survey]], collected from July 2022 to July 2023 — the first time since the COVID-19 pandemic began that the survey collected data on how people spend their daily lives — together with a new study of how [[DefinedTerm/working-from-home]] is linked to time use and well-being. Statistics Canada describes it as its first study on both teleworking and time use since the pandemic's onset. The release sets the study against the pandemic-era shift in working arrangements: the share of Canadians working most of their hours from home rose from 7% in May 2016 to 24% in July 2022, then fell to 21% in July 2023.

The study uses the survey's time diary to compare three groups of employees (not the self-employed) who did at least two hours of paid work on the diary day: work-from-home teleworkers, who teleworked in the previous week and worked at home that day; on-site teleworkers, who teleworked in the previous week but worked on-site that day; and non-teleworkers. Its estimates come from regression models that adjust for socioeconomic and job characteristics, such as gender, age, type of job and usual work hours, so that they can be read as predicted time in an activity with other characteristics held constant.

## Details

Teleworkers saved over an hour, on average, by not commuting on days they worked from home, whereas non-teleworkers commuted 63 minutes and on-site teleworkers 74 minutes. Paid work time, however, did not differ between the three groups once characteristics were taken into account. On days they worked from home, employees spent about 16 minutes (21%) more on unpaid housework than non-teleworkers on paid workdays, with both men and women doing more, although the gender gap in housework remained; working parents at home spent about 71 minutes more per day actively caring for, supervising or being with their children, while mothers still spent 52 minutes more with children than fathers whether or not they worked from home. Work-from-home teleworkers also slept 23 minutes more than non-teleworkers and 19 minutes more than on-site teleworkers, spent about 30 minutes more on active and passive leisure, and spent 24 minutes less on personal care than non-teleworkers.

On well-being, work-from-home teleworkers were 12 percentage points more likely than non-teleworkers, and 14 points more likely than on-site teleworkers, to be satisfied or very satisfied with their [[DefinedTerm/work-life-balance]]. Unexpectedly, on-site teleworkers were the most likely to report time pressure ([[DefinedTerm/time-stress]]) — for example, constantly being under stress, cutting back on sleep and worrying about not spending enough time with family and friends. Statistics Canada says the reason is not clear, but suggests that alternating between the workplace and home might make schedules less stable. The release draws on the study *Telework, time use, and well-being: Evidence from the 2022 Time Use Survey*, published in the series *Spotlight on Canadians: Results from the General Social Survey*.
