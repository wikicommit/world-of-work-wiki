*Employment and Social Trends 2026* is a report in the International Labour Organization's World of Work Series, published in Geneva in 2026. Its executive summary, subtitled "Steadiness amid high uncertainty", argues that although the global economy and labour markets have remained resilient and global unemployment is stable, the world continues to fall short of meaningful reductions in decent work deficits, and that improvement in the quality of employment has slowed over the past two decades.

The report attributes much of this slowdown to a slower transformation of economies towards sectors with more productive workers and better working conditions: the movement of workers across economic activities has halved globally over the last two decades, which the ILO links both to the deceleration in improvements in work quality and to weakening productivity growth. It concludes that, with sluggish global growth and falling official development assistance, countries will increasingly need to rely on domestic policies and drivers of economic transformation to promote decent work.

## Details

**Employment quality.** Between 2015 and 2025 the share of workers living in extreme poverty (less than US$3.00 a day) fell by only 3.1 percentage points, to 7.9 per cent, compared with a fall of 15 points in the previous decade, leaving 284 million workers in extreme poverty. In low-income countries, extreme and moderate working poverty rates rose over the period, to almost 68 per cent of workers in 2025. The global rate of [[DefinedTerm/informal-employment]] rose by 0.3 percentage points between 2015 and 2025 after falling in the previous decade, mainly because employment grew in countries with high informality, chiefly in Africa and Southern Asia; 2.1 billion workers are projected to be informally employed in 2026. The incidence of own-account work also rose again.

**Unemployment and employment.** The global unemployment rate was estimated at 4.9 per cent in 2025, unchanged from 2024, and is projected to stay at a similar level until 2027, with 186 million unemployed in 2026 and a broader jobs gap of 408 million. Slower labour force growth amid demographic shifts is stabilising unemployment rates despite weak employment growth: the global labour force participation rate is projected to fall by around 0.2 points a year, to 60.5 per cent in 2027. Employment growth is projected at 1.0 per cent in 2026, with employment forecast to decline in high-income countries and to grow by 3.1 per cent in low-income countries.

**Gender and youth.** Women represented only two fifths of global employment in 2025 and were 24.2 percentage points less likely than men to be in the labour force, while young women were 14.4 points more likely than young men to be not in employment, education or training (NEET). The global youth unemployment rate rose to 12.4 per cent in 2025 and the youth NEET share to 20.0 per cent. The report notes preliminary evidence from high-income countries that young people with advanced education entering the labour market may face greater difficulties because of AI adoption.

**Wages and trade.** The global labour income share, at 52.6 per cent in 2025, remained below its 2019 level of 53.0 per cent, indicating that real wage growth has not kept up with productivity growth. Around 465 million jobs in 80 countries and territories depended on foreign demand in 2024, about 15.3 per cent of global employment; sectors with more trade-linked employment tend to have lower informality and better pay, and ILO modelling suggests that a moderate increase in trade policy uncertainty may reduce real wages across all sectors.
