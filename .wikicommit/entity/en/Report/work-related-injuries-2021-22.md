*Work-related injuries* is the Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS) release on people who experienced a work-related injury or illness, including the type of injury, details of the job in which it occurred and work-related injury rates. The 2021-22 financial year issue was released on 15 February 2023 (the release previously used catalogue number 6324.0). Of the 14.1 million people who had worked at some time in the previous 12 months, 497,300 experienced a work-related injury or illness in that period — a rate of 3.5%, down from 4.2% in 2017-18 and 6.4% in 2005-06.

The ABS notes that trends in work-related injuries are influenced by many factors, including improvements in workplace health and safety and a shift towards clerical and service-related work. The 2021-22 data were collected between July 2021 and June 2022 and cover injuries in the 12 months before interview (as far back as July 2020); the ABS advises caution in reading the period, because reduced economic activity during COVID-19 lowered the likelihood of being injured at work.

## Details

**Who was injured.** Men made up 58% of those injured. The rate for men fell from 4.4% in 2017-18 to 3.9% in 2021-22, and for women from 4.1% to 3.1%. By age, the highest rate was among people aged 55-59 (4.7%), followed by those aged 20-24 (4.0%, down from 5.5% in 2017-18). Of the people injured, 85% continued in the job where the injury occurred, 8% changed jobs and 7% left or lost that job and were not working at interview.

**Injuries and causes.** The most common injuries were sprains, strains or dislocations (26%), chronic joint or muscle conditions (20%) and cuts or open wounds (12%), the same leading types as in 2017-18. The most common causes were lifting, pushing, pulling or bending (24%), falls on the same level including slips (17%) and hitting or being hit or cut by an object or vehicle (11%). Most injuries happened in the workplace (91%); 6% occurred while travelling for or to and from work or during breaks, and 1% while working from home.

**Jobs, occupations and industries.** Of those injured, 88% were employees in the job where the injury occurred — 68% with and 20% without paid leave entitlements — and 12% were owner managers; 63% were working full-time and 32% under [[DefinedTerm/shift-work]] arrangements. The occupations with the highest injury rates were community and personal service workers (7.0%), machinery operators and drivers (6.5%), labourers (5.7%) and technicians and trades workers (5.3%). By industry, rates were highest in accommodation and food services (5.6%), agriculture, forestry and fishing (5.4%) and transport, postal and warehousing (5.1%), and lowest in professional, scientific and technical services (1.0%). Injured men were most often technicians and trades workers (24%), while injured women were most often community and personal service workers (27%) or professionals (20%).

**Time off, compensation and reporting.** Two in three people injured (66%) had time off work, and 34% of those took more than ten days off. Stress or other mental health conditions carried the highest average absence, at 44 days, followed by fractures (29 days) and chronic joint or muscle conditions (22 days). 31% of people injured received workers' compensation, up from 27% in 2017-18. Of the 323,700 who did not apply for it, 39% considered the injury minor or compensation unnecessary, 14% did not think they were eligible and 10% were not covered or not aware of it. 88% reported the injury to someone in the workplace, most often a supervisor or line manager (77%).

Microdata from the work-related injuries collection, covering September 2000 to 2021-22, is available in ABS DataLab as a supplementary file to the Longitudinal Labour Force microdata.
