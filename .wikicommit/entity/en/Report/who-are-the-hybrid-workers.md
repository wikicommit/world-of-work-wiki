*Who are the hybrid workers?* is an article by the UK Office for National Statistics (ONS), published on 11 November 2024, almost five years after the start of the COVID-19 pandemic. It looks at the types of workers most likely to follow a [[DefinedTerm/hybrid-work]] pattern — part travelling to work and part working at home — and at how people spend their time on days they work from home. It combines data from three ONS sources: the Opinions and Lifestyle Survey, which asks adults about their well-being and working patterns; the Time Use Survey; and the Business Insights and Conditions Survey of businesses.

Its headline finding is that more than a quarter of working adults in Great Britain (28%) were hybrid working in autumn 2024, based on data collected between 2 and 27 October. The ONS observes that while working only from home has fallen since 2021, a hybrid model has become the "new normal" for around a quarter of workers, though more for some workers than others.

## Details

**Who hybrid works.** Using Opinions and Lifestyle Survey data for 10 April to 30 June 2024, the article reports that:

- 29% of workers aged 30 and over were hybrid working, compared with 19% of those aged 16 to 29;
- working parents were more likely to hybrid work than non-parents (35% against 24%), and working fathers more likely than working mothers (41% against 30%);
- 45% of managers, directors and senior officials hybrid worked, against 27% of all workers and 3% each in elementary occupations and in caring, leisure and other services, with professional occupations also above average;
- workers with a degree or equivalent were about ten times as likely to hybrid work as those with no qualifications (42% against 4%).

The ONS suggests explanations but presents them as possibilities: older workers are more likely to be parents and managers; parents may find hybrid working helps with childcare or working around the school day; senior staff may have more desk-based work even in high-contact industries; and people with higher qualifications may have more access to jobs that can be done remotely. It notes that its data did not consider people with other unpaid care responsibilities, such as caring for an elderly relative.

**Industry.** In the Business Insights and Conditions Survey for December 2023, hybrid working was most common among businesses in information and communication (49%) and in professional, scientific and technical activities (42%), and least common in accommodation and food service activities (4%), with manufacturing, construction and transportation and storage also low — industries the article describes as often requiring site-specific or manual tasks, in-person collaboration or face-to-face customer roles.

**Time use.** According to the Time Use Survey, people working from home saved an average of 56 minutes that day by not commuting. In March 2024, those who worked from home on a given weekday spent on average 24 minutes more on sleep and rest and 15 minutes more on exercise, sports and well-being than those working away from home. They reported about 10 minutes less working time, but the ONS says its estimates are not precise enough to tell whether that difference is real, and that the data cannot show how time use affects productivity. "Improved staff well-being" has remained one of the reasons businesses most often give for using or intending to use more hybrid and home working.
