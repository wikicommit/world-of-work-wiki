*Managing psychosocial risks at work* is a set of guidelines for all businesses published by WorkSafe New Zealand in April 2025. It is addressed to persons conducting a business or undertaking (PCBUs) and explains how to recognise, manage and prevent [[DefinedTerm/psychosocial-risks]] and how to respond to harm. Its starting point is the [[Legislation/health-and-safety-at-work-act-2015]] (HSWA), which defines health as physical and mental health: a business must ensure, so far as is [[DefinedTerm/reasonably-practicable]], the health and safety of its workers, so managing psychosocial risks is a legal obligation rather than an optional extra, and businesses must consult workers when deciding how to manage them.

The guidelines distinguish what the law requires ("must") from recommended practice ("should"), and state that they do not cover general wellbeing, health promotion or mental illness unless these are directly linked to workplace risks; wellbeing measures are encouraged but do not replace the legal duty. They also stress that workplace health and safety concerns risks and harm at work, not medical diagnoses.

## Details

The guidelines define psychosocial hazards as factors at work that may cause psychological and physical harm, arising from how work is designed, from social factors at work, or from the work environment, and psychosocial risk as the likelihood that such a hazard will cause harm. Exposure can lead to mental illness, anxiety, depression, fatigue, burnout, cardiovascular disease and musculoskeletal disorders, and to social harms such as substance use and conflict between work and family life. Common hazards listed include [[DefinedTerm/long-working-hours]], unmanageable workloads, low job control, shift work without enough rest, constant digital monitoring, poor leadership, bullying or harassment, job insecurity and low pay, working remotely or in isolation, and exposure to traumatic events. Leaders are given a central role: officers must exercise due diligence to ensure the business meets its obligations, and the guidance lists protective factors — supportive leadership, psychological safety, role clarity, sustainable workloads and access to mental health resources — that help prevent harm.

The core of the guidance is a risk management cycle. Businesses identify hazards by asking workers and by examining sources such as workload data, overtime hours, rosters, incident records and complaints; they assess risk by considering the frequency, duration and intensity of exposure and the likely consequences, bearing in mind that hazards can combine and that some workers, such as young, new or informally employed workers, are more at risk. Control measures should put most effort into eliminating risks through the way the organisation operates — adequate staffing, workable schedules, clear roles and fair policies — then into minimising them in everyday work, and finally into minimising harm where risks are inherent to a role, for example through support services and return-to-work planning. Businesses that share duties must consult, cooperate and coordinate with each other, and control measures must be monitored and reviewed with workers on an ongoing basis.

Where harm occurs despite these measures, the guidelines set out how to respond: engage with the worker early, offer support people and employee assistance, make reasonable adjustments, respond to traumatic events, notify WorkSafe where required, and manage recovery at work or return to work. WorkSafe states that it may intervene when a business or sector shows a persistent pattern of serious harm or poorly managed serious risk, and is unlikely to intervene in one-off or individual cases. Appendices provide a glossary, a table of risk and protective factors, and summaries of HSWA duties, the reasonably practicable test, overlapping duties and worker engagement.
