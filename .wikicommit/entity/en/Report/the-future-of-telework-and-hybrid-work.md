The future of telework and hybrid work is a research report published in 2023 by Eurofound, the European Foundation for the Improvement of Living and Working Conditions, a tripartite EU agency. Starting from the observation that 48% of employees were working from home in July 2020, and that two years later a significant share of EU employees were still in hybrid forms of work, it uses a foresight approach to ask how telework and [[DefinedTerm/hybrid-work]] might develop in the EU by 2035 and whether policy action is needed. The report stresses that its scenarios are not predictions but a tool for dialogue among policymakers, employers, workers and their representatives.

For its purposes, the report defines telework using the 2002 EU social partners' Framework Agreement on Telework — work that could be performed at the employer's premises but is carried out away from them on a regular basis, using information technology, within an employment relationship — and treats hybrid work as a form of work organisation at the intersection of telework or remote work and on-site work. The scenarios were built with a key-factor-based methodology: a literature review, three online workshops with experts and stakeholders from research, trade unions, employers, governments and EU and international bodies, and the selection of nine key factors, such as the nature of work, crisis-driven shifts, mindsets towards the place of work, management skills, work organisation, regulation and technology adoption.

## Details

**Background.** Drawing on EU Labour Force Survey data, the report notes that fewer than 8% of employees worked from home sometimes or usually in 2008, rising to 11% in 2019, 19% in 2020 and 22% in 2021. Eurofound's Living, working and COVID-19 e-survey found that by spring 2022 two out of three respondents worked exclusively from the employer's premises and 12% worked entirely from home, while the share in hybrid work rose from 14% in summer 2020 to 18% in summer 2022. The report also summarises evidence on teleworkers' working conditions during the pandemic, including a greater likelihood of long working hours and of working in free time, a lower likelihood of reporting poor work–life balance, and more frequent reports of headaches, eyestrain and anxiety.

**Four scenarios to 2035.** (1) *Telework and hybrid work in an equitable world of work*: wide adoption across all Member States, collaborative high-autonomy work organisation, trained line managers and an EU directive that protects teleworkers' health and safety and establishes a right to request remote or hybrid work. (2) *Surging and selective telework and hybrid work*: overall growth but diverging Member States, with access concentrated among highly skilled white-collar employees and used as a recruitment tool, and EU regulation limited to some health and safety aspects. (3) *Shrinking and polarised telework and hybrid work*: overall decline, with flexibility largely confined to large companies in sectors such as IT and finance, and a patchwork of regulation. (4) *Disengaging from telework and hybrid work in a turbulent world*: a return to pre-pandemic levels amid economic and geopolitical crises, command-and-control management and no regulatory framework. Each scenario is illustrated with fictitious stories of individual workers.

**Implications.** Using Eurofound's job quality framework, the report finds that Scenario 1 produces the best outcomes for working time, work intensity, task discretion and autonomy, career prospects and gender equality; it links better control over working hours in Scenarios 1 and 2 to the adoption of a [[DefinedTerm/right-to-disconnect]]. Across the scenarios it discusses health and well-being outcomes such as fatigue, anxiety, work-related stress and [[DefinedTerm/virtual-presenteeism]].

**Cross-cutting issues and policy pointers.** Regardless of scenario, the report highlights the risk that the division between those who can and cannot work remotely becomes a new source of inequality, including for women; the need to review work organisation and give hybrid teams autonomy; the critical role and training needs of line managers; a patchwork of national regulation on issues such as the frequency of telework, cross-border telework, monitoring and the right to disconnect; job quality risks such as physical and psychosocial risks, working time and work–life balance; and the importance of consulting employees. It also discusses cross-border taxation, urban–rural effects, environmental effects and gender equality. Its policy pointers address employer organisations, line-manager training, national minimum standards, social dialogue at national and EU level (including the EU social partners' agreement to negotiate an update to the 2002 Framework Agreement on Telework), EU-level monitoring and support for small and medium-sized enterprises.
