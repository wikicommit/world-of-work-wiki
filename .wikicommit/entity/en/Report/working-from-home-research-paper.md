Working from home is a research paper published in September 2021 by the Productivity Commission, the Australian Government's independent research and advisory body. It examines the rapid increase in [[DefinedTerm/working-from-home]] caused by the COVID-19 pandemic, which the Commission describes as a "forced experiment" that showed many people could do their jobs at home as well as in the office. The paper asks what the change means for workers, firms and cities, how it will continue to evolve, and whether Australia's regulatory frameworks can deal with the issues it raises. It offers judgments rather than predictions, and concludes that the shift is broadly beneficial and that governments should aim to smooth, rather than impede, the transition.

The paper draws on 2016 Census microdata, the [[Dataset/hilda-survey]], Australian Bureau of Statistics business and household surveys, and Australian and international research, together with consultations and a roundtable with employers, unions, government agencies and researchers. An appendix sets out a simple economic model of how access to working from home changes labour supply.

## Details

**Scale of the change.** Using task-based occupational classification, the Commission estimates that about 35% of Australian workers in 2016 had jobs that could be done from home (see [[DefinedTerm/potential-to-work-from-home]]). Before the pandemic, about 8% of employees had a formal work-from-home arrangement, working a median of one day a week at home, and only about 2% of total hours were worked from home. During the pandemic the share of people working from home at least some of the time rose to around 40%, and remained just under that level even after stay-at-home orders eased in 2021. The Commission judges that the long-term level will settle between pre-pandemic and lockdown levels; if half of those who could work from home did so two days a week, just under 7% of all hours would be worked at home, and if all of them did so, about 13%.

**A second wave of experimentation.** Because the main benefit — the avoided commute — accrues to workers, workers generally want more work from home than firms do, although more firms than workers favour fully remote models. The paper expects these preferences to be reconciled through testing, negotiation, job switching and learning, with many firms trying [[DefinedTerm/hybrid-work]] arrangements. It finds the evidence on individual productivity mixed but judges that, at the economy-wide level, productivity is likely to stay the same or improve as firms and workers sort, learn and adapt. It sketches three long-term scenarios — continued growth, a stable plateau, and a partial retreat — without predicting which will occur.

**Regulation.** The paper finds Australia's work health and safety (WHS) laws, which are broad and principles-based and apply wherever work is carried out, well placed to handle more home-based work, while noting that employers' reduced visibility and control of home workspaces makes the question of what is [[DefinedTerm/reasonably-practicable]] more complex. It discusses the risk of work bleeding into home life and union calls for a [[DefinedTerm/right-to-disconnect]], and recommends that regulators and workers' compensation insurers monitor whether more working from home is associated with more psychological injury. It observes that most work-from-home arrangements will be set by workplace policies and individual agreements rather than the formal workplace relations system, which provides mainly procedural rights such as the [[DefinedTerm/right-to-request-flexible-working-arrangements]] and consultation on major workplace changes. It argues against a substantive entitlement to work from home as blunt, costly and contrary to experimentation, and points to the 2023 review of the model WHS laws as an opportunity to check that they keep pace with changing work practices.

**Cities.** The paper describes working from home as largely a shock to central business districts, where jobs amenable to remote work are concentrated. It expects some economic activity to shift toward the suburbs and regions, but judges that the continuing benefits of proximity, the hybrid model and falling office rents will keep city centres attractive. It does not expect more working from home to solve road congestion on its own, given the shift from public transport to car travel during the pandemic.

**Wellbeing and equity.** Using pre-pandemic HILDA data, the Commission finds that employees who worked from home reported similar levels of loneliness, mental health and family functioning to those who did not, but lower physical activity; they also reported higher satisfaction with [[DefinedTerm/work-life-balance]], although some people find it hard to switch off. Working from home was not associated with a fairer division of [[DefinedTerm/unpaid-work]] between partners. The benefits are likely to flow mainly to better-paid and more educated workers, but more remote work could open up employment for carers, parents of young children, some people with disability and people in regional areas. The paper also notes the risk that people who work from home miss out on training and promotion, which could weaken its potential to promote gender equality.
