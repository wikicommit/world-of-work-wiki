*Home-based teleworking and preventive occupational safety and health measures in European workplaces: evidence from ESENER-3* is a European Risk Observatory report commissioned by the European Agency for Safety and Health at Work (EU-OSHA) and published in 2021. Written by Stefania Capecchi of the University of Naples Federico II, it sets out to identify the main characteristics of employees who telework from home and of the workplaces that allow it, and the occupational safety and health (OSH) prevention practices applied, covering both physical risk factors — musculoskeletal disorders in particular — and psychosocial ones.

The report deliberately uses data collected before the COVID-19 pandemic, arguing that they describe non-exceptional working conditions: trends from the [[Dataset/eu-labour-force-survey]], the characteristics and health of teleworkers from the 2015 [[Dataset/european-working-conditions-survey]], and the risks and measures reported by establishments in the 2019 wave of the [[Dataset/esener]] (ESENER-3). It concludes that before 2020 home-based teleworking remained marginal and depended mainly on country-level factors and establishment size rather than on sector alone, and that teleworkers face particular psychosocial risks that call for preventive measures, especially support for micro and small establishments.

## Details

**Trends.** According to EU-LFS data, about 3.2% of EU-27 employees worked mostly from home throughout 2010–2019, while the share working from home sometimes rose from 5% to 8%; in 2019 Finland, the Netherlands and Luxembourg had shares almost three times the EU average. In 2020 the share working mostly from home jumped to 10.8%, with a tenfold increase in Italy. The report relates these differences to [[DefinedTerm/teleworkability]], the extent to which an occupation can be performed remotely with the available technology.

**Teleworkers in the EWCS.** Defining teleworkers as employees who work from home at least several times a week and use a digital device for at least three-quarters of their working time, the report identifies 1,177 respondents, 3.7% of employees in the EU-27 in 2015. Compared with non-teleworkers, teleworkers more often reported headaches and eye strain (48.8% against 36.2%) and anxiety (24.8% against 14.4%), sleep problems, always being stressed (20% against 8.7%), worrying about work when not working (about 33.7% against 12.9%), working in their free time daily or several times a week (34.3% against 6.9%) and working while sick (54.8% against 34.1%), while they reported pain in the lower limbs less often. Half of teleworkers (50.2%) sat all or most of the time. Teleworkers of both sexes more often cared for children or grandchildren daily. The report summarises that teleworkers are more frequently exposed to psychosocial risks including long working hours, [[DefinedTerm/presenteeism]] and increased caring and domestic responsibilities, and links the blurring of work and private life to the [[DefinedTerm/right-to-disconnect]].

**Establishments in ESENER-3.** The share of establishments with teleworking in place ranged from 30.5% in the Netherlands to 2.4% in Italy (EU-27 average 11.6%) and was highest in information and communication (37.7%). Establishments with teleworking in place reported prolonged sitting far more often than others (81.3% against 58%; see [[DefinedTerm/sedentary-work]]) and more often reported psychosocial risk factors such as time pressure (62.5% against 42.6%), poor communication and long working hours. Only 30.7% of them carried out risk assessments that also covered workplaces at home, rising to 42.8% in establishments with 250 or more employees. They were nonetheless more likely to have preventive measures in place — for example providing ergonomic equipment (83.1% against 65.2%), encouraging regular breaks, conducting an employee survey on work-related stress, having a procedure for bullying or harassment, offering confidential counselling and intervening when excessively long or irregular hours are worked ([[DefinedTerm/psychosocial-risks]], [[DefinedTerm/work-related-stress]]).

**Models.** Two heteroskedastic probit models estimated on 34,741 establishments showed that larger size, belonging to a services group of sectors and being in the group of countries where more than 10% of establishments had teleworking in place all raised the probability of having teleworking in place, as did OSH outsourcing, stress awareness and the two preventive measures tested (encouraging regular breaks and providing ergonomic equipment); the age composition of the workforce was not significant. The report suggests that the provision of equipment is shaped more by legal and regulatory systems, whereas measures such as encouraging breaks more directly reflect company culture and management style.

**Limitations and recommendations.** The analysis rests on self-reported data from surveys designed to study working conditions in general rather than telework specifically, and the report calls for a survey designed for teleworkers. It recommends identifying country-specific determinants of teleworking, policies that counter its identified negative effects on OSH — for example by limiting availability for work during non-working time — and action to help micro and small establishments develop OSH measures.
