*Healthy and safe telework: technical brief* was published in 2021 by the World Health Organization and the International Labour Organization, developed by a joint WHO/ILO technical advisory group. It is aimed at employers, teleworkers and worker representatives, and gives technical information on the impact of telework on health, safety and well-being together with practical advice on organizing telework according to existing WHO guidelines and ILO standards. It was based on a rapid review of the literature from 2000 to June 2021 — 47 articles were included after screening — and on a scoping of relevant WHO guidelines and ILO standards; the brief states that the review was not intended to produce evidence-based recommendations.

The brief defines telework as the use of information and communications technology — desktop computers, laptops, tablets and smartphones — for work performed outside the employer's premises, whether at home, in a satellite office or elsewhere (see [[DefinedTerm/working-from-home]]); "hybrid" work combines telework with work on the employer's premises (see [[DefinedTerm/hybrid-work]]). Its central message is that, when organized and carried out properly, telework can benefit physical and mental health and social well-being, but that telework settings may fail to meet the occupational safety and health standards of traditional worksites.

## Details

**Prevalence.** The brief describes the COVID-19 pandemic as causing an unprecedented and rapid transition to telework: in Europe, the share of workers teleworking rose from 11% before the pandemic to 48% during it, and more than 23 million people in Latin America and the Caribbean moved to telework in the second quarter of 2020. Most of the transition occurred among workers with higher income and education.

**Health impacts.** The evidence reviewed is mixed and described as preliminary. Some studies found lower blood pressure while teleworking and more physical activity among teleworkers, while poor workstation design and long hours can cause problems, and some teleworkers continued working while sick. Studies disagree on whether telework reduces or increases work-related stress; several report social isolation, and a review found more conflict between work and family than with traditional schedules. A Japanese study during the pandemic found that workers who preferred to telework experienced less psychological distress as telework frequency rose than those who did not, suggesting that preference shapes telework's mental health impact.

**Physical environment and ergonomics.** Poor workstation set-up can cause musculoskeletal injuries such as neck or lower back pain, and eye strain. The brief recommends a private, quiet and secure dedicated workspace, an adjustable ergonomic chair, external keyboards and monitors for laptops, glare control, cable management, adequate ventilation and noise control, varied postures and regular stretch breaks, and training in workstation set-up. Employers should ensure, as far as reasonably practicable and commensurate with their degree of control, that telework office space meets ergonomics standards.

**Psychosocial risks.** Telework can bring faster work pace, longer hours, interference with work–life balance, isolation and detachment, communication difficulties, cyberbullying, and a higher risk of domestic violence and harassment ([[DefinedTerm/psychosocial-risks]]). Employees working from home tend to work longer hours, partly because commuting time is replaced by work and the boundaries between paid work and personal life blur. Recommended measures include setting boundaries on working hours, employers avoiding contact outside scheduled hours, regular virtual social interaction, attention to the number of back-to-back online meetings, and refraining from excessive monitoring or surveillance, which reduces trust and may increase stress. The brief notes that some countries have established a [[DefinedTerm/right-to-disconnect]].

**Roles and responsibilities.** Drawing on the ILO Occupational Safety and Health Convention, 1981 (No. 155) and the Promotional Framework for Occupational Safety and Health Convention, 2006 (No. 187), the brief sets out that governments should cover telework in national occupational safety and health policy, that employers should manage telework risks using the hierarchy of controls — for example hybrid models, ergonomic equipment, scheduling software, ICT support, telework policies, training and regular breaks — and that workers should cooperate with employers. It points to ILO conventions on working hours and weekly rest for commerce and offices, and to the 2002 European Union Framework Agreement on Telework, under which teleworkers keep the same legal protections as workers at the employer's premises. Occupational health services are urged to expand delivery through online consultations, tele-ergonomic assessments and digital mental health support, and to assess and report work-related injuries and illnesses among teleworkers, which may otherwise be under-reported.
