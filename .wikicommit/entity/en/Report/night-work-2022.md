*Night work, 2022* is an article in Statistics Canada's *Quality of Employment in Canada* series (catalogue no. 14-28-0001-X), released on 13 June 2023. It presents the night work indicator: the number of workers whose usual schedule in their main job is a regular night shift or night hours, expressed as a percentage of all employed persons. Statistics Canada sets this against the International Labour Organization's Night Work Convention, 1990 (No. 171), under which night work is work performed during a period of no less than seven consecutive hours that includes the interval from midnight to 5 a.m.; its own data cover people who work regular night shifts or night hours and do not limit the definition to seven consecutive hours.

The article treats [[DefinedTerm/night-work]] as a relatively uncommon schedule that is nonetheless worth tracking because the workers on it may find it harder to balance work and personal life. It notes that night work can be a voluntary adaptation of work hours, but that many people work at night because of operational needs in settings such as hospitals and factories; that it may affect workers' ability to combine their hours with care and family responsibilities; and that night shifts are associated with a higher risk of depression, metabolic syndrome and other health conditions, citing meta-analyses of observational studies.

The figures come mainly from a supplement to the Labour Force Survey collected in April 2022 as part of Statistics Canada's labour market indicators program, among workers aged 15 to 69 living in the provinces; they refer to the main job and are not seasonally adjusted. The sample consisted of households in their second to fifth month of participation in the Labour Force Survey, and the analysis reports differences that are statistically significant at the 95% confidence level. Earlier data points come from the 2016 General Social Survey (Canadians at Work and Home) and the 2020 Survey on Quality of Employment.

## Details

**Prevalence and trend.** In April 2022, 323,000 people, or 1.7% of workers aged 15 to 69, usually worked a regular night shift or night hours. Although the rate may be affected by seasonal employment patterns, data collected at other times of year suggest it has been relatively stable: 2.0% of workers had a regular night shift in the fall of 2016, and 1.8% of those working or who had worked in the previous 12 months had a regular night shift or night hours in March 2020, just before the COVID-19 pandemic.

**Who works at night.** Night work was more common among men (2.1%) than women (1.3%), with little difference between workers aged 15 to 24, 25 to 54 and 55 to 69. Among workers aged 25 to 69, those with a high school diploma or less (2.5%) were more than twice as likely to work at night as those with a bachelor's degree or higher (0.9%). Landed immigrants (2.3%) were more likely to work at night than people born in Canada (1.5%), and among racialized groups Filipino (4.3%) and Black workers (3.3%) were among the most likely to do so, compared with 1.4% of workers who were neither racialized nor Indigenous.

**Industries, occupations and job characteristics.** In no industry or major occupational group did night work exceed 10% of workers. Transportation and warehousing had one of the highest rates (5.5%), and rates were also relatively high in business, building and other support services (4.5%), accommodation and food services (2.8%), retail trade (2.7%) and manufacturing (2.7%). By occupation, the share was relatively high among processing and manufacturing machine operators and related production workers (6.6%), labourers in processing, manufacturing and utilities (6.4%) and assisting occupations in support of health services such as nurses' aides and orderlies (5.9%). Private sector employees (2.1%) were more likely to work at night than public sector employees (1.3%) and the self-employed (0.6%), while night work was equally common among full-time and part-time workers (1.7% each) and differed little between permanent (1.9%) and temporary (1.8%) employees.

**Flexibility and place of work.** Night workers had less control over their schedules: 13.5% could choose when they started and finished their workday, against 37.2% of workers with regular daytime hours. Even while [[DefinedTerm/working-from-home]] was elevated during the COVID-19 pandemic, 97.2% of night workers usually worked exclusively at locations other than their home, compared with 72.2% of regular daytime workers.
