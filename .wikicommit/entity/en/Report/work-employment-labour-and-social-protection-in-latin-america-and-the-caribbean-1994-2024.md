*Trabajo, empleo, protección laboral y social en América Latina y el Caribe, 1994–2024* ("Work, employment, and labour and social protection in Latin America and the Caribbean, 1994–2024") is number 50 in the ILO's Southern Cone technical report series (Informes Técnicos OIT Cono Sur), published on 17 April 2025 and presented in an ILO press release from Santiago the same day. It was prepared by Fabio Bertranou, Deputy Director of the ILO Office for Latin America and the Caribbean, and Sonia Gontero, Specialist in Wages and Working Time at the ILO Office for the Southern Cone of Latin America. Drawing on key indicators and structural trends, it analyses the main changes in the world of work in the region over the last three decades, covering progress, lags and new challenges in employment, job quality, labour and social protection, gender equality, youth, technology and climate change.

The report finds that job quality, labour inclusion and social protection have improved substantially since the mid-1990s, but that structural transformation remains insufficient to create the quality jobs the region's growing labour supply needs, to raise productivity so that the gains from growth are better distributed, and to secure fairer and more sustainable working lives for all. It argues that, amid growing global uncertainty, investing in [[DefinedTerm/decent-work]], social protection and strong labour institutions is not only a matter of social justice but also a strategic bet on more sustainable and resilient growth.

## Details

**Three decades of change.** Among the points the ILO highlights from the report:

- The productivity gap between large and small enterprises persists and limits the creation of quality jobs.
- Labour informality (see [[DefinedTerm/informal-employment]]) still affects roughly half of workers in the region, with significant differences between countries, sectors and population groups, which constrains economic growth, social protection and productivity.
- Intraregional migration has gained relative importance, and the labour integration of migrants is a major policy challenge for destination countries.
- Young people are on average better educated than earlier generations, but those from more vulnerable households still face major obstacles in accessing formal, well-paid jobs that allow upward career paths.
- The substantial entry of women into paid work has shaped the region's labour market, though there is still considerable room to improve their full labour market integration.
- Labour institutions have advanced, through the ratification of international conventions, improvements in labour legislation, stronger minimum wages (see [[DefinedTerm/minimum-wage]]), reductions in excessive working hours, wider collective bargaining coverage and the promotion of social dialogue.
- Structural reforms have improved social protection systems, but their governance and the coordination between contributory and non-contributory components need further strengthening.

**New challenges for the future of work.** The report examines the technological, environmental and demographic transitions. Technological advances have allowed new business models to expand through digital platforms, and the spread of artificial intelligence, particularly generative AI, is transforming the organisation of work. Climate change poses a double challenge: adapting to put safety and health at work first in the face of extreme climate factors, and retraining so as not to leave behind the workers, communities and sectors that depend on the most polluting industries — which requires coordinating labour, training, production and social protection policies. The region also has high labour force participation among people above the legal retirement age, mainly out of economic necessity; with rapid population ageing, the report calls for policies that support a desired extension of working life by redesigning active employment policies and modernising labour and social security institutions.

**Recommendations.** The report concludes with a call to strengthen labour institutions as a pillar of social cohesion and economic sustainability. Its main recommendations are to promote integrated productive development and employment policies with a territorial and just-transition approach; to drive labour formalisation through integrated measures — incentives, labour inspection, training, access to finance and social protection; to reinforce tripartite social dialogue to build consensus on the regulation of working time, minimum wages, collective bargaining and labour rights; and to guarantee inclusive working lives for young people, women and older people by adapting public policy to new employment realities.
