*Satisfaction with work-life balance: Fact sheet* is a Statistics Canada publication in the series *Spotlight on Canadians: Results from the General Social Survey* (catalogue no. 89-652-X), released on 14 April 2016. It examines satisfaction with [[DefinedTerm/work-life-balance]] among mothers and fathers of children aged 17 and under who work full-time (30 hours or more a week), using data from the 2012 General Social Survey.

The fact sheet sets the question against the rise of dual-earner families — 69% of couple families with at least one child in 2014, up from 36% in 1976 — and against growing concern about work-life balance, especially for women, who continue to devote on average more time than men to raising children and household chores and are more likely to be caregivers.

## Details

**Overall satisfaction.** In 2012, 75% of parents working full-time were satisfied or very satisfied with their work-life balance; 15% were neither satisfied nor dissatisfied, 8% dissatisfied and 1% very dissatisfied. Among those not satisfied, the most frequently mentioned reason was not having enough time for family life (59%), followed by too much time on their job or main activity (36%) and not enough time for other activities (13%). Mothers were a little less likely than fathers to be satisfied (72% against 78%).

**Family situation and caregiving.** 67% of lone parents were satisfied, against 76% of parents in couples. Parents with at least one child aged 0 to 4 were less often satisfied (72%) than those whose children were all aged 5 to 17 (77%). Parents who were also caregivers for a family member or friend — described as "sandwiched" between raising children and caring for a sick relative or friend ([[DefinedTerm/sandwich-generation]]) — were less often satisfied (69%) than parents who were not caregivers (78%), and among these caregiving parents mothers (63%) less often than fathers (73%).

**Education and hours.** A higher level of education was not associated with greater satisfaction, and satisfaction differed little across occupational groups, although women in professional occupations were less satisfied than their male counterparts. Parents working 50 or more hours a week were less often satisfied (66%) than those working 30 to 39 hours (78%). Fathers were more satisfied than mothers regardless of hours worked: among those working 50 or more hours, 69% of fathers and 60% of mothers were satisfied.

**Schedules and workplace measures.** 69% of parents who worked shifts were satisfied, against 77% of those with a regular daytime schedule ([[DefinedTerm/shift-work]]). Among employees, 79% of those with a flexible schedule allowing them to choose when their work day starts and ends were satisfied, against 73% of those without one ([[DefinedTerm/flexible-working-hours]]); 80% of parents who felt they could use a flexible schedule without harming their career were satisfied, against 71% of those who felt they could not. The possibility of taking leave without pay — to care for children, for a spouse, partner or other family member, or for personal reasons — was also associated with greater satisfaction; the possibility of extended leave for personal reasons mattered especially for mothers (74% satisfied against 59% without it), and not for fathers. Working at home was not associated with different satisfaction for men, but women who at least occasionally did some of their paid work at home were more often satisfied (78% against 70%) ([[DefinedTerm/working-from-home]]).

**Provinces.** Parents in Prince Edward Island were the most satisfied (90%) and those in Manitoba the least (70%), against 75% for Canada. The gap between mothers and fathers varied by province: there was no statistically significant difference in Ontario, while in Saskatchewan 62% of mothers and 82% of fathers were satisfied, and the gap was also above average in Alberta and Quebec.

**Conclusion.** In a multivariate analysis holding all the fact sheet's factors constant, women remained less likely than men to report being satisfied. The 2012 survey did not collect data on how household chores and childcare were divided between fathers and mothers, and the fact sheet suggests that a study taking these into account could help explain the differences.
