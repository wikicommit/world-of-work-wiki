Jornada y horarios de trabajo. Datos a partir de la VII Encuesta Nacional de Condiciones de Trabajo ("Working hours and schedules: data from the 7th National Survey of Working Conditions") is a report by three authors at the National Centre for Working Conditions in Barcelona of Spain's National Institute for Safety and Hygiene at Work (INSHT). Prepared in October 2013 and published by INSHT in Madrid in August 2014, it presents the survey's main results on working time: usual working schedules, night work, work on Saturdays and public holidays, the need to extend the usual working day with or without compensation, and how far work can be combined with social and family commitments. The 2011 survey covered 8,892 workers, and several results are compared with the 2007 survey (11,054 workers).

The report frames working time as more than the number of hours: it distinguishes the working day (*jornada*) from the working schedule (*horario*) — how working time is distributed over a day or week — and gives particular attention to shift work, especially when it includes night work, because of its effects on health. It also notes that the combination of longer or irregular hours with time pressure and high workloads has given rise to a risk factor described as "intensification", and that growing social concern with combining work and personal life has increased interest in managing working time as a basic element of reconciling work and family life.

## Details

**Length of the working week.** The average working week was 38.5 hours, 1.5 hours less than in 2007. It was longest in agriculture (41.56 hours) and construction (41.43) and shortest in services (37.81), and more than 40 hours a week were worked in 27.7% of the smallest firms (1–10 workers). Men averaged 41.16 hours and women 35.59, because more women worked under 35 hours and more men over 40. Most respondents (85.8%) worked full-time. Part-time work, voluntary or not, was more common among women and among the young: 46.8% of the youngest women worked part-time, and only among women aged 35–44 and the oldest women was "voluntary" part-time work more common than involuntary. Part-time workers more often said their schedule let them combine work and family commitments "very well" (37.7% against 22.9% overall), but those working part-time — especially involuntarily — felt they had fewer chances to develop at work ([[DefinedTerm/part-time-employment]]).

**Extended working days.** Up to 40% of workers said they habitually extended their working day, with or without financial or time-off compensation, down from 44% in 2007 ([[DefinedTerm/overtime]]). Extending hours without any compensation was most common among company or public-administration managers (40.3%) and teaching staff (33.8%). Those who extended their working day without compensation were the least satisfied with the fit between their hours and personal commitments and reported worse perceived health (4.9% bad or very bad against 2.3% of those who did not extend their working day). Drawing on the literature, the report associates long daily hours with acute effects of tiredness such as drowsiness and inattention, and long weekly hours with both acute and chronic effects, including health problems and greater conflict between work and personal life. It also notes that a high share of night workers extend their working day, even though Spanish law limits night workers to an average of eight hours a day over a fifteen-day reference period and bars them from overtime.

**Schedules, shift and night work.** The most common schedule was a split day with a break of at least an hour for lunch (40.2%), followed by a fixed morning shift (28.6%). The report notes that Spain's Workers' Statute ([[Legislation/workers-statute]]) defines night work as work between 10 p.m. and 6 a.m., while the ILO uses a different definition, which makes comparisons harder ([[DefinedTerm/night-work]]). In the survey, 9.9% of workers had a usual schedule involving night work, and 7% did at least three hours between 10 p.m. and 6 a.m. daily or on more than half their working days. Night workers did not differ in perceived health, which the report links to the good adaptation night workers often perceive, but 12.5% had had an accident at work in the previous two years against 7% of other workers. They more often reported pain in the legs, knees or feet, trouble falling asleep, and tiredness or exhaustion; those working nights every day were less often offered and given a medical examination, and those always working nights less often had a workplace risk assessment or training. Night workers were also more exposed to threats and physical violence, but more often said they could get help from colleagues if they asked ([[DefinedTerm/shift-work]]). The report adds that age makes adaptation to irregular schedules harder, yet 7% of workers over 55 worked schedules involving night work.

**Weekend and holiday work.** Work on Saturdays and on public holidays declined between the two surveys. It was most common among the youngest workers and in hospitality and cleaning, domestic service, agriculture, commerce, and defence and security occupations.

**Fit with family and social life.** The share of respondents who found it hard to reconcile their working hours with family life (22.6%) was little changed from 2007 (23.1%); difficulties were greatest among vehicle drivers, commerce workers and hospitality and cleaning workers, and among company directors. Women more often than men said their schedule fitted family and social obligations very well (24.9% against 19.8%), which the report suggests may reflect women's greater use of contract types or schedule adjustments that help them balance demands. Difficulties were highest for rotating morning/afternoon/night shifts (38.5%) and fixed night shifts (37.1%), while 91.5% of those on a continuous morning schedule said it fitted their commitments well or very well. Workers who always or almost always worked weekends or holidays, and those with commutes over 50 minutes, also reported more difficulty ([[DefinedTerm/work-life-balance]]).

The report closes with a non-exhaustive list of Spanish general and sectoral regulations, European directives and ILO conventions and recommendations on the organisation of working time.
