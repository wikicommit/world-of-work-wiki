*World Employment and Social Outlook 2025: Asia and the Pacific May Update* is an ILO Brief published by the International Labour Organization in May 2025. It reports a moderate deterioration in the region's employment outlook, driven among other things by geopolitical tensions and trade disruptions. Before the recent trade uncertainty, employment in Asia and the Pacific was set to grow by 1.9 per cent (38 million jobs) in 2025; the ILO now estimates growth of 1.7 per cent (34 million), which is still expected to be the highest of any ILO region.

The brief finds the region susceptible to changing trading patterns, with nearly 3 per cent of total employment — more than 55 million jobs — linked to final demand in the United States through trade and supply chains, manufacturing accounting for nearly half. As workers and enterprises face heightened risks of disruption, it calls for efforts to improve their resilience, such as expanding and diversifying markets, improving social protection schemes and bolstering active labour market policies, including reskilling for in-demand occupations.

## Details

**Macroeconomic context.** Global GDP growth for 2025 is projected at 2.8 per cent, a downward revision. Asia and the Pacific remains the fastest-growing region, but given its exposure to trade uncertainty the International Monetary Fund has cut its 2025 growth forecast to 3.9 per cent (from 4.4 per cent), against 4.6 per cent in 2024 and 4.0 per cent projected for 2026, with risks to the downside. Exports account for a third or more of GDP in several countries, such as Cambodia (67 per cent), the Republic of Korea (44 per cent), Thailand (65 per cent) and Viet Nam (87 per cent). Following the IMF revision, the ILO lowered its forecast of global employment growth in 2025 from 60 million to 53 million jobs, with Asia and the Pacific accounting for more than half (4 million) of the revision.

**Trade-exposed jobs.** Across 71 countries with available data, 4.3 per cent of employment is directly or indirectly linked to final demand in the United States, a little more than 17 per cent in Canada and Mexico and 2.9 per cent in Asia and the Pacific. Manufacturing accounts for just over 27 million of the region's 55 million such jobs — close to 9 per cent of manufacturing employment — while the more than 28 million non-manufacturing jobs represent less than 2 per cent of employment in those sectors. East Asia has the largest volume (close to 15 million manufacturing jobs, notably in electrical and optical equipment, and nearly 11 million non-manufacturing jobs). In South-East Asia almost 14 per cent of manufacturing employment is linked to US final demand, mainly in textiles; in South Asia and the Pacific the shares are 6.9 and 5.4 per cent of manufacturing employment (textiles, and basic and fabricated metals, respectively), under 1 per cent of total employment. The estimate comes from input–output modelling with the Asian Development Bank's multiregional input–output tables (72 economies, 2023) across 35 sectors, assuming that within each sector the share of employment linked to US trade equals the share of value added; the brief notes that exporting firms may be more productive, which would mean employment linked through trade is overestimated. The regional analysis covers 25 countries and territories accounting for 95 per cent of the region's employment.

**Informality.** The region is home to around 1.3 billion informal workers, approximately 66 per cent of total employment, and more than eight in ten enterprises are informal ([[DefinedTerm/informal-employment]]). Incidence ranges from nearly 87 per cent in South Asia to 35 per cent in the Pacific Islands (around 27 per cent in East Asia excluding China). Progress towards formalisation over two decades has stalled since the onset of COVID-19, and the brief warns that slower employment growth risks making formal job creation harder still.

**Next steps.** Where fiscal space permits, the brief recommends properly designed, funded and shock-resilient social protection systems to soften job transitions; active labour market policies, including better job matching through employment facilitation and access to training and reskilling across occupations and sectors; and support for enterprises to access new opportunities, expand their markets (for example through intra-regional trade) and formalise. The brief has the DOI 10.54394/KQSG9490.
