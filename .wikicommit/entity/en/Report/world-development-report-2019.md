*World Development Report 2019: The Changing Nature of Work* is the World Bank Group's flagship report for 2019, published in Washington, DC, by the World Bank under a Creative Commons Attribution 3.0 IGO licence (ISBN 978-1-4648-1328-3; DOI 10.1596/978-1-4648-1328-3). It asks how technology is changing work, the firms that organise it and the skills that labour markets reward, and what governments can do in response. The report was prepared by a team led by Simeon Djankov and Federica Saliola. According to its foreword, it was the first World Development Report since the series began in 1978 whose draft was made publicly available online, updated each week, throughout the writing process; over seven months it drew thousands of comments.

The report's central argument is that fears of technology-driven mass unemployment are, on balance, unfounded: technology replaces workers in routine, "codifiable" tasks but also creates new sectors and tasks, and industrial jobs lost in advanced economies have been more than offset by the rise of industrial employment in East Asia. What technology does change is the demand for skills — rewarding advanced cognitive skills, sociobehavioral skills such as teamwork, and skill combinations predictive of adaptability. For emerging economies, the report identifies persistent informality and the absence of social protection for workers as the most pressing concern, one that predates the current wave of technological change.

In response, it proposes three areas for government action: investing in human capital, particularly in early childhood; enhancing social protection through a guaranteed social minimum and strengthened social insurance, complemented in some emerging economies by reforms of labour market rules; and creating fiscal space to pay for both. Together these would form what it calls a new social contract.

## Details

**Structure.** After an overview, seven chapters cover the changing nature of work, the changing nature of firms (including digital platform firms, superstar firms, competitive markets and tax avoidance), building human capital, lifelong learning, returns to work, strengthening social protection, and ideas for social inclusion. Chapter 3 introduces the World Bank's human capital index, which measures the productivity as a future worker of a child born in 2018 relative to a benchmark of complete education and full health, for 157 economies.

**How work is changing.** Estimates of jobs at risk from automation vary widely — from 2 to 41 percent in Bolivia, 5 to 56 percent in Lithuania and 6 to 55 percent in Japan — and the report judges such predictions unreliable, noting that most rely on automation probabilities assigned by machine-learning experts to U.S. occupational categories. Since 2001 the share of employment in occupations intensive in nonroutine cognitive and sociobehavioral skills has risen from 19 to 23 percent in emerging economies and from 33 to 41 percent in advanced economies, while the demand for routine job-specific skills is declining; developed countries show [[DefinedTerm/job-polarization]], but developing countries do so only unevenly. Technology is also giving rise to the [[DefinedTerm/gig-economy]], though the report's best estimate is that it involves less than 0.5 percent of the active labour force globally. By contrast, about two-thirds of the labour force in emerging economies is in [[DefinedTerm/informal-employment]], a share that has remained remarkably stable despite growth and regulatory improvements.

**Returns to work.** The report measures how much human capital workers accumulate on the job through [[DefinedTerm/returns-to-work-experience]], which are lower in emerging than in advanced economies, lower in informal than in formal jobs, lower for women than for men, and lower in rural than in urban areas. Across the world, 49 percent of women over the age of 15 are employed, compared with 75 percent of men; 65 economies restrict women from mining jobs, and in 29 of 189 economies women cannot work the same hours as men. The report proposes raising returns to work by creating formal jobs for the poor, removing legal restrictions on women's work, and raising agricultural productivity.

**Social protection and labour regulation.** Because the Bismarckian model of social insurance, financed by taxes on wages, presumes steady wage employment, clear definitions of employers and employees and a fixed point of retirement, it has remained largely aspirational where informality is the norm. The report proposes a guaranteed social minimum expanded according to [[DefinedTerm/progressive-universalism]], discusses a [[DefinedTerm/universal-basic-income]] as one option while calling it untested and fiscally prohibitive for emerging economies, and recommends social insurance that does not fully depend on formal wage employment — subsidised basic coverage, mandated earnings-based contributions applying at least initially to formal workers, and voluntary savings "nudged" by the state. As protection improves, labour regulation could where appropriate be made more flexible, for example by providing income support through unemployment benefits rather than [[DefinedTerm/severance-pay]], in a balance of security and flexibility of the kind associated with [[DefinedTerm/flexicurity]]. It also suggests that labour codes define more clearly what it means to be an employee, and notes that collective bargaining coverage in high-income countries fell from 37 percent of workers in 2000 to 32 percent in 2015.

**Financing.** Investments in human capital, basic social protection and productive opportunities for youth are put at 6–8 percent of GDP. The report suggests the resources could come mainly from better tax administration and policy changes — value added, excise, carbon and immovable property taxes, reforms of energy subsidies, and action against tax avoidance by multinational and platform companies — estimating, for example, that Sub-Saharan African countries could raise 3 to 5 percent of GDP in additional revenue through reforms that improve the efficiency of their tax systems.
