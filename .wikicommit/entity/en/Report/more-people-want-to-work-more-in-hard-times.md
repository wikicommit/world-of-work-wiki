Fler vill jobba mer i sämre tider ("More people want to work more in hard times") is a short analysis (*Kort analys*) by Statistics Sweden (SCB), last updated on 3 February 2026. Drawing on the Swedish Labour Force Survey (Arbetskraftsundersökningarna, AKU), it reports that [[DefinedTerm/underemployment]] — wanting to work more hours each week than one actually does — has become more common in Sweden. Today about six per cent of the employed want to work more hours per week than they usually do, and the share is largest among those working part time.

The analysis links the trend to economic conditions: underemployment turned upward in 2018 after several years of decline for both women and men, rose further when the pandemic brought redundancy notices, cutbacks and lay-offs in 2020, and has risen again in recent years, which SCB attributes to inflation and higher living costs. At the same time, women have come to work more hours than before, which has narrowed the gap between women and men.

## Details

During the pandemic many employers reduced their employees' working time instead of dismissing staff, and the share of the employed who were underemployed rose by about 2 percentage points between 2019 and 2020. In the early 2000s, 3–4% of men and 8–9% of women were underemployed; before 2020 the shares for women and men fell and moved closer together. SCB explains the fall among women partly by women working more hours per week over time, while men's underemployment has risen somewhat.

Underemployment falls with age. Over the past five years 17% of employed people aged 20–24 were underemployed — many in that group are still studying and work alongside their studies — against 4% of those aged 45–54. The number of extra hours the underemployed want has also fallen: in the early 2000s they wanted just over 12 more hours a week (men almost 14, women about 12), whereas after the pandemic's effects faded the average is about 9 hours, with little difference between women and men.

In 2024 underemployment depended strongly on a person's position in the labour market. About one in five people in [[DefinedTerm/temporary-employment]] (20.2%) was underemployed, which SCB relates to their shorter average weekly working time, against 4.7% of permanent employees and 5.7% of the self-employed. Among employees the difference between men and women was very small; among the self-employed the share was somewhat higher for women (7.4%) than for men (5.0%). By usual working time, 3.4% of those working full time (at least 35 hours a week) were underemployed, against 18.4% of those usually working part time ([[DefinedTerm/part-time-employment]]); among full-time workers the share was higher for men (4.4%) than for women (2.1%).

In AKU, respondents are asked how many hours they usually work per week and how many they would like to work. Everyone whose labour supply in hours per week is not met — who wants to work more hours per week than they usually do — is counted as underemployed. The underemployed are counted in the unused labour supply together with the unemployed and latent job seekers.
