Zur Regulierung von flexiblen Arbeitszeiten ("On the regulation of flexible working hours") is IAB-Stellungnahme 7/2018, a statement by a researcher at the Institut für Arbeitsmarkt- und Berufsforschung (IAB) for a public hearing of the Committee on Labour, Health and Social Affairs of the North Rhine-Westphalia state parliament on 2 May 2018. It comments, on the basis of research results, on a motion by the SPD parliamentary group of 9 January 2018, "Gute Arbeitszeiten sichern – Schutzrechte der Beschäftigten stärken – Die Digitalisierung der Arbeitswelt gestalten!" (state parliament document 17/1665), which discusses the [[Legislation/working-hours-act-germany]] of 1994 and its function in regulating [[DefinedTerm/flexible-working-hours]], and asks whether the act needs reform.

The statement starts from the distinction between the legitimate but different interests that establishments and individual employees have in flexible working hours. Establishments want to adjust working and operating hours quickly to fluctuating demand and so save costs, shorten delivery times, raise performance and productivity, and speed up innovation; employees want to adjust working time to demands and interests outside work — meeting obligations outside work, pursuing their interests, protecting their health and continuing their education. Because these can conflict, the author argues that combining them as well as possible is a core task of modern working societies that requires "flexibility compromises", and that using the term "flexibilisation" as if the interests naturally overlapped risks underestimating that task and calling for deregulation instead of using the collective agreements the law permits. Assuming the interests are always opposed is equally inappropriate: the statement cites establishments' use of short-time work and the running down of working-time account balances and overtime instead of mass dismissals in the 2008/09 recession as an example of interests that did overlap.

## Details

**Deviations from the normal working day.** The statement uses as its yardstick a "normal working day" of 35 to 40 hours a week spread over Monday to Friday, worked during the day and not varying in timing. According to the Microcensus, around a quarter of dependent employees frequently or regularly worked on Saturdays or in the evening in 2016, 16% regularly worked rotating shifts, 14% worked on Sundays and public holidays and 9% at night; weekend and evening work grew most since the 1990s with the move to a service economy, and more than half of employees worked in at least one of these forms at least occasionally. Using Socio-Economic Panel data for 2003–2011 as presented in earlier research, it reports that fewer than half of employees had fixed hours not varying in timing or duration, about a quarter had flextime with a working-time account and some self-determination, and 11% of men and 8% of women had no formal arrangement — which, it stresses, should not be confused with self-determined hours. It warns against treating self-determination of working time as a synonym for a successful work-life balance. Citing an employee survey, it also notes that around 17% of employees reported actual weekly hours of over 48.

**Establishments' perspective.** Analyses of the IAB Establishment Panel cited in the statement show part-time employees in three-quarters of establishments, overtime used in about half and working-time accounts in just under a third. Establishments use a wide range of instruments: shift systems that extend operating time to multiples of the normal week — from 40 to 120 hours with three shifts, or 168 hours in fully continuous systems — staggered hours, changes to part-time employees' hours, and flexible or regular Saturday work. A third of establishments use trust-based working time, which the statement reads as leaving employees to adapt their working time to the establishment's needs: what is left to them is the management of their hours, not the purpose.

**Constant availability.** In an excursus, the statement discusses the "delimitation" (Entgrenzung) of working time through smartphones, tablets and similar media, which lets work reach into evenings, weekends and commutes. It cites survey findings that 17.2% of employees fully agreed that modern communication means often turn their free time into working time — more among salaried employees with greater responsibility — and that 22% said they were expected to be reachable in their private lives while 12% were actually contacted there frequently. People contacted frequently report more time and performance pressure, less satisfaction with their work-life balance and more health complaints. Noting that employers' associations had proposed shortening statutory rest periods, it warns, citing a literature review, that permanently shortened rest periods can carry risks for physical and mental health and a higher risk of accidents, and that interruptions make it harder to switch off from work, on which recovery depends.

**Determinants of flexibility.** Research using linked employer-employee data from the Socio-Economic Panel, summarised in the statement, examined whether overtime and different working-time arrangements depend more on characteristics of establishments, jobs or employees. Establishment and job characteristics proved decisive, while employee characteristics played only a subordinate role: paid overtime depends most on establishment characteristics such as the industry, unpaid overtime on job characteristics such as a management position, flextime with a working-time account on establishment size, and self-determined hours on high job autonomy. The statement concludes from this that working-time flexibility in Germany is predominantly employer-oriented, which points to power asymmetries between employers and employees and to employees' legitimate need for protection.

**Conclusions.** The author finds that a large range of instruments for flexible working and operating hours already exists in Germany and that large parts of the workforce do not work a rigid "nine-to-five" day, so that there is in fact great flexibility. The 1994 act sets a binding framework that nonetheless permits sufficient flexibility, chiefly through the many deviations that collective agreements, or works agreements based on them, can make under its section 7. While demographic change and skill shortages might make employee-oriented arrangements more important, new compromises could be negotiated within that framework; the statement quotes IAB researchers suggesting that establishments could be allowed more flexibility, such as on the daily maximum, if unions or works councils agree and employers make concessions elsewhere. Because such room for manoeuvre has to be found through collective arrangements, the author argues, it prevents the power asymmetry between establishments and individual employees from working to employees' detriment, and makes the act an important precondition for reconciling the interests of both sides. As an illustration of how much the act already allows, the statement points to a collective agreement on mobile work for the North Rhine-Westphalia metal and electrical industry, which uses the act's exception to shorten rest periods.
