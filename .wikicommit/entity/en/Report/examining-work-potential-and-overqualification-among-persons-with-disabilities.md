*Examining work potential and overqualification among persons with disabilities* is a report in Statistics Canada's *Reports on Disability and Accessibility in Canada* series (catalogue no. 89-654-X), written by Diana Simionescu and Skyla Baroud and released on 4 June 2026, with a correction on 5 June 2026. It explores two forms of underrepresentation in the labour market among persons with disabilities aged 25 to 64: [[DefinedTerm/work-potential]] among those not currently employed, and [[DefinedTerm/over-qualification]] among those who are employed. The report frames both as possibly underutilized resources at a time when population aging, declining birth rates and instability in export-dependent industries are putting pressure on workforce capacity in Canada.

The data come from the 2022 [[Dataset/canadian-survey-on-disability]]. The report finds that 42.0% of non-working persons with disabilities had work potential and that, among employed persons with disabilities, 39.3% were well matched to their jobs, 34.0% overqualified and 26.6% underqualified, concluding that underutilization occurs both at the point of entry into the labour market and within employment through skill mismatch. The analysis is descriptive: the authors suggest that future research could use multivariate methods such as logistic regression to hold sociodemographic characteristics constant.

## Details

**Measures.** Work potential identifies non-working persons with disabilities who would be likely to enter paid employment in an inclusive labour market without discrimination and with full accessibility and accommodation; the report stresses that it does not measure individuals' capacity to work. Qualification status is measured objectively, by comparing a person's highest level of education with the level typically required by their occupation's TEER (training, education, experience and responsibilities) category in the National Occupational Classification; management occupations (TEER 0) are excluded. People are classified as overqualified, well matched or underqualified, based on the job held in the reference week of 2 to 8 May 2021 or, failing that, the longest-held job since 1 January 2020. A subjective measure — how qualified respondents feel for their job — is reported for context: 75.2% of employed persons with disabilities felt qualified, 19.6% overqualified and 1.9% underqualified. The report notes that the two measures may not refer to the same job.

**Work potential.** Among non-working persons with disabilities, work potential was 88.7% for youth aged 15 to 24, 42.0% for those aged 25 to 64 and 6.0% for seniors; youth and seniors are excluded from the rest of the analysis. Within the 25-to-64 group it was higher among those aged 25 to 44 (67.1%) than 45 to 64 (30.5%), and did not differ significantly between men and women, by Indigenous identity or by immigration status. It was higher in the Prairie provinces (49.0%), British Columbia (47.8%) and the territories (63.2%) than in Ontario (41.2%), lower in rural areas (35.4%) than population centres (43.5%), and higher among racialized persons (51.7%), 2SLGBTQ+ persons (60.7%), couples with children (53.4%) and lone parents (46.1%) than among the respective reference groups, as well as among those with a bachelor's degree or above (55.3%). It fell with severity — 61.6% for mild, 52.2% moderate, 40.1% severe and 24.1% very severe disabilities — and with the number of disability types, and was lower for people with mobility, flexibility, dexterity, pain-related or memory disabilities, for those with progressive limitations (27.7%), for those who experienced barriers to accessibility and for those who needed assistive aids or devices, whether or not that need was met.

**Overqualification.** Among employed persons without disabilities, 41.7% were well matched, 32.7% overqualified and 25.6% underqualified. Among persons with disabilities, overqualification differed significantly by immigration status (42.5% for immigrants against 31.9%), racialized group (41.8% against 32.4%) and place of residence (35.1% in population centres against 28.2% in rural areas), but not by gender, household composition or Indigenous identity. It was higher among those with very severe disabilities (39.6% against 32.0% for mild), four or more disability types (38.0% against 31.8% for one), and mental health-related, mobility or developmental disabilities, and lower among those with hearing disabilities (28.5% against 35.1%). Employment-related differences included non-permanent jobs (40.1% against 32.9% in permanent ones), recent hires (37.6% against 31.4%), no recent training (35.9% against 32.1%), working under 30 hours a week (41.9% against 32.9%), periods of unemployment in the past five years (39.5% against 30.5%) and having had to change the kind of work because of their condition (40.6% against 31.7%). Overqualification was also higher among those who felt their condition made it hard to change jobs or advance, or who considered themselves disadvantaged in employment because of it; differences by workplace size and by needs, or unmet needs, for workplace accommodations were not statistically significant. The authors relate these findings to existing research suggesting that persons with disabilities may encounter systemic and attitudinal barriers in the labour market.

**Correction.** A correction notice of 5 June 2026 states that the conclusion's estimate of overqualification among employed persons with disabilities was revised from "nearly one in three (30.3%)" to "around one in three (34.0%)" to align with the data in the tables; the corrected conclusion text itself reads 34.3%, while the report's tables and Section 2 give 34.0%.
