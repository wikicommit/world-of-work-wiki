Norges sykefravær passerte Sveriges og Nederlands etter lovendringer ("Norway's sickness absence overtook Sweden's and the Netherlands' after legal changes") is an analysis published by Statistics Norway (SSB) on 25 February 2019 in its *SSB analyse* series (2019/08), with the subtitle "Sickness absence in Norway and six other northern European countries 1983–2017"; its author is Håvard Lien. Following up an earlier SSB publication, it compares [[DefinedTerm/sickness-absence]] in Norway, Sweden, Denmark, Finland, Iceland, the United Kingdom and the Netherlands using the Labour Force Survey (Arbeidskraftundersøkelsen, AKU).

According to the Labour Force Survey, Norway has had higher sickness absence than Sweden and the Netherlands for the last 13 years covered, whereas before the turn of the millennium the situation was the reverse. SSB writes that much suggests Swedish and Dutch welfare reforms have contributed to the difference, and concludes that the likely reason for Norway's higher level is that sick people in Norway stay on sickness benefit, while in the other countries they are more exposed to being unemployed while sick.

## Details

**Measure.** Sickness absence is defined here as at least one week's absence from work because of one's own health problems, based on what Labour Force Survey respondents report. It therefore need not be medically certified absence, nor does the person need to be formally classed as on sick leave under their country's legislation. SSB notes that the survey is not designed to measure sickness absence but does so as a by-product of measuring employment and working time; because all European countries use the same variable definitions, it argues that nothing in the data source suggests Norway's results should differ from other countries', and that the measure is unaffected by differences in national sick-pay schemes.

**Trends.** Norway has had the highest sickness absence of the seven countries since 2006. Its level has been relatively even over the whole period, while absence has fluctuated considerably in the Netherlands and Sweden; Norway now lies above them because absence fell in those two countries, not because it rose much in Norway. Denmark, Finland, Iceland and the United Kingdom have also had fairly even levels throughout, with Norway above them over the whole period studied.

**Composition of the employed.** In Norway in 2017, age, disability and type of occupation played a large role in absence, while working time and being an employee or self-employed played a somewhat smaller one. Norway has somewhat more older people in work than Denmark, Finland, the Netherlands and the United Kingdom — roughly the same as Sweden — which probably explains part of its higher absence, but the age difference is small. There were no large differences in the share of women among the employed or in occupational composition, and Norway has a smaller share in the occupational group with the highest absence (cleaners, helpers and similar). Of the countries with data, Norway was the weakest at integrating people with disabilities into working life, so disability cannot explain its higher absence.

**People out of work because of ill health.** The share of unemployed people who left their last job because of health problems was at first sight similar in Norway and the other countries, but because unemployment was higher elsewhere (Denmark's was 35% higher than Norway's in 2017), a larger share of the population in those countries had lost their job through illness. Norway had by far the largest share of people outside the labour force who had stopped working because of health problems, which in isolation pulls its measured absence down.

**The Dutch reform.** The Netherlands carried out a major reform in 2002, the *Wet Verbetering Poortwachter* (rendered by SSB in Norwegian as the "gatekeeper improvement law"), under which company doctors issue sick notes and employers are responsible both for paying sick pay and for follow-up and reintegration of employees on sick leave. In the period right after the reform, the number of people present at work stayed practically stable, the number on sick leave fell and the number of unemployed rose, while the number outside the labour force because of health problems stayed stable. SSB cautions that rising unemployment after the dot-com bubble burst makes a clear-cut conclusion difficult, but reads the figures as showing that Dutch unemployment probably includes many people who in Norway would be counted as on sick leave.
