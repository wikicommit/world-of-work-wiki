This European Risk Observatory report, commissioned by EU-OSHA and published in 2020, investigates whether specific characteristics of the workforce — gender, nationality, sexual orientation and gender identity, and age — are associated with a greater likelihood of being in a job with poor working conditions, higher exposure to occupational safety and health (OSH) risks and health problems, with a specific focus on [[DefinedTerm/work-related-musculoskeletal-disorders]] (MSDs). It covers three groups: women workers, migrant workers and lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender and intersex (LGBTI) workers. The report was written by researchers from IKEI, Panteia and Oxford Research together with EU-OSHA; its contents are the authors' own and do not necessarily reflect EU-OSHA's views. It is part of EU-OSHA's 2018-2020 research programme on work-related MSDs and was meant to inform the Healthy Workplaces Campaign 2020-2022 on MSDs.

The study combined a literature review; secondary analysis of EU data sources, among them the 2015 [[Dataset/european-working-conditions-survey]], the [[Dataset/eu-labour-force-survey]], the [[Dataset/european-health-interview-survey]] and FRA surveys on violence against women and on LGBT people; interviews with 30 stakeholder organisations and six focus groups with workers in Denmark, Germany, Hungary, the Netherlands and Spain; nine policy case studies; and three validation webinars held in June 2020. Its conceptual framework treats MSDs as the result of combinations of physical, organisational and psychosocial risk factors, among which it counts [[DefinedTerm/workplace-discrimination]] and harassment.

## Details

**Health and MSDs.** The report finds that all three groups tend to be in poorer physical and mental health and report a higher prevalence of MSDs than other workers. In the 2015 EWCS, 60 % of women workers in the EU reported one or more MSDs — backache (45 %), muscle pains in the shoulders, neck and/or upper limbs (44 %) and in the lower limbs (30 %) — and every type was more common among women than men. Migrant workers report poorer health and more accidents at work, although in some countries this is confounded by the "healthy migrant effect" of young, healthy newcomers; second-generation migrants were more likely than native-born or first-generation migrant workers to report upper-limb MSDs (46 % against 42 % and 42 %). Evidence on LGBTI workers is limited, but they report poorer well-being and mental health and, according to the fieldwork, more musculoskeletal problems than the general population.

**Risk factors.** Most workers in these groups are exposed not to a single risk factor but to a combination. Women are exposed to lifting and moving people, repetitive movements, awkward postures and prolonged static standing or sitting — demands the report says are often underestimated — as well as emotional demands, the double burden of paid work and unpaid care, and tools, protective equipment and workstations designed mainly for male bodies. Migrant workers are especially exposed to heavy loads, awkward postures, repetitive movements and environmental hazards, and to language barriers and poor knowledge of their rights. In the 2015 EWCS, 17 % of first-generation migrant workers reported discrimination in the past year, against 8 % of second-generation migrants and 6 % of native workers. LGBTI workers face discrimination in recruitment and at work and often conceal their sexual orientation or gender identity, which the report treats as an additional psychosocial risk; transgender workers are the group most exposed to discrimination, abuse and bullying. The report also highlights intersectionality, where several disadvantageous characteristics combine in one worker.

**Regression analysis.** Logistic regressions on 31,662 EWCS 2015 respondents aged 18-65 working at least 12 hours a week found that discrimination was positively associated with backache and with upper- and lower-limb MSDs, and with anxiety, fatigue and sleep problems. Once mental-health indicators were added, the direct association between discrimination and MSDs became much smaller, supporting the hypothesis that it is mediated by mental health; once other risk factors were added, it was no longer significant. The higher MSD prevalence among workers born in another country disappeared once discrimination, sector and occupation were taken into account.

**Segregation.** Women and migrant workers are concentrated in sectors and occupations with high exposure to MSD-related risks — health, social care, hotels and restaurants and office jobs for women, and the so-called "3D" jobs (dirty, dangerous and demanding) for migrants (see [[DefinedTerm/occupational-segregation]]). Migrants made up 56 % of workers employed by households and 26 % in accommodation and food services in 2018. Evidence on LGBTI workers points to "prejudice-based segregation", the tendency to choose jobs where less intolerance and discrimination is expected.

**Case studies.** The nine examples analysed are Denmark's National Strategy for the Work Environment, Airbus's Diversity and Inclusion policy, the Shared Concerns and Joint Recommendations on migrant domestic and care work, a risk assessment toolkit for third-country nationals from Brescia (Italy), the Dutch Research Council's diversity policy, the Spanish Business Network for LGBTI Inclusion and Diversity (REDI), Osalan's toolkit for integrating a gender perspective into risk prevention (Basque Country), Sweden's Women's Work Environment initiative and a Transgender Workplace Support Guide from Scotland.

**Recommendations.** The report makes 18 policy recommendations, among them: more interdisciplinary MSD research that reflects workforce diversity rather than a "native-born heterosexual man-centred approach"; a diversity perspective in public authorities and labour inspectorates; a culture of inclusion and zero tolerance of discrimination in companies; participative MSD prevention; a gender perspective in OSH policy and treating work-life balance as an OSH issue; ergonomic and protective equipment adapted to women; better access to health services, language support and recognition of qualifications for migrant workers; and non-binary safety and health legislation and LGBTI company policies.
