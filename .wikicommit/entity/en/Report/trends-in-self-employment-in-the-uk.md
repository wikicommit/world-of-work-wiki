*Trends in self-employment in the UK* is an article by the UK Office for National Statistics (ONS), released on 7 February 2018, that analyses the characteristics, income and wealth of the self-employed. It starts from the rapid growth of [[DefinedTerm/self-employment]] in the UK labour market: the number of self-employed rose from 3.3 million people (12.0% of the labour force) in 2001 to 4.8 million (15.1%) in 2017. The ONS notes that earlier studies had examined the characteristics of the self-employed but largely sidestepped their income, partly because survey-based measures of income are harder to report accurately for people with irregular hours or project-by-project work.

The first part of the article uses the Labour Force Survey and the Department for Work and Pensions' Family Resources Survey to look at characteristics and income; the second uses Wave 5 (July 2014 to June 2016) of the Wealth and Assets Survey to compare the property, private pension and financial wealth of the self-employed and employees.

## Details

**Growth and composition.** Self-employment contributed more to employment growth after the economic downturn than its share of employment: while employee jobs fell during and after the downturn, self-employment jobs kept growing, contributing the most to employment growth until the last quarter of 2014 and around one-third of it afterwards. The increase was driven by people working on their own or with a partner but no employees — 4.0 million workers in 2016 against 2.4 million in 2001 — while the number with employees fell. Self-employment rose in every region, most as a share of employment in London, Yorkshire and The Humber, and the South East.

**Who became self-employed.** Among men, part-time self-employment grew fastest between 2001 and 2016 (from 273,000 to 569,000); among women both full-time and part-time self-employment grew strongly while the number of female employees was relatively stable, which the ONS reads as a shift in women's employment mix towards self-employment. The downturn saw a sharp rise in self-employment among people aged 65 and over (from 159,000 to 469,000 between 2001 and 2016), and part-time self-employment grew strongly among 16- to 24-year-olds. Growth was driven mainly by people with a degree or equivalent, whose share of the self-employed rose from 19.3% to 32.6%, so that relatively highly qualified people were becoming more concentrated in self-employment.

**Income.** In the financial year ending 2016, self-employed weekly earnings were centred around £240, against around £400 for employees. Full-time employees earned more than the full-time self-employed throughout 2001 to 2016, and the gap widened: by the financial year ending 2016, full-time male and female employees earned £533 and £428 a week, against £363 and £243 for their self-employed counterparts. Among part-time workers the pattern differed by sex — part-time self-employed men earned more than part-time male employees, while part-time self-employed women earned less than their employee counterparts. Counting income from all sources, part-time self-employed men appeared better off still, but around a third of their total income came from pensions and retirement income, reflecting that around 60% of the group were aged 55 and above.

**Wealth.** The self-employed tended to hold more property wealth than employees — among those aged 35 to 54, 27.0% of the self-employed had more than £250,000 in net property wealth against 17.6% of employees — but far less private pension wealth: 45.1% of the self-employed aged 35 to 54 had none, against 16.4% of employees. Gross financial wealth was broadly similar for the 35 to 54 age group, while among those aged 55 and above a higher share of the self-employed were in the top band. The ONS concludes that the two groups hold quite different mixes of assets.
