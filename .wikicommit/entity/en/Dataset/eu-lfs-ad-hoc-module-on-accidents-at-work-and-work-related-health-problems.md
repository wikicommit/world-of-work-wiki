The EU-LFS ad hoc module on accidents at work and work-related health problems is a set of additional questions added to the [[Dataset/eu-labour-force-survey]] (EU-LFS) in 2007, 2013 and 2020. It collects people's own reports of accidents at work, of [[DefinedTerm/work-related-health-problem]]s — illnesses and physical or mental health problems they consider caused or made worse by their job — and of their exposure at work to risk factors for their physical health and mental well-being. Because both the health problems and their link to work are self-perceived, the module captures cases whether or not they were reported to or recognised by the authorities, and complements administrative sources such as the [[Dataset/european-statistics-on-accidents-at-work]].

## Details

**Coverage.** The 2020 module was conducted in all EU Member States and in Iceland, Norway and Switzerland; for Cyprus it covers only the areas controlled by the Government of the Republic of Cyprus. Eurostat's published results concern people aged 15 to 64. The questions on work-related health problems were put to people who were in work at the time of the interview or had worked at any time during the previous year, while the questions on exposure to risk factors refer to the employed population.

**What is measured.** Respondents report health problems they suffered during the 12 months before the survey; a problem that began earlier is included if it persisted into that period, and injuries from accidents at work are excluded. Where a person has more than one problem, only the most serious one is recorded, by type (for example bone, joint or muscle problems; stress, depression or anxiety; headaches, eye-strain or migraine; cardiovascular disorders). The module also records whether the problem limited the person's daily activities, work and private alike, and how long they were absent from work because of it. Exposure is measured against fixed lists of risks. For mental well-being these are severe time pressure or work overload, violence or its threat, harassment or bullying, poor communication or cooperation, dealing with difficult customers, patients or pupils, job insecurity, and lack of autonomy. For physical health they are tiring or painful positions, repetitive hand or arm movements, heavy loads, noise, strong vibration, chemicals, dust, fumes, smoke or gases, strong visual concentration, slips, trips and falls, machines or hand tools, and vehicles. Results are broken down by sex, age, country, economic activity (NACE Rev. 2) and occupation (ISCO-08) of the job that caused or worsened the problem.

**Limits.** Eurostat warns that differences between countries may reflect not only working conditions and economic structure but also differences in awareness, treatment, compensation, reporting and prevention of work-related health problems, and cultural differences in how people perceive and disclose their health. Poland's 2020 results are not fully comparable because of a different national implementation of the questionnaire, and the German data for 2020 are preliminary, the LFS there having been integrated into a redesigned microcensus and affected by low response rates during the COVID-19 crisis. Comparisons of 2020 with earlier rounds should also allow for the pandemic's effects on health, on ways of working such as teleworking, and on economic activity.

**Results.** In the 2020 round, 10.3 % of people aged 15-64 who had worked reported a work-related health problem in the previous 12 months, compared with 8.8 % in 2013 and 14.6 % in 2007. In the same year 62.3 % of the employed reported exposure to risk factors for their physical health and 44.6 % to risk factors for their mental well-being; time pressure or work overload was the most frequently named mental well-being risk (19.5 % of the employed) and tiring or painful positions the most frequently named physical one (13.2 %). Regulation (EU) 2019/1700 provides for a module on accidents at work and work-related health problems every eight years from reference year 2028.
