The Time Use Survey is Statistics Canada's survey of how Canadians spend their time. It is part of the General Social Statistics Program and is a cross-sectional survey whose target population is non-institutionalized persons aged 15 and older living in the 10 provinces. It began as a theme of the General Social Survey (GSS), a program Statistics Canada established in 1985 as a series of independent, annual, cross-sectional surveys each covering one theme in depth; the first GSS on Time Use was conducted in 1986, and the theme has been revisited every 5 to 7 years since. The 2022 survey, with data collected from July 2022 to July 2023, was the seventh since the survey began in 1986, and the first since the COVID-19 pandemic began to collect data on how people spend their daily lives.

## Details

The survey uses a 24-hour time diary. So that data are collected equally across the days of the week, each respondent is assigned a reference day, the diary day, which is usually the previous day. Respondents report what they were doing, how long each activity lasted, where they were, whether they did another activity at the same time, and who else was present. Combined with questions about the respondent's job, the diary lets analysts compare groups on the same day: Statistics Canada used the 2022 data to study how [[DefinedTerm/working-from-home]] relates to commuting, paid and [[DefinedTerm/unpaid-work]], sleep, leisure and well-being, published as [[Report/time-use-and-teleworkers-2022-time-use-survey]].

According to [[Report/time-use-total-work-burden-unpaid-work-and-leisure]], the retrospective diary records day-to-day activities lasting at least 10 minutes, with the location of each and, for non-personal activities, who was with the respondent. The 2010 cycle was the first to ask about participation in, and the duration of, activities performed at the same time as the primary activity (see [[DefinedTerm/simultaneous-activity]]); the 2015 cycle kept the participation questions but not the duration questions. The 2015 cycle also used a "light" diary that distinguished far fewer child-care tasks — four, against nearly 20 in 2010. Its response rate fell to 38%, from 55% in 2010 and 8 to 15 percentage points below other cycles of the previous decade. Statistics Canada adjusted the survey weights for non-response and validated key estimates against other data sources, but estimates for small population groups carry higher sampling error and risk of non-response bias, so the 2015 data do not support analysis of gendered time use among Aboriginal peoples, visible minorities, immigrants or people with disabilities. Because a daily diary captures intermittent care poorly, analyses of caregiving to adults have drawn on the [[Dataset/general-social-survey-on-caregiving-and-care-receiving]] alongside it.
