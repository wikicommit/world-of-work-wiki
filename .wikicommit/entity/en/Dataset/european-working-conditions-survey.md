The European Working Conditions Survey (EWCS) is the survey through which Eurofound has monitored the development of [[DefinedTerm/job-quality]] since its first round in 1990. Each edition asks workers about their working conditions, and the results are organised around the seven dimensions of Eurofound's job quality framework — physical environment, social environment, working time quality, work intensity, skills and discretion, prospects and earnings — each measured by its own index. Eurofound presents the insights, including trends over time, as serving to shape EU policies such as the Competitiveness Compass and the Quality Jobs Roadmap.

## Details

The EWCS is a repeated cross-sectional survey of employees and self-employed workers, conducted every five years. In each wave a random sample of workers is interviewed face to face; according to the EU-OSHA report [[Report/work-related-msds-prevalence-costs-and-demographics-in-the-eu]], the number of completed interviews grew from 12,800 in 1991 to more than 40,000 in each of the 2005, 2010 and 2015 waves.

The 2024 edition included 35 countries. Several questions have long time series: exposure to high temperatures has been captured since 1995, and many of the indices and indicators in the 2024 first findings are compared with editions going back to 2005 or 2010. Besides employees, the survey covers self-employed people — for example, training they paid for themselves is recorded alongside training paid for or provided by employers and on-the-job training.

Questions cover exposure to physical risks and demands, adverse social behaviour and social support, working hours and control over them, the pace and emotional demands of work, skills use, training and participation in the organisation, career prospects and job security, and earnings. The survey does not ask who adverse social behaviour came from (co-workers, supervisors or third parties), but asks how often the main job involves direct interaction with people who are not employees, such as customers, passengers, pupils or patients.

The survey also asks about health problems in the past 12 months. The 2015 wave asks whether respondents had backache, muscular pains in the shoulders, neck and/or upper limbs, or muscular pains in the lower limbs — generic questions covering severe and less severe problems whatever their cause. The first four waves asked only about work-related musculoskeletal problems, and from the fifth wave (2010) the questions were changed to cover them in general, so prevalence figures from before and after the change cannot be compared directly. With its many questions on physical, organisational and psychosocial risk factors, the 2015 wave was the main data source for EU-OSHA's analysis of [[DefinedTerm/work-related-musculoskeletal-disorders]], which also used it to measure [[DefinedTerm/presenteeism]] and to build scales of [[DefinedTerm/employee-voice]] and mental well-being.

Earnings are measured mainly through a question on net monthly earnings from the main paid job, with earnings bands offered to respondents who cannot give an exact figure. Around 17 % of respondents to the 2024 survey refused to provide this information, which Eurofound notes may compromise the robustness of the indicator; at the time of the first findings, a standardised earnings index was still being calculated, so the earnings findings focused on the predictability of earnings and [[DefinedTerm/effort-reward-imbalance]].

**Selected 2024 first findings.** Eurofound reports that working time quality has improved for both men and women over two decades, with the share of EU-27 workers working 48 hours or more a week falling from 20 % in 2005 to 11 % in 2024; that the quality of the physical environment has improved since 2010, though episodic exposure to high temperatures has increased; that the social environment has been stable since 2015 while the gender gap widened; that the work intensity index has deteriorated for women but improved for men; that skills use and training have improved while workers' ability to influence collective work processes has decreased; and that career prospects have improved by 15 percentage points since 2010 for both men and women.
