"Auswirkungen flexibler Arbeitszeiten auf die Gesundheit von Beschäftigten" ("Effects of flexible working hours on the health of employees") is IAB Discussion Paper 1/2019, published on 8 January 2019 by the Institut für Arbeitsmarkt- und Berufsforschung (IAB), the research institute of the German Federal Employment Agency. Its authors are Gerhard Krug, Kirsten Kemna and Katja Hartosch. The paper starts from a policy dispute: advocates of [[DefinedTerm/flexible-working-hours]] cite positive effects on health, while opponents expect negative ones, and the authors note that earlier studies mostly relied on small samples, specific populations and cross-sectional data.

The study uses the waves 2003, 2005, 2007, 2009 and 2011 of the [[Dataset/socio-economic-panel]] — the only waves that had asked about flexible working-time arrangements at the time of writing — and covers dependent employees aged 20 to 65, excluding the self-employed. It estimates fixed-effects panel regressions, comparing fixed working hours with three forms of flexibility: flexible hours set by the employer, flextime with a working-time account and partial self-determination, and fully self-determined working time with no formal arrangement. Health is measured by self-rated health on a five-point scale, the number of working days with reported sick leave, and whether a person has serious worries about their health. The analyses are run for all employees and separately for those who did not change employer, and are checked with fixed-effects individual-slopes (FEIS) regressions.

## Key Points

- Employer-controlled flexible working hours were associated with slightly worse self-rated health (a coefficient of about −0.03 on the five-point scale), in line with the authors' first hypothesis.
- Partial self-determined flexibility (flextime) was also associated with significantly lower self-rated health, of a similar size to employer-controlled flexibility; the coefficient for fully self-determined working time was negative but not statistically significant.
- No form of flexibility had a statistically significant effect on the number of sick-leave days or on worries about one's health in the pooled models; the authors suggest that flexibility may let employees arrange absences without formal sick notes.
- FEIS regressions gave similar estimates, which the authors read as indicating that declining health is probably not the main reason people move into flexible working hours.
- Effects differed by gender: men's self-rated health was more affected by partial self-determined flexibility, women's by employer-controlled flexibility, and fully self-determined flexibility was associated with significantly fewer sick-leave days among women (more than two days on average) but hardly at all among men.
- Among men, partial flexibility strengthened the negative association between age and self-rated health; among women who did not change employer, fully and partially self-determined flexibility reduced the increase in health worries with age.
- Overall, the authors conclude that the results speak most clearly against positive health effects of flexible working hours, and point to negative effects of rather small magnitude, mainly for employer-controlled flexibility and flextime.

## Notes

The authors note that the analysis captures only rather short-term effects, so positive and especially negative consequences that arise only after longer periods in flexible working-time arrangements cannot be ruled out. The findings rest on one German household panel.
