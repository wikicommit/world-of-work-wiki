"Short Sleep Duration by Occupation Group — 29 States, 2013–2014" is an article published on 3 March 2017 in the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's *Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report* (MMWR, volume 66, issue 8, pages 207–213), by two authors from CDC's National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health and its National Center for Chronic Disease Prevention and Health Promotion. Its aim was to give updated and more detailed information about which occupation groups have the highest prevalence of [[DefinedTerm/short-sleep-duration]] — fewer than 7 hours of sleep per day.

The analysis used data from currently employed adults surveyed in 2013 and 2014 for the [[Dataset/behavioral-risk-factor-surveillance-system]] in the 29 states that administered its optional industry and occupation module. Respondents' free-text descriptions of their work were coded to 574 Census occupation codes and grouped into 93 detailed and 22 major occupation groups, and prevalence was adjusted for age group, sex, race/ethnicity, marital status and education using logistic regression. The final sample was 179,621 respondents. The authors describe it as the first study to evaluate short sleep duration by 93 detailed occupation groups and at a multistate level.

## Key Points

- Overall, 36.5% of currently employed adults in the 29 states reported short sleep duration.
- Among the 22 major occupation groups, prevalence was highest in Production (42.9%), Healthcare Support (40.1%), Healthcare Practitioners and Technical (40.0%), Food Preparation and Serving-Related (39.8%) and Protective Service (39.2%), and lowest in Education, Training, and Library and in Farming, Fishing, and Forestry (both 31.3%).
- Among the 93 detailed groups, prevalence was highest among communications equipment operators (58.2%), other transportation workers (54.0%) and rail transportation workers (52.7%), and lowest among air transportation workers (21.4%) and religious workers (22.4%).
- Within major groups, the highest detailed-group prevalences were among firefighting and prevention workers (45.8%) in Protective Service and among nursing, psychiatric and home health aides (43.3%) in Healthcare Support.
- The share of employed adults working in any of the five major groups with the highest prevalence ranged from 17.6% in Wyoming to 26.8% in Mississippi.
- Prevalence was lower among workers aged 55–64 (33.8%) and 65 and over (29.2%) than in younger age groups, and highest among men, non-Hispanic black workers, those with some college education, and those who were divorced, widowed, separated or never married.

## Notes

The authors note that the five major groups with the highest prevalence also have some of the highest rates of alternative [[DefinedTerm/shift-work]], and that shift work disturbs the circadian rhythm. They suggest that the Federal Aviation Administration's 2011 overhaul of commercial airline pilot scheduling might account for the low prevalence among air transportation workers, while the hours limits for railroad employees mandated by the Railroad Safety Improvement Act of 2008 did not require compliance until 2018. They conclude that shift work and existing occupational regulations are likely important factors and that work-related factors such as schedules, stress and physical demands should be evaluated to guide prevention.

They list four limitations: the data are cross-sectional, so temporal relationships cannot be determined; sleep was self-reported, and in regulated occupations such as transportation there may be a bias toward reporting sufficient sleep; the 29 states may not represent the national employed population; and occupation may occasionally have been misclassified.
