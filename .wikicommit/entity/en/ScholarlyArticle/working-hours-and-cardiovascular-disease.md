"Working hours and cardiovascular disease" is an editorial published online on 18 March 2024 in the *Scandinavian Journal of Work, Environment & Health* (volume 50, issue 3, pages 129–133). Prompted by a paper in the same issue on night shift work and cardiovascular disease in Germany — [[ScholarlyArticle/night-shift-work-and-cardiovascular-diseases-gutenberg-health-study]] — it reflects briefly on research into working hours and cardiovascular health.

The editorial opens by placing working hours — their number and their arrangement, such as shift work, night work and quick returns — among the classic topics of work environment and health research, and the struggle for working time reduction and the eight-hour day among the oldest fights of the labour movement. It notes that the regulation and reduction of working time was one of the main aims of the International Labour Organization when it was established in 1919, and that although working hours have been greatly reduced in many high-income countries, the subject remains topical, as the debate about a [[DefinedTerm/four-day-week]] and health concerns about hours above 48 a week in South-East and East Asian countries such as Japan, South Korea and Taiwan show.

## Key Points

- Several large-scale studies and meta-analyses published over the past ten years have examined [[DefinedTerm/long-working-hours]] and cardiovascular outcomes. Systematic reviews for the WHO/ILO joint estimates project, which defined long working hours as 55 hours or more a week, found a small increased risk of ischaemic heart disease (pooled risk ratio 1.17) and a larger increased risk of stroke (1.35), and WHO and the ILO estimated that 745,194 deaths globally were attributable to long working hours in 2016, with the largest burden in South-East Asia.
- The editorial notes that this estimate rests on assumptions, including that the epidemiological estimates reflect a causal association and that data on the worldwide prevalence of long working hours are valid. Other analyses it reviews suggest that the harmful effect on ischaemic heart disease may be limited to workers of low socioeconomic position, and point to the need to distinguish ischaemic from haemorrhagic stroke, with long working hours associated with haemorrhagic stroke in Danish register data and in a French cohort.
- Because socioeconomic position is linked to the kind of work done, its possible role as an effect modifier could mean that other, unmeasured working conditions also modify the association — what happens during working hours may matter as well as their length.
- On [[DefinedTerm/shift-work]], a systematic review and meta-analysis it cites found an increased risk of cardiovascular disease among shift workers, with each additional five years of shift work associated with a 7% increased risk. On night shift work, studies of healthcare workers in Stockholm linked frequent and permanent night shifts with higher risks of cerebrovascular and ischaemic heart disease, while a Danish cohort of healthcare workers gave less clear results, with an increased risk of ischaemic heart disease among male but not female workers doing night work.
- Of the German study in the same issue, the editorial reports hazard ratios of 1.19, 1.32 and 1.14 for low, middle and high night shift work in the most-adjusted model; it identifies the comprehensive clinical examination of participants as a strength and the very low number of cases as a limitation, so that the wide confidence intervals all include unity and do not allow firm conclusions, though the estimates can be included in meta-analyses.
- Challenges for future research include making better use of electronic working time registration systems to measure exposure more precisely, clarifying whether health behaviours such as diet, lack of leisure-time physical activity or smoking are confounders or mediators of the association between working time and cardiovascular disease, and examining how working time arrangements relate to the content of the work done during them.

## Notes

The editorial's author declares involvement in both the IPD-Work Consortium and the WHO/ILO Joint Estimates of the Work-related Burden of Disease and Injury project, two of the bodies of research it discusses.
