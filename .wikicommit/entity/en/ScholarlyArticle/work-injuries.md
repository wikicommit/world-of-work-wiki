*Work injuries* is an article in Statistics Canada's *Health Reports* (Vol. 18, No. 3) that examines acute, non-fatal occupational injuries in Canada. It starts from the burden of injury at work — in 2002 to 2004, acute injuries on the job resulted in an average of 465 deaths a year and close to 300,000 compensated time-loss claims. It states that a substantial share of such injuries can be prevented, and that a better understanding of the circumstances associated with them should contribute to more effective preventive strategies. The article notes that much of the research on work-related injury in Canada has focused on specific occupational categories, such as farmers, and usually on specific geographic regions, and that most injury statistics come from compensation agencies, which may not cover the self-employed and some professionals, collect no socio-economic or health risk-factor data, and record only compensated injuries, although fewer than half of injured workers file a claim.

The article instead uses the 2003 [[Dataset/canadian-community-health-survey]] (cycle 2.1), which it describes as large, population-based, independent of workplace reporting and covering a broad range of social, personal and work-related factors. It estimates the number of employed Canadians aged 18 to 75 who sustained at least one non-fatal, activity-limiting injury on the job in 2003, compares injury occurrence across occupational categories, and examines relationships with work-related and personal factors, first as unadjusted estimates and then in multivariate models fitted separately for men and women. Its framework, drawn from earlier models, treats injury risk as arising from tangible conditions of the job, the organization of work (such as overtime or shifts) and individual characteristics. Only acute injuries are counted; [[DefinedTerm/repetitive-strain-injury]] is not included.

## Key Points

- An estimated 630,000 Canadians experienced at least one activity-limiting occupational injury in 2003 — 5% of employed men and 2% of employed women — and 28% of employed people aged 18 to 75 who reported an activity-limiting injury sustained their most serious injury at work. The article says these figures underestimate the actual frequency of work injury.
- Injury was more common in blue-collar jobs: 9% of workers in trades, transport and equipment operation were injured, more than four times the 2% rate in business, finance or administration and in social science, education, government service or religion. Within occupations, 13% of machinists, metal formers, shapers and erectors and 7% of chefs and cooks were injured.
- Overexertion or strenuous movement and falls caused 49% of occupational injuries; the hand (28%) and lower back (16%) were the body parts most often injured, and sprains or strains the most frequent type of injury.
- 66% of people injured at work sought treatment, but 20% of work injuries were classed as serious, a smaller share than for injuries in other activities, so the findings do not support the suggestion that occupational injuries are relatively more serious than those sustained elsewhere.
- In fully controlled models, men in trades, transport or equipment operation, primary industries and processing, manufacturing or utilities had over twice the odds of injury of white-collar men; heavy physical exertion raised men's odds by 70%. Compared with men working under 35 hours a week, men working 45 to 79 hours had 40% higher odds of injury, and those working 80 hours or more nearly twice the odds ([[DefinedTerm/long-working-hours]]); no significant relationship with hours emerged for women.
- In the fully controlled models, shift work and daily smoking were associated with higher odds of injury for both sexes, and self-employment was protective for women; in the unadjusted results, self-employed men and women were both less likely to be injured. Women's odds of injury rose with perceived job stress — those in jobs they described as extremely stressful had nearly three times the odds of women in jobs they did not find stressful, even after accounting for personal life stress — while no such pattern emerged for men. Obese women had nearly twice the odds of injury of women of normal weight.

## Notes

The article cautions that the association between work stress and injury in women rests on cross-sectional data and may reflect reverse causation, since sustaining an injury may itself increase stress. It also notes that the estimates refer only to each respondent's most serious injury and are affected by memory decay. In its concluding remarks it suggests that, besides programs to promote smoking cessation, healthy weight and stress reduction, workplace-based safety programs could be emphasized for occupations at higher risk, and that factors modifiable by employers — workplace hazards, equipment design, work schedules and workload distribution — matter to injury risk ([[DefinedTerm/shift-work]], [[DefinedTerm/work-related-stress]]).
