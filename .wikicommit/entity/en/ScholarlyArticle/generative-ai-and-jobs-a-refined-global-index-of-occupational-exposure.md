This International Labour Organization working paper, written by researchers from the ILO Research Department and Poland's NASK National Research Institute, updates the ILO's 2023 global estimates of [[DefinedTerm/occupational-exposure-to-generative-ai]]. It takes a task-based approach — treating jobs as "bundles of tasks", so that automating a task may or may not mean automating a job — and refines the earlier method, which used the task descriptions of the International Standard Classification of Occupations (ISCO-08) and GPT-4 to score tasks. The paper describes itself as the first in a series of ILO–NASK publications on assessing the employment effects of AI.

The method starts from Poland's national 6-digit occupational classification, which contains 29,753 tasks for 2,541 occupations — roughly ten times as many tasks as ISCO-08 — and is aligned with ISCO-08 at the 4-digit level. A survey of 1,640 people employed in Poland scored the automation potential of a representative sample of 2,861 tasks from their own broad occupational group (52,558 valid data points after exclusions). Experts from the ILO, NASK and the Polish Ministry of Family, Labour and Social Policy then reviewed 608 tasks; two AI models arbitrated between survey and expert scores; and the resulting adjusted scores were used as a knowledge repository for an AI assistant that predicts scores for all ISCO-08 and Polish tasks. Predictions based on ISCO-08 and on Polish tasks correlated at 0.92 at the 4-digit level.

## Key Points

- Occupations are classified by the mean and standard deviation of their task scores into four exposure gradients, plus "minimal exposure" and "not exposed" categories, replacing the 2023 framework of automation, augmentation and "the big unknown"; 112 occupations are classed as exposed.
- Clerical occupations remain the most exposed, although their mean scores fell compared with 2023, which the authors attribute to a reassessment of tasks such as taking meeting notes or scheduling appointments that were judged too optimistically in 2023; most of the 13 occupations in gradient 4 are clerical roles such as data entry clerks and typists.
- Some highly digitised occupations — such as web and multimedia developers, financial analysts, and database, statistical and software-related occupations — show higher exposure than in 2023, which the authors link to the multimodal and emerging agentic capabilities of large language models.
- About a quarter of global employment (24%) is in one of the four exposure gradients and 3.3% is in gradient 4; exposure rises with country income, from 11% of employment in low-income countries to 34% in high-income countries.
- Women's employment is more exposed than men's: globally 4.7% of female employment versus 2.4% of male employment is in gradient 4, and in high-income countries 9.6% versus 3.5%, up from 7.8% and 2.9% in the 2023 estimates; in Europe and Central Asia, 39% of female employment is in an exposure gradient compared with 26% of male employment.
- The estimates measure exposure, not actual employment effects: they are an upper threshold assuming full implementation, ignore barriers such as infrastructure, skills, cost and social acceptance, and do not account for new jobs; because nearly all occupations include tasks requiring human input, the authors see transformation of jobs as the most likely impact.

## Notes

The authors note several limitations: the survey sample is not representative of the employed population (scoring varied with respondents' familiarity with GenAI and with the task), all tasks within an occupation are weighted equally because neither classification records task frequency or importance, and any task-scoring system carries a degree of subjectivity. Task-level scores are published openly. The paper concludes that the transition should be managed through social dialogue and a human-centred approach to adoption, both to limit technological unemployment and to ensure the technology supports those who remain employed.
