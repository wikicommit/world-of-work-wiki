"The importance of extended working hours for work-related injuries" is an editorial published online on 11 August 2021 in the *Scandinavian Journal of Work, Environment & Health* (volume 47, issue 6, pages 411–414). It argues that [[DefinedTerm/long-working-hours]] are a promising candidate risk factor for work-related injuries, because they leave less time outside work for sleep and restitution, and the resulting sleepiness can impair cognitive performance — a plausible mechanism linking long hours to accidents, with possible consequences for the safety of others such as patients and road users.

The editorial introduces a systematic review and meta-analysis by Matre and colleagues in the same issue, which looked at both daily and weekly working hours and found that long daily (more than 12 hours) and long weekly (more than 55 hours) working hours are associated with an increased risk of safety incidents. It then discusses what the field needs to move forward.

## Key Points

- Working hours are comparatively easy to measure: an hour can be measured objectively and with high precision, and duration can be assessed with relatively high confidence from a single survey question, which makes the exposure attractive for large population cohorts with a standardized measure across jobs, gender, age and socioeconomic groups.
- Extended hours are intertwined with other working-hour characteristics — short time between shifts (quick returns) and evening or night work — so risks need to be studied for specific combinations of working hours.
- Long work days do not necessarily mean long working weeks: the same weekly hours can be worked in fewer, longer days ([[DefinedTerm/compressed-work-week]]).
- Causality is uncertain, particularly for long-term outcomes: long weekly hours, especially overtime, may reflect high workload and [[DefinedTerm/job-strain]], and long hours also mean longer exposure to all other workplace risk factors such as chemical and physical exposures.
- What counts as normal working hours varies by region, and the reasons for long hours may differ — in countries with high social security and a minimum wage, long hours are likely to be more of a voluntary choice than where workers need them for financial reasons — which may modify the relationship between long hours and health.
- Payroll data are described as the gold standard for measuring working hours, though less valid in jobs where unpaid extra hours are common (for example physicians and other academic staff) or where hours in secondary jobs are not included. Day-to-day payroll registrations give better information on timing, less misclassification, and allow designs such as the case–crossover design, which removes confounding by time-invariant variables.

## Notes

The editorial places the review alongside other recent systematic reviews by the World Health Organization, the International Labour Organization and the IPD-Work consortium, which examined long working hours mainly in relation to long-term health outcomes such as cardiovascular disease, cancer, type 2 diabetes, alcohol consumption and depression. It notes that working hours have long been regulated — the first piece of factory legislation in the United Kingdom already limited apprentices' hours — and that in Europe the [[Legislation/working-time-directive]] (2003/88/EC) regulates their duration today.
