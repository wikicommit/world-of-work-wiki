"Measuring workplace psychosocial factors in the federal government" is an article in Statistics Canada's journal *Health Reports*, released on 19 January 2022 by five researchers from Statistics Canada and Concordia University. It asks whether the [[Dataset/public-service-employee-survey]] (PSES) — a survey the federal government runs to improve people management, not to measure mental health — can be used to assess the 13 workplace psychosocial factors that the [[DefinedTerm/national-standard-for-psychological-health-and-safety-in-the-workplace]] says employers should measure and monitor. A secondary aim was to explore, in a preliminary way, which of those factors predict [[DefinedTerm/job-satisfaction]].

The study took the national standard as its starting point. The one existing instrument aligned with the standard — a 141-item survey built for the Department of National Defence and the Canadian Armed Forces — was judged too long and complex to administer across the public service. The authors therefore took the 89 items common to the 2017 and 2019 PSES, had a panel of five experts (four research psychologists and one practitioner) map them onto the standard's factors plus diversity and inclusion, and refined the mapping with exploratory factor analysis and exploratory structural equation modelling. The resulting 43-item subset is named the Public Service Psychosocial Questionnaire (PSPQ). Its fit was tested on the 2017 sample (174,544 respondents) and then on the 2019 sample (182,306 respondents) through a sequence of measurement-invariance tests. The study's framing for the job-satisfaction analysis is the [[DefinedTerm/job-demands-resources-model]], in which all of the standard's factors count as job resources.

## Key Points

- The 43-item PSPQ captured 10 of the standard's 13 psychosocial factors: clear leadership and expectations, organizational culture, civility and respect, growth and development, recognition and reward, workload management, involvement and influence, balance, psychological protection, and protection of physical safety.
- Three factors could not be measured with PSES items: the experts mapped no item to psychological competencies and requirements and only one to engagement, and the expected psychological support factor did not emerge in the factor analysis.
- Diversity and inclusion, added because of their importance to the federal government, emerged as two separate but correlated factors; inclusion was measured through items about using one's chosen language for written materials, meetings and communication with one's supervisor.
- The expert mapping showed only moderate inter-rater agreement (Fleiss's kappa 0.450 across the 89 items; a majority agreed on 78% of them), which the authors flag as a content-validity concern.
- The measurement model was fully invariant across the 2017 and 2019 samples — factor loadings, thresholds, uniquenesses, latent variances, covariances and means — which the authors take as preliminary evidence that it generalizes across the public service.
- The factors' composite reliability averaged 0.79 (range 0.45–0.96); the two-item diversity factor was weakest at 0.45. Factor correlations were moderate, averaging 0.38.
- Every factor was significantly related to the single job-satisfaction item ("Overall, I like my job"), but only recognition and reward (standardized coefficient 0.400) and involvement and influence (0.229) showed moderate associations; the relations were the same in both samples.
- The authors read recognition and reward as encompassing leader reward behaviours, and involvement and influence as participative decision making, innovation and initiative — candidate "levers" for job satisfaction, pending replication with a fuller measure.

## Notes

The authors list several limitations. Mapping pre-existing survey items onto the factors reverses the usual approach of selecting items from the literature to fit predefined constructs, and the iterative item pruning risks over-fitting the model to the data, although replication across two samples mitigates this. All data were self-reported and both samples are cross-sectional, so causal conclusions about the factors and job satisfaction cannot be drawn; the authors suggest linking survey responses to human-resources records such as absenteeism and turnover, using longitudinal designs, developing items for the three missing factors and a third diversity item, and testing correlations with established measures and with outcomes such as burnout and psychological distress.

They also question the standard itself. Citing an earlier qualitative study of employers that found the 13 factors and the standard's documentation complex to work with, they note that the moderate correlations among the PSPQ factors suggest some redundancy, and propose that future work concentrate on a core set of factors that consistently predict important outcomes. For practice, they argue that knowing which factors act as "levers" for outcomes such as job satisfaction can inform the design of interventions, which should use psychometrically sound measures, adequate experimental designs and sufficiently large samples. They cite Michie and Williams's call for interventions based on human-resources management practices — primary prevention that reinforces workplace resilience factors, rather than secondary prevention such as training managers to identify employees in distress.

The study builds on earlier work that had distinguished 9 of the 13 factors using the 2017 PSES alone. Related WHO, EU and New Zealand framings of the same subject are summarized under [[DefinedTerm/psychosocial-risks]].
