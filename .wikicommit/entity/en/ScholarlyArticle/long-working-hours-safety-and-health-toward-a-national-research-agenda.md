"Long Working Hours, Safety, and Health: Toward a National Research Agenda" is a report published in the *American Journal of Industrial Medicine* (volume 49, pages 930–942) in 2006. It was written by the National Occupational Research Agenda (NORA) Long Work Hours Team, a group of experts from industry, labour and government recruited by the US National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH). Its stated aim is to motivate and guide future research by proposing a framework for studying [[DefinedTerm/long-working-hours]] and discussing research gaps, rather than to offer a comprehensive review of earlier studies.

The team based the agenda on research reports and literature reviews and on input from a conference on long work hours organized with the University of Maryland in spring 2004, attended by about 175 researchers and representatives of labour and industry; an analysis of the conference discussion transcripts served as an initial basis for the agenda.

## Key Points

- The report proposes a conceptual framework in which economic, cultural, institutional, legal and individual factors combine to produce work schedules with long hours; these schedules reduce the time available for sleep, recovery from work, family and other non-work activities and lengthen exposure to workplace hazards and demands, with impacts on the worker, the family, the employer and the community.
- Whether long hours lead to negative outcomes may depend on moderating factors — characteristics of the worker (such as age, gender, health status, sleep needs, family support and financial resources) and of the job and organizational context (job demands, hazardous exposures, work rewards, support and control).
- Most studies describe work schedules only by total hours worked; together with differing definitions of long work hours across studies, this has made results hard to compare. The report lists schedule characteristics for future studies to record, including shift length, recovery time between shifts, time of day, shift rotation, predictability and worker control over the schedule, commuting and electronic connection to work outside working hours.
- It names as research priorities: demanding schedules such as mandatory [[DefinedTerm/overtime]] and [[DefinedTerm/shift-work]]; a wider range of outcomes, including chronic disease and impacts on employers, families and communities; occupations involving public safety (health care, law enforcement, transportation, utilities); vulnerable groups such as pregnant women, older workers, workers with chronic illnesses and socio-economically disadvantaged workers; more systematic tracking of work schedules in the United States; and the development and testing of interventions.
- It notes that few studies have developed or evaluated interventions; candidate employer measures include limits on overtime, protected recovery days, staffing matched to workload peaks, reduced-hours options, strategic rest breaks and naps, education programmes and fatigue-detection technologies, while government and industry measures to evaluate include limits on mandatory overtime and fatigue management requirements for high-risk occupations.
- On regulation, the report notes that while some European countries have mandatory vacation time and a ceiling on work hours, no laws limit work hours for the vast majority of US workers, with federal limits in transportation as an exception.

## Notes

The authors present the agenda as not definitive and say the findings and conclusions are their own and do not necessarily represent the views of NIOSH. They also note that long hours may have positive effects for employees and employers, although the framework focuses on potential negative effects.
