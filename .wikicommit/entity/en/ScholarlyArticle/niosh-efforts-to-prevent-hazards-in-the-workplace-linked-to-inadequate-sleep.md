This short article, published in the Sleep Research Society Bulletin in autumn 2013 and catalogued by CDC as guidance from the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH), outlines why demanding work hours are an occupational hazard and what NIOSH does about them. It is listed as not peer reviewed.

## Key Points

- Almost 15 million Americans, or 15% of full-time workers, have shift schedules outside the daytime hours of 7:00 a.m. to 6:00 p.m.; working at night or at irregular hours runs against human physiology, which is set to sleep at night and be active during the day.
- [[DefinedTerm/shift-work]] is often dictated by society's need for round-the-clock services in public safety, healthcare, utilities, food services, manufacturing and transportation.
- Citing the National Health Interview Survey (NHIS), it reports that 19% of Americans worked 48 hours or more per week in 2010, and that [[DefinedTerm/long-working-hours]] often leave too little time for sleep.
- NHIS data for 2004–2007 show almost 30% of full-time US workers reporting [[DefinedTerm/short-sleep-duration]] (6 hours or less per day), up from 24% in 1985 and 1990; rates were 34% in manufacturing and 44% among those who usually worked at night, and much higher rates have been reported for night shift workers in transportation and warehousing (69.7%) and in health care and social assistance (52.3%).
- Insufficient sleep is linked to errors at work and to errors while driving on the commute that endanger workers and others; poor sleep, shift work and long hours are linked to a wide range of acute and chronic diseases.
- NIOSH describes a long-standing commitment to preventing hazards from demanding work hours through research, guidance and recommendations, and dissemination of information to workers, their families, employers and the community.
