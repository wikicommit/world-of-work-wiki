"Shift work and health" is an article by Margot Shields of Statistics Canada's Health Statistics Division, published in Statistics Canada's journal *Health Reports* (Vol. 13, No. 4, July 2002). It describes the characteristics of Canadian shift workers, compares the stress factors and health behaviours of shift and regular daytime workers, and, following the same people over four years, explores whether working shift is associated with new chronic conditions and changes in psychological distress. [[DefinedTerm/shift-work]] is defined broadly as any schedule other than a regular daytime one, and four types are compared with daytime work: evening, night, rotating and irregular shifts.

The prevalence profile uses cycle 1.1 of the [[Dataset/canadian-community-health-survey]] (2000/01), restricted to people aged 18 to 54 in the 10 provinces who worked throughout the year before their interview. Work stress, psycho-social factors, health behaviours, chronic conditions and distress are examined with the 1994/95 cross-sectional file of the [[Dataset/national-population-health-survey]], and four-year outcomes with its longitudinal file (1994/95, 1996/97 and 1998/99), using multiple logistic regression for chronic conditions and linear regression for distress, with separate models for men and women that control for occupation, hours, age, family and socio-economic characteristics, [[DefinedTerm/job-strain]] and other work stress, personal stress, mastery and health behaviours. Ten-year trends draw on the 1991 and 1995 [[Dataset/survey-of-work-arrangements]]. Because of small samples, night shift workers are left out of the National Population Health Survey analyses.

The article frames its analysis around three potentially interrelated pathways that researchers have proposed to link shift work and health problems: disruption of circadian rhythms, adoption or worsening of unhealthy behaviour, and stress.

## Key Points

- In 2000/01, 30% of men and 26% of women aged 18 to 54 who were employed throughout the year had non-standard schedules; about a quarter of them worked evening or night shifts, while rotating and irregular shifts each accounted for around 4 in 10.
- Shift work was more common in blue-collar and in sales and service occupations than in white-collar or clerical jobs, among men and women working fewer than 30 hours a week and men working more than 40, among weekend workers, and among younger, single or previously married, less-educated and lower-income workers; rotating shifts, however, were relatively common among men in more affluent households.
- The main reason given for non-standard hours, by both sexes, was that the job required it. "No choice" was reported by 94% of men and 91% of women on rotating shifts and was lowest for the evening shift (65% of men and 53% of women); women were more likely than men to cite caring for family.
- In 1994/95, men and women on evening or rotating shifts were more likely than daytime workers to report job strain, and job insecurity was common among both sexes on rotating or irregular schedules.
- Men on the evening shift were more likely than daytime workers to report relationship problems (if married), difficulty finding a compatible partner (if single), low mastery and daily smoking (45% versus 27%); women on an irregular shift more often reported high personal stress, and women on a rotating shift low mastery. Daily smoking among evening-shift men was the only significant difference in health behaviour.
- Once demographic, socio-economic, work stress, psycho-social and behavioural factors were taken into account, shift workers' prevalence of chronic conditions and their distress levels in 1994/95 did not differ significantly from those of daytime workers.
- Over four years, men who worked an evening, rotating or irregular shift in 1994/95 had higher odds of a new diagnosed chronic condition by 1998/99 than men with daytime schedules (adjusted odds ratios of 2.0, 1.7 and 1.7); for women, no such association was found.
- For both sexes, working the evening shift in 1994/95 was associated with an increase in psychological distress by 1996/97, but by 1998/99 their predicted distress no longer differed from that of daytime workers.
- In the 2000/01 data, men and women on evening, rotating or irregular shifts tended to report more trouble falling or staying asleep, less than six hours of sleep in 24 hours, and sleep that was not refreshing than daytime workers, showing that sleep problems are not confined to the night shift.
- Fewer than one in five people who worked an evening, rotating or irregular shift in 1994/95 kept that schedule in both 1996/97 and 1998/99 (for an irregular shift, 12% of men and 11% of women), against about 75% of daytime workers who kept daytime hours; most transitions were to daytime work or out of the labour force.
- Across the 1991 and 1995 Survey of Work Arrangements, the first three cycles of the National Population Health Survey and the 2000/01 Canadian Community Health Survey, the share of employed people reporting shift work stayed relatively stable at approximately 30%.

## Notes

The author interprets the gap between the cross-sectional and longitudinal results through the rapid turnover out of shift work: workers who have trouble adjusting tend to switch to daytime schedules after a short time, so their problems may not appear in a cross-sectional analysis, and those who stay form a more robust group — consistent with earlier studies suggesting a "healthy survivor effect" ([[DefinedTerm/shift-work-intolerance]]). Women's apparently greater ability to cope with the evening shift may, in the author's view, reflect that they are more likely than men to work such schedules to care for family, in which case the schedule may even help accommodate other priorities.

The limitations the article lists are that the length of time people had worked shift could not be controlled for; shift status was based on the main job only; small samples prevented analysis of transitions between survey cycles and forced the exclusion of night shift workers; chronic conditions had to be grouped, and four years may be too short for their full effect to emerge; diagnoses were self- or proxy-reported and not independently verified; and the change from mostly in-person interviews in 1994/95 to mostly telephone interviews in later cycles may have affected reported distress.
