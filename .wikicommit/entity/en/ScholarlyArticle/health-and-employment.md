*Health and employment* is an article by Sharanjit Uppal in the September 2009 issue of Statistics Canada's *Perspectives on Labour and Income*. It studies the relationship between health status and labour market outcomes — whether a person is employed, and how many hours a year they work — for working-age men and women aged 15 to 64. The article frames the question as one of cost: if health problems lower labour productivity, participation or hours, they impose a production loss on the economy, alongside employers' sick leave costs, Employment Insurance sickness benefits, Canada Pension Plan disability benefits and the cost of accommodating workers with limitations. It adds that the aging of the population makes the relationship more pressing.

The study uses the 2002 [[Dataset/canadian-community-health-survey]]: Mental Health and Well-being (Cycle 1.2), chosen because, as a health survey with a strong focus on mental health, it identifies health conditions thoroughly while also capturing employment and hours. The author notes that previous studies dealt primarily with physical health, mostly concerned the U.S. population and older working-age men, and in Canada focused mostly on disability. Four measures of health status are used — self-reported physical and mental health, the presence of a diagnosed long-term physical or mental health condition, the number of such conditions, and self-reported change in health over the past year — together with several estimation techniques to check robustness and to address the possibility that health and employment affect each other.

## Key Points

- About 46% of working-age men and 54% of women had at least one chronic physical condition, and 15% of men and 18% of women reported at least one mental health problem; back problems were the most common physical problem for both sexes (20%).
- 83% of men and 72% of women in good to excellent physical health were employed, against 62% and 54% of those in fair or poor physical health; for mental health the figures were 82% and 70% against 64% and 59%.
- In logistic regressions, fair or poor health lowered the odds of being employed — by 0.63 for men and 0.44 for women for physical health, and by 0.53 and 0.31 for mental health — and the conclusion held for every measure of health status, including the number of conditions and a worsening of health over the previous year.
- Propensity score matching estimated that fair or poor physical health reduced the chance of being employed by 24 percentage points for men and 19 for women, and fair or poor mental health by 21 and 14 points.
- Among the employed, the effect on annual hours was statistically significant only for women and only for mental health: women in fair or poor mental health were estimated to work 136 hours (about 3.5 weeks) less a year, and each additional mental health problem reduced hours by about 38 a year. Results for men were inconclusive.
- The author concludes that both mental and physical health problems adversely affect the probability of being employed, more strongly for men than for women, and that mental health problems are associated with women working fewer hours.

## Notes

To address the two-way causality between health and labour market outcomes — poor health can lead to adverse labour market outcomes, and adverse outcomes such as unemployment might cause poor (especially mental) health — the study uses change in health status over the previous year, which current employment status is unlikely to affect, as well as propensity score matching. A Heckman maximum likelihood model corrects the hours-worked results for sample selection, giving very similar results.

The author lists several limitations: the data are cross-sectional, so unobserved heterogeneity cannot be accounted for; there is no information on the severity of health problems, for which the number of conditions may serve as a proxy; hourly earnings are not available as a control; and annual hours worked are computed from usual weekly hours multiplied by weeks worked in the previous 12 months ([[DefinedTerm/usual-hours-of-work]]). The survey covered the civilian population aged 15 and older in private dwellings in the 10 provinces.
