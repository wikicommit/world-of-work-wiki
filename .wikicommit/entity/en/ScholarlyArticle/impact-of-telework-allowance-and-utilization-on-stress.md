"The impact of telework allowance and utilization on physiological and perceived stress among Swedish white-collar workers" is an original article published online on 28 May 2025 in *Scand J Work Environ Health* (volume 51, issue 5, pages 404–412; issue date 1 September 2025). It assesses how telework conditions ([[DefinedTerm/working-from-home]]) during the COVID-19 pandemic related to stress among 294 white-collar workers in Sweden.

The study separates two aspects of telework: the *allowance* to telework — the degree to which the employee could decide whether to telework — and the *utilization* of that allowance, both measured by self-reported questions with answers dichotomized into "high" and "low". Perceived stress was measured with the Single Item Stress Question, and physiological stress with heart rate variability (HRV) recorded continuously for three days, using two parameters: the root mean square of successive differences (RMSSD) and the standard deviation of the interbeat intervals of normal heart beats (SDNN). Multilevel linear mixed models examined the effects of telework allowance and utilization on perceived stress and on HRV during work, leisure and sleep.

## Key Points

- A high allowance to telework was associated with higher HRV, that is, lower physiological stress.
- High utilization of telework was associated with higher perceived stress and lower HRV, that is, more stress.
- After adjusting for age, sex, body mass index and objectively measured physical activity, these associations became smaller and/or non-significant, except that high allowance remained positively associated with higher RMSSD.
- The authors conclude that giving employees more autonomy in telework decisions is associated with reduced physiological stress (see [[DefinedTerm/job-autonomy]]).
- They suggest organizations can use the results to improve telework conditions (how, where and how much), while watching that white-collar workers do not use increased autonomy to work extensively and for long hours outside work.

## Notes

Allowance and utilization were self-reported and dichotomized, and the study population was limited to Swedish white-collar workers during the COVID-19 pandemic. The authors state that further verification, preferably using prospective designs, is needed to confirm the results.
