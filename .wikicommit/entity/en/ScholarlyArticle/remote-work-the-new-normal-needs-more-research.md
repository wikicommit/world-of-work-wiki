"Remote work – the new normal needs more research" is an editorial published online on 27 January 2025 in the *Scandinavian Journal of Work, Environment & Health* (volume 51, issue 2, pages 53–57). It starts from the COVID-19 pandemic, which drastically changed how people in expert positions, or doing other work that can be performed using mobile devices, do their work, and from the resulting growth in research: PubMed-indexed papers on remote work numbered 1–10 a year from 1990 to 2019, 134 in 2020 and 346–470 a year in 2021–2023. Despite this interest, the editorial argues, knowledge gaps remain.

## Key Points

- Remote work has existed for decades but goes by many names — telework, work from home ([[DefinedTerm/working-from-home]]), and [[DefinedTerm/hybrid-work]], in which people work both remotely and at their employer's premises. The editorial quotes Eurofound's definition of telework as a work arrangement in which work is performed outside a default place of work, normally the employer's premises, by means of information and communication technologies.
- Measures have mainly relied on self-reported survey items, which are prone to recall and reporting bias and may not capture days split between home and the employer's premises. The editorial calls for longitudinal studies using employers' register data on when and where people work, to examine the effects of combining remote and on-site work on health, well-being and work performance.
- Research on remote work among knowledge workers has concentrated on the years before or early in the pandemic, often with cross-sectional designs or survey data focused on psychosocial aspects, so little is known about remote work after the pandemic. During the pandemic, regulations, guidelines and logistical factors such as public transport and workspace capacity shaped whether employees could work remotely, while infrastructure such as fast internet and affordable housing with a workspace influences its possibility and attractiveness.
- Remote work challenges employee well-being, and national legislation restricting connectivity after work is rare: in Europe, only nine countries — Belgium, Croatia, France, Greece, Italy, Luxembourg, Portugal, Slovakia and Spain — have legislation providing a [[DefinedTerm/right-to-disconnect]]. The editorial argues for national and local regulation alongside longitudinal, high-quality studies using daily register data.
- Because remote work is usually done at home, a private place over whose conditions employers have no say, it poses a challenge for occupational safety and health legislation, most of which places responsibility for protecting workers on the employer. Employees set their own working conditions, from well-equipped workstations to the sofa or kitchen table, and long-term poor physical conditions may harm musculoskeletal health, eye ergonomics or cognitive functions — a particular concern for young employees and those of lower socioeconomic status.
- The editorial frames remote work as a form of employee-oriented flexibility — employees' entitlement to influence when and where they work — as opposed to company-based flexibility, in which the employer sets working times and location according to the company's needs. Its author predicts that within one to five years the discussion will be about flexibility rather than remote work, leading to legislation and regulation at local, national and international levels.
