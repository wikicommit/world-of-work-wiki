"The impact of junior doctors’ worktime arrangements on their fatigue and well-being" is an original article published online on 23 April 2010 in the *Scandinavian Journal of Work, Environment & Health* (volume 36, issue 6, November 2010, pages 458–465). Its starting point is that many doctors report working excessively demanding schedules that nonetheless comply with the [[Legislation/working-time-directive]]. The study compared groups of junior doctors working on different schedules to identify which features of schedule design most negatively affected their fatigue and well-being in recent weeks.

The data come from questionnaires completed by 336 doctors, covering their personal circumstances, work situation, work schedules, sleep, and perceptions of fatigue, work–life balance and psychological strain.

## Key Points

- Working 7 consecutive nights was associated with greater accumulated fatigue and greater work–life interference than working just 3 or 4 nights.
- Having only 1 rest day after working nights was associated with increased fatigue.
- Working a weekend on-call between 2 consecutive working weeks was associated with increased work–life interference.
- Frequent on-calls, at weekends or during the week, were associated with increased work–life interference and psychological strain.
- Inter-shift intervals of less than 10 hours were associated with shorter sleep and increased fatigue.
- The number of hours worked per week was positively associated with work–life interference and with fatigue on night shifts.
- The authors conclude that these findings identify parameters for designing schedules that limit their impact on doctors' fatigue and well-being, in addition to those specified in the Working Time Directive.

## Notes

The findings rest on a single questionnaire survey of junior doctors and describe associations between schedule features and self-reported outcomes.
