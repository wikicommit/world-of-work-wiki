"How to schedule night shift work in order to reduce health and safety risks" is a discussion paper published online on 8 September 2020 in the *Scandinavian Journal of Work, Environment & Health* (volume 46, issue 6, pages 557–569). It sets out to give scientifically based recommendations on night shift schedules — the number of consecutive shifts, the intervals between shifts and the duration of shifts — that may reduce health and safety risks.

The paper treats short-term physiological effects — circadian disruption, inadequate sleep duration and quality, and fatigue — as possible links between night shift work ([[DefinedTerm/night-work]]) and four selected health and safety risks: cancer, cardio-metabolic disease, injuries and pregnancy-related outcomes. Its recommendations come from a workshop held in early 2020 at which 15 experienced shift work researchers identified relevant scientific literature in their main research areas and discussed knowledge gaps and possible recommendations on the basis of current evidence.

## Key Points

- The consensus was that schedules which reduce circadian disruption may reduce cancer risk, particularly for breast cancer, and schedules that optimise sleep and reduce fatigue may reduce the occurrence of injuries.
- This is generally achieved with fewer consecutive night shifts, sufficient intervals between shifts and shorter night shifts.
- To reduce the risk of injuries and possibly breast cancer, the authors recommend that night shift schedules have no more than 3 consecutive night shifts, shift intervals of at least 11 hours, and shifts lasting no more than 9 hours.
- To reduce the risk of miscarriage, pregnant women should not work more than one night shift in a week.
- In special cases, such as oil rigs and other isolated workplaces where workers have better possibilities to adapt to daytime sleep, additional or other recommendations may apply.

## Notes

The authors describe their recommendations as based on limited existing literature.
