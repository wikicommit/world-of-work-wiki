"Long working hours and depressive symptoms: systematic review and meta-analysis of published studies and unpublished individual participant data" is a review published online on 8 February 2018 in the *Scandinavian Journal of Work, Environment & Health* (volume 44, issue 3, pages 239–250). It aims to quantify the relation between [[DefinedTerm/long-working-hours]] and the onset of depressive symptoms by combining published study-level data with unpublished individual-participant data.

The authors searched PubMed and Embase for published prospective cohort studies and added available cohorts with unpublished individual-participant data, identifying ten published cohort studies and individual-participant data from 18 further studies. Summary estimates were calculated with a random-effects meta-analysis. In most cohorts long working hours were defined as working 55 or more hours a week.

## Key Points

- In multivariable-adjusted meta-analyses of 189,729 participants from 35 countries (96,275 men and 93,454 women, followed up for 1 to 5 years, with 21,747 new-onset cases), the overall association between long working hours and the onset of depressive symptoms was 1.14 (95% CI 1.03–1.25), with significant heterogeneity between studies (I² = 45.1%, P = 0.004).
- The association was moderate in Asian countries (1.50, 95% CI 1.13–2.01), weaker in Europe (1.11, 95% CI 1.00–1.22), and absent in North America (0.97, 95% CI 0.70–1.34) and Australia (0.95, 95% CI 0.70–1.29).
- Differences by other characteristics were small.
- The authors conclude that this observational evidence suggests a moderate association between long working hours and the onset of depressive symptoms in Asia and a small association in Europe.
