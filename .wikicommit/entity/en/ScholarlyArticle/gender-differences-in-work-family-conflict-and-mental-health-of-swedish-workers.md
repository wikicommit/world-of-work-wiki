"Gender differences in work–family conflict and mental health of Swedish workers by childcare responsibilities: findings from the SLOSH cohort study" is an original article published online on 21 April 2025 in the *Scandinavian Journal of Work, Environment & Health* (volume 51, issue 5, pages 413–422; issue date 1 September 2025). Its starting point is that in Sweden the number of working-aged women in employment is now almost equal to that of men, which brings challenges in organizing work and non-work responsibilities around children that may affect employees' mental health.

The study draws on the Swedish Longitudinal Occupational Survey of Health (SLOSH) cohort, using three waves of data over five years (2014–2018) for 5,846 women and 4,219 men aged 20–64 at baseline. It prospectively examines gender differences in how [[DefinedTerm/work-family-conflict]] — work interfering with family (WFC) and family interfering with work (FWC) — and caring for children relate to mental health. Depressive symptoms were measured with a brief (Hopkins) symptom checklist depression scale, and linear fixed-effects analyses examined within-person changes in WFC and FWC and the associated changes in depressive symptoms, by childcare intensity (0, 1–10 and more than 10 hours a week) and sex.

## Key Points

- Within-person changes in both WFC and FWC were associated with changes in depressive symptoms in both men and women.
- For men without childcare, the estimates were 1.31 (95% CI 1.13–1.49) for WFC and 0.70 (95% CI 0.43–0.96) for FWC; for men caring for children more than 10 hours a week, 1.39 (95% CI 0.53–2.25) and 1.24 (95% CI 0.27–2.21).
- For women without childcare, the estimates were 1.57 (95% CI 1.41–1.73) for WFC and 1.04 (95% CI 0.79–1.30) for FWC; for women caring for children more than 10 hours a week, 2.04 (95% CI 1.36–2.73) and 1.57 (95% CI 0.82–2.32).
- The authors conclude that higher levels of WFC and FWC are associated with increased depressive symptoms in both men and women, and that the impact is greater for those with more intensive childcaring responsibilities.
