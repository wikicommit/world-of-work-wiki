*Measuring the Gig Economy in Canada Using Administrative Data* is a research paper by Sung-Hee Jeon, Huju Liu and Yuri Ostrovsky, released by Statistics Canada on 16 December 2019 as No. 437 of its Analytical Studies Branch Research Paper Series. It presents itself as the first Canadian study to systematically identify gig workers with administrative data and to measure their share among all Canadian workers. The authors motivate it by the lack of reliable Canadian estimates: survey-based figures ranged from a very small share of adults offering ride or accommodation services through sharing platforms to estimates that as many as 30% of Canadians take part in some form of informal paid work.

The paper adapts a typology of work arrangements developed in earlier U.S. research to the way work is reported to the Canada Revenue Agency. In this framework, gig workers are unincorporated self-employed workers (sole proprietors) who report business, professional or commission income on their individual tax return and attach at least one Statement of Business and Professional Activities (form T2125) without a business number (BN). The absence of a BN is used as the dividing line because obtaining one signals an expectation of continuity, and because businesses that employ people or import and export goods must have one. The main data are the [[Dataset/canadian-employer-employee-dynamics-database]], covering all workers aged 15 and older in annual tax files for 2005 to 2016, with a 25% subsample linked to 2016 Census of Population microdata to add education, occupation and immigrant status.

## Key Points

- The share of gig workers among all Canadian workers rose from 5.5% in 2005 to 8.2% in 2016 (about 991,000 to 1.67 million people), for both men (4.8% to 7.2%) and women (6.2% to 9.1%); the share was higher among women in every year, and the gap widened over time.
- The growth came in two jumps: one during the 2008/2009 recession, which the authors attribute largely to push factors such as declining job prospects, and one around 2012/2013, which they suggest may be related to the spread of online platforms in Canada.
- In 2016, 48.6% of gig workers had no wage or salary job, 36.3% had one and 15.1% had several, so gig workers were split almost evenly between those relying on gig earnings alone and those supplementing wages with them; both groups grew over the period.
- Gig income was typically small: median net gig income in 2016 was $4,303, and for many gig workers net gig income was negative. For about half of gig workers, gig earnings were more than three-quarters of their total earnings, but the median share of gig income in total income was only 22%.
- Workers in the bottom 40% of the income distribution were about twice as likely to do gig work as other workers.
- Gig work was usually temporary: roughly half of those who entered it in a given year had no gig income the next year, while about a quarter remained gig workers for three or more years.
- Entry into gig work followed a sharp drop in wage and salary earnings, and Employment Insurance benefits rose in the years before entry — which, given the eligibility rules, the authors read as a sign that outside shocks such as job loss contribute to the decision to enter gig work.
- Gig work was more prevalent among immigrants than among the Canadian-born — 10.8% of male immigrants in Canada for less than five years, against 6.1% of Canadian-born men — and among graduate-degree holders (13.7% of men and 16.5% of women), although more than a third of male gig workers had a high school diploma or less.
- By main occupation, the share of gig workers was highest in art, culture, recreation and sport (24.2% of men and 26.6% of women in those occupations); male gig workers were concentrated in professional, scientific and technical services and in construction, female gig workers in health care and social assistance and in professional, scientific and technical services.
- Unlike in the United States, unincorporated self-employment with T2125 income stayed nearly flat in Canadian tax data (12.7% to 12.9% of workers), and only a slightly larger share of sole proprietors reported income from several T2125 sources; the authors read the rise in gig work as reflecting both a decline in the share of unincorporated self-employed workers with a steady business and more self-employed workers doing gig work in addition to their main business.

## Notes

The paper compares self-employment estimates across sources. In 2016, tax data identified 9.5% of workers as incorporated self-employed and 14.6% as unincorporated self-employed, against 4.4% and 7.5% in the census and 7.1% and 9.5% in the [[Dataset/canadian-labour-force-survey]]. The authors suggest the differences partly reflect coverage: in tax data anyone with a stake of more than 10% in an incorporated business counts as incorporated self-employed and the reference period is the preceding calendar year, whereas census respondents are asked only about their main job in a reference week; tax data also capture occasional self-employment that survey respondents may ignore. They find the divergence between survey and administrative trends less pronounced in Canada than in the United States.

The authors note several limitations. Businesses with more than $30,000 in taxable revenue generally need a BN to collect sales taxes, so some gig workers above that threshold are missed and the true share is somewhat higher than 8.2% — though no higher than 10.3% even if all such sole proprietors were counted. Tax data say little about the nature of a job, its hours, hourly wages or duration, and the approach is unlikely to capture informal activities such as occasional babysitting, dog walking or lawn mowing. The authors also distinguish gig work from precarious work, which usually covers many traditional arrangements such as temporary, part-time and seasonal employment. They argue that because the underlying tax data will keep being produced regularly, the method allows the gig economy in Canada to be measured consistently over time.
