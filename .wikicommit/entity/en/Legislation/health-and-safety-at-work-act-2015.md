The Health and Safety at Work Act 2015 (HSWA) is New Zealand's key work health and safety law. According to WorkSafe New Zealand's guidance [[Report/managing-psychosocial-risks-at-work]], all work and workplaces are covered by it unless specifically excluded — for example, it does not apply to the armed forces in certain situations. The Act defines health as physical and mental health, which is why businesses must manage [[DefinedTerm/psychosocial-risks]] as well as physical ones.

## Details

The Act sets out duties for four types of duty holder. A person conducting a business or undertaking (PCBU) — which can be a company, a self-employed person, a partnership, a government agency, a local council or a school, but not its officers or workers, a volunteer association with no employees, or a home occupier paying for residential work — has the primary duty of care: it must ensure, so far as is [[DefinedTerm/reasonably-practicable]], the health and safety of its workers and that other persons are not put at risk by its work. It must manage risks by first eliminating them so far as is reasonably practicable and, where that is not possible, minimising them. Officers, such as company directors, partners and chief executives, must exercise due diligence to ensure the PCBU meets its duties. Workers — a broad category including employees, contractors, labour-hire employees, outworkers, apprentices, people on work trials and volunteer workers — must take reasonable care of their own health and safety and that of others, and comply with reasonable instructions and policies. Other persons at a workplace, such as visitors and customers, must take reasonable care as well. Upstream PCBUs, such as designers, manufacturers, suppliers and installers, must ensure that what they provide does not create health and safety risks.

Section 22 sets out what "so far as is reasonably practicable" means: what is known about a risk and about the control measures available to eliminate or minimise it, with cost justifying inaction only when it is grossly disproportionate to the risk. Under section 34, PCBUs whose duties overlap — for example in a shared workplace or a contracting chain — must consult, cooperate and coordinate with each other; they cannot contract out of their duties, although they may agree how specific duties will be fulfilled. Part 3 requires PCBUs to engage with workers on health and safety matters that affect them and to have practices that give workers reasonable opportunities to participate in improving health and safety; workers may be represented by health and safety representatives, unions or other authorised persons, and health and safety committees are another established means of participation.
