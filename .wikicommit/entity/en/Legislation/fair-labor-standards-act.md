The Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) is a United States federal law that sets minimum wage and overtime pay standards. It requires that employees receive at least the minimum wage and that they not be employed for more than 40 hours in a week without receiving at least one and one-half times their regular rate of pay for the overtime hours. The Wage and Hour Division of the US Department of Labor publishes fact sheets explaining how its provisions apply.

## Details

Because both entitlements depend on how much time is worked, the Act's reach turns on what counts as [[DefinedTerm/hours-worked]]. By statutory definition, the term "employ" includes "to suffer or permit to work", so work an employer did not request but allowed to be performed must be paid. The Wage and Hour Division applies this principle to waiting time, on-call time, rest and meal breaks, sleeping time, training and travel, noting that its guidance is general information and does not have the force and effect of law.

The overtime provisions apply on a workweek basis. Unless specifically exempted, covered employees must be paid for hours over 40 in a workweek at no less than one and one-half times their [[DefinedTerm/regular-rate-of-pay]], a rate that cannot be less than the minimum wage and that includes all remuneration for employment except payments the Act itself excludes. The workweek is a fixed and regularly recurring period of 168 hours — seven consecutive 24-hour periods — that may begin on any day and at any hour, may differ between employees, and cannot be averaged with other weeks. The Act places no limit on the number of hours employees aged 16 and older may work in a week, does not require premium pay for weekend or holiday work as such, and does not allow the overtime requirement to be waived by agreement; see [[DefinedTerm/overtime]].
