The Working Time Directive (Directive 2003/88/EC, concerning certain aspects of the organisation of working time) is the European Union law that sets minimum standards for working hours applicable throughout the EU. The European Commission presents it as a means of protecting workers' health and safety, grounded in the right to fair working conditions set out in Principle 10 of the European Pillar of Social Rights and in Article 31 of the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union, which gives every worker the right to limitation of maximum working hours, to daily and weekly rest periods and to an annual period of paid leave.

The Directive requires Member States to guarantee its rights for all workers, while allowing derogations under certain circumstances and with due regard to workers' health and safety. It also contains special rules for certain categories of workers — mobile workers, offshore workers and workers on board seagoing fishing vessels — and separate directives govern working hours for certain workers in specific transport sectors (air, rail, sea, inland waterways and road).

## Details

**Rights guaranteed.** According to the Commission's summary, Member States must guarantee:

- a limit on weekly working time: average working time in each seven-day period must not exceed 48 hours, including [[DefinedTerm/overtime]], with the average calculated over a reference period of up to 4, 6 or 12 months depending on national legislation or collective agreements;
- a rest break during working hours when a worker is on duty for longer than 6 hours;
- a minimum daily rest period of 11 consecutive hours in every 24 hours;
- a minimum weekly rest period of 24 uninterrupted hours in each seven-day period, in addition to the 11 hours' daily rest;
- paid annual leave of at least 4 weeks per year;
- extra protection for night work: average working hours must not exceed 8 hours per 24-hour period, night workers must not perform heavy or dangerous work for longer than 8 hours in any 24-hour period, and night workers have the right to free health assessments and, under certain circumstances, to transfer to day work.

How each of these rules has been interpreted is set out on the pages for [[DefinedTerm/maximum-weekly-working-hours]], [[DefinedTerm/rest-breaks-at-work]], [[DefinedTerm/statutory-annual-leave]], [[DefinedTerm/night-work]] and [[DefinedTerm/shift-work]], and the Directive's central definitions on [[DefinedTerm/working-time-and-rest-period]] and [[DefinedTerm/on-call-and-stand-by-time]].

**Origins and legal basis.** The Commission's 2023 [[Report/interpretative-communication-on-the-working-time-directive]] explains that the first directive of general application on working time, Directive 93/104/EC, was adopted in 1993 on the Treaty basis for minimum requirements to improve the working environment and protect workers' health and safety; the Court of Justice confirmed that this basis covers certain aspects of the organisation of working time. The current Directive repealed and replaced it and was adopted on the successor basis, now Article 153(2) TFEU. Apart from a wider scope and the deletion of the earlier provision that weekly rest should in principle include Sunday — which the Court had annulled as not justified on health and safety grounds — its provisions are essentially identical to those of the 1993 Directive as amended in 2000, so the Court's rulings on the earlier texts remain relevant. The Communication stresses that, in line with Recital 4, the improvement of workers' safety and health is an objective that should not be subordinated to purely economic considerations.

**Minimum standards.** The Directive sets minimum requirements, which Member States must transpose but may exceed: Article 15 preserves their right to apply more favourable laws or collective agreements, in which case the Court's interpretations do not govern the more protective national provisions. Article 23 provides that implementing the Directive may not be used to reduce the general level of protection afforded to workers; the Commission reads it in the same way as the Court has read the similar clause in the Fixed-Term Work Directive. The Directive does not regulate pay, apart from pay during annual leave, nor the "statutory" working week above which overtime is often paid, which is left to Member States within the 48-hour limit.

**Scope.** The Directive applies to all sectors of activity, public and private, and the Court interprets its scope broadly. The exclusion of certain specific public service activities that derives from the framework directive on safety and health at work (89/391/EEC) is read restrictively: it covers particular tasks rather than whole sectors, typically exceptional events such as natural or technological disasters, attacks or serious accidents. Activities of the armed forces, police, civil protection, emergency and firefighting services carried out under normal circumstances are covered. The Court has so far explicitly applied the exclusion only to state-employed foster parents who care for children continuously, and has held that certain activities of members of the armed forces — training, actual military operations, activities unsuited to a rotation system and exceptional events — fall outside the Directive while others, such as administrative, maintenance or health services, cannot be excluded in their entirety. Seafarers covered by the seafarers' working time directive are excluded, and under Article 14 the Directive gives way where other EU instruments contain more specific requirements, including those for young workers, civil aviation, road transport, cross-border railway services, inland waterways and work in fishing; workers outside the scope of those instruments remain covered by the general Directive. The Directive applies to anyone who qualifies as a [[DefinedTerm/worker-eu-law]], whatever their status under national law. Where a worker has several contracts with the same employer, the Court has held that the minimum daily rest period applies to the contracts taken as a whole; for contracts with different employers, on which the Court has not ruled, the Commission considers that the limits should as far as possible apply per worker.

**Derogations.** Member States may derogate, for specific categories of workers or sectors, from the rules on maximum weekly working time, minimum daily rest, breaks, minimum weekly rest and the length of night work. They may also allow an individual worker to opt out of the maximum weekly working time, subject to strict conditions on the worker's consent — in particular that the worker should not suffer any detriment for refusing — and to specific provisions on keeping records of opt-out decisions (see [[DefinedTerm/working-time-opt-out]]).

The Interpretative Communication describes these derogations as numerous and varied. It notes that they apply only if a Member State has chosen to use them, that they are interpreted so that their scope is limited to what is strictly necessary, and that they are subject to strict conditions protecting workers' safety and health. The main forms it identifies are:

- the "autonomous workers" derogation (Article 17(1)), from the rules on daily and weekly rest, breaks, maximum weekly working time, the length of night work and reference periods, for workers whose working time as a whole is not measured or predetermined or can be determined by the workers themselves, particularly managing executives, family workers and those officiating at religious ceremonies. The Court applies it only where the whole of the working time is of this kind, and the Commission considers the list of examples non-exhaustive, that not every managing executive qualifies, and that the derogation cannot be applied broadly to a whole category of workers; no compensatory rest is required;
- derogations for certain activities (Article 17(3)) — such as work far from home, security and surveillance, activities needing continuity of service or production, foreseeable surges of activity, railway transport, and accidents or unforeseeable circumstances, a list the Court treats as non-exhaustive — for shift and split work (Article 17(4)), and in any sector by collective agreement (Article 18). These require that the workers receive equivalent periods of compensatory rest, which, for daily rest, the Court has held must directly follow the working time it compensates; only in exceptional cases, where compensatory rest is objectively impossible, may "appropriate protection" be given instead, and an annual ceiling on days worked alone does not qualify;
- limits on extending the reference period for averaging weekly working time (Article 19): to no more than six months by law for the activities in Article 17(3), and no more than twelve months by collective agreement;
- special regimes for mobile workers not covered by transport-specific rules (Article 20), who may be excluded from the rules on daily and weekly rest, breaks and the length of night work but remain entitled to adequate rest; for offshore workers, for whom the reference period may be extended to twelve months; and for workers on board seagoing fishing vessels (Article 21), for whom working time must still be limited to 48 hours a week on average over up to twelve months, with either maximum hours of work (14 hours in any 24 hours and 72 in any seven days) or minimum hours of rest (10 hours in any 24 hours and 77 in any seven days).

**Implementation.** In 2023 the Commission adopted an Interpretative Communication, updating its 2017 one, to increase legal certainty and clarity about how the Directive is to be interpreted, together with an Implementation Report analysing the state of its transposition. The Directive itself (Article 24) requires such an implementation report every five years. The Commission presents the two documents as helping Member States and stakeholders implement the Directive better, in line with its objectives on the effective application and enforcement of EU law. The Communication itself stresses that it is not binding, that only the Court can interpret EU law authoritatively, and that since 1993 more than 80 judgments and orders of the Court have interpreted the Directive.
