The Working Time Directive (Directive 2003/88/EC, concerning certain aspects of the organisation of working time) is the European Union law that sets minimum standards for working hours applicable throughout the EU. The European Commission presents it as a means of protecting workers' health and safety, grounded in the right to fair working conditions set out in Principle 10 of the European Pillar of Social Rights and in Article 31 of the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union, which gives every worker the right to limitation of maximum working hours, to daily and weekly rest periods and to an annual period of paid leave.

The Directive requires Member States to guarantee its rights for all workers, while allowing derogations under certain circumstances and with due regard to workers' health and safety. It also contains special rules for certain categories of workers — mobile workers, offshore workers and workers on board seagoing fishing vessels — and separate directives govern working hours for certain workers in specific transport sectors (air, rail, sea, inland waterways and road).

## Details

**Rights guaranteed.** According to the Commission's summary, Member States must guarantee:

- a limit on weekly working time: average working time in each seven-day period must not exceed 48 hours, including [[DefinedTerm/overtime]], with the average calculated over a reference period of up to 4, 6 or 12 months depending on national legislation or collective agreements;
- a rest break during working hours when a worker is on duty for longer than 6 hours;
- a minimum daily rest period of 11 consecutive hours in every 24 hours;
- a minimum weekly rest period of 24 uninterrupted hours in each seven-day period, in addition to the 11 hours' daily rest;
- paid annual leave of at least 4 weeks per year;
- extra protection for night work: average working hours must not exceed 8 hours per 24-hour period, night workers must not perform heavy or dangerous work for longer than 8 hours in any 24-hour period, and night workers have the right to free health assessments and, under certain circumstances, to transfer to day work.

**Derogations.** Member States may derogate, for specific categories of workers or sectors, from the rules on maximum weekly working time, minimum daily rest, breaks, minimum weekly rest and the length of night work. They may also allow an individual worker to opt out of the maximum weekly working time, subject to strict conditions on the worker's consent — in particular that the worker should not suffer any detriment for refusing — and to specific provisions on keeping records of opt-out decisions.

**Implementation.** In 2023 the Commission adopted an Interpretative Communication, updating its 2017 one, to increase legal certainty and clarity about how the Directive is to be interpreted, together with an Implementation Report analysing the state of its transposition. The Directive itself (Article 24) requires such an implementation report every five years. The Commission presents the two documents as helping Member States and stakeholders implement the Directive better, in line with its objectives on the effective application and enforcement of EU law.
