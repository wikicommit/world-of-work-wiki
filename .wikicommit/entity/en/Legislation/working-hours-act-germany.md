The Working Hours Act (Arbeitszeitgesetz, ArbZG) of 1994 is the German law that frames the regulation of working time, including flexible working hours. In the reading of [[Report/regulation-of-flexible-working-hours]], an IAB statement to the North Rhine-Westphalia state parliament, it is part of the solution to a core task of modern working societies: reconciling establishments' interest in adjusting working and operating hours to demand with employees' interest in adjusting working time to their lives outside work. It helps prevent those interests from turning into practices that either damage employees' lives or block establishments' needs to adapt, and it opens up a wide range of adjustment options that the parties to collective agreements or the parties at establishment level can conclude as fair agreements.

## Details

**Rest periods.** The act provides that the uninterrupted rest period between the end of work and its resumption should be at least eleven hours. Under section 7(1), the parties to a collective agreement may shorten it by up to two hours if the nature of the work requires this and the reduction is compensated within a period to be set. If rest is interrupted during on-call duty (Rufbereitschaft) and the work done amounts to less than half of the rest period, the rest can be made up at other times (section 5(3)). How contact by the employer outside working time and outside standby and on-call duty is to be treated is not addressed.

**Deviations by agreement.** Section 7 permits many deviations — explicitly from sections 3 to 6 — on the basis of a collective agreement, or of a works or service agreement based on a collective agreement. As an example, collective agreements can extend working time beyond ten hours per working day where it regularly and to a considerable extent includes readiness for work or standby duty, or set a different compensation period.

**Assessment.** The IAB statement concludes that the act sets a framework that is binding yet permits a sufficient degree of flexibility, and that, because its room for manoeuvre has to be used through collective arrangements, it keeps the power asymmetry between establishments and individual employees from working to employees' detriment. It considers that new compromises — for example more flexibility on the daily maximum in exchange for employer concessions, subject to union or works-council agreement — could be negotiated within the act's framework.
