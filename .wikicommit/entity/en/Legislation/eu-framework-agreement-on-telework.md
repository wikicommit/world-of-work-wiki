The EU Framework Agreement on Telework is an agreement adopted by the European social partners in 2002 to clarify the rights, obligations and protection of workers in telework arrangements. According to Eurofound's report [[Report/telework-in-the-eu-regulatory-frameworks-and-recent-updates]], it is still the main EU reference for a normative definition of telework, and most Member States implemented national-level regulations after it was adopted. It defines telework as:

> Any form of organising and/or performing work using information technology, in the context of an employment contract/relationship, in which work, which could also be performed at the employer's premises, is carried out away from those premises on a regular basis.

Eurofound identifies five elements in this definition: telework is a work arrangement rather than a type of labour contract, so only employees with an employment contract are covered; it entails the use of ICT for work; only telework carried out on a regular basis is covered, which Eurofound's report interprets, citing the social partners, as between one and five days a week; it covers only stationary jobs that could also be performed at the employer's premises, which excludes jobs in which mobility is required by the labour process; and it may include several workplaces other than the employer's premises.

## Details

**Principal provisions.** As described in the Eurofound report, the agreement rests on a voluntary principle: telework is taken up by agreement between employer and employee. It requires companies to give teleworkers written information on matters such as a description of the work to be done and details of their immediate superior, and it acknowledges a reversibility principle — the chance to return to working at the employer's premises. Under its section on work organisation, the teleworker manages the organisation of their own working time, and employers are asked to ensure that measures are taken to prevent the teleworker from being isolated from the rest of the working community in the company, such as giving them the opportunity to meet colleagues regularly and access to company information. On equipment, Article 7 provides that, as a general rule, the employer is responsible for providing, installing and maintaining the equipment necessary for regular telework unless the teleworker uses their own, and that, where telework is performed on a regular basis, the employer compensates or covers the costs directly caused by the work, in particular those relating to communication. On occupational safety and health it contains mainly general recommendations on informing and consulting teleworkers about OSH policies and on preventing some psychosocial risks, notably isolation.

**Implementation.** The agreement was implemented in different ways according to national industrial relations systems. In Belgium, France and Luxembourg it was implemented through national cross-sectoral agreements that were extended to all employees by royal, ministerial or Grand-Ducal decree respectively; these first-generation national agreements include France's 2005 cross-industry agreement (*accord national interprofessionnel*) and Belgium's Collective Labour Agreement No. 85 of 2005. In Greece, Italy and Spain it was implemented through cross-sectoral non-binding agreements, which provide only recommendations and guidelines for lower-level bargaining. Portugal and Slovenia implemented it through reforms of their labour codes, and Denmark, Finland and Sweden through national framework agreements giving general guidelines, together with sectoral collective agreements. Germany's statutory legislation implementing it covers only home-based telework. In Latvia, the social partners implemented it through a non-binding framework agreement in 2006, and in Norway through guidelines in 2005.

Its influence is visible in national law: most countries with a statutory definition of telework follow an approach similar to the agreement's, and Eurofound found that Latvia, Lithuania, Malta and Slovakia seem to follow exactly the same approach. The most common rule on compensation in national legislation likewise follows Article 7, making the employer responsible for the provision, installation and maintenance of equipment. The main departures concern regularity: several countries also cover occasional telework, and those that cover only regular telework measure regularity differently (see [[DefinedTerm/working-from-home]]).

**Assessment.** Eurofound's 2022 report concludes that the agreement provides a conceptual common ground but could be considered outdated. Technological progress has made its ICT requirement largely irrelevant, since nearly all remote work is now performed through ICT, and regular telework is no longer the only form of remote work: telework is also done occasionally or with a degree of mobility, and in around half of the Member States employees who work remotely only occasionally can be excluded from telework regimes and from protections such as mutual written agreements or the [[DefinedTerm/right-to-disconnect]] where these apply only to regular telework. The report suggests a clearer definition may be needed at EU level as terms such as [[DefinedTerm/hybrid-work]] and "mobile work" spread, and its policy pointers call for EU-level social dialogue to assess whether the 2002 agreement continues to meet the needs of both businesses and workers.
