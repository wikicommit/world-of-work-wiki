Flexible (or variable) working hours, in the sense adopted by [[ScholarlyArticle/effects-of-flexible-working-hours-on-employee-health]] on the basis of earlier literature, are arrangements in which the duration of working time is not at issue, but its timing or its distribution over the day or the working week varies. The central distinction is who decides on that timing: under employer-controlled (externally determined) flexibility, the employer decides on the number of working days per week and/or the timing of working hours within the day, for example through shift work, emergency duties or on-call duties; under employee-controlled (self-determined) flexibility, the employees decide themselves.

## Usage

The paper distinguishes two main variants of self-determined flexibility. Flextime (German *Gleitzeit*) is a regime of partially self-determined flexibility in which attendance is compulsory only during core hours while employees decide freely when to start and finish their day, typically with a working-time account on which hours can be accumulated and run down. Under fully free scheduling, core hours disappear and there is no obligation to balance hours over a set period; this often comes with the option of working from home. The German [[Dataset/socio-economic-panel]] records four categories: fixed start and end times, varying daily hours set by the employer, no formal arrangement with hours set by the employee, and flextime with a working-time account.

Drawing on earlier research, the paper sets out mechanisms in both directions. Self-determined flexibility can make more efficient use of people's working capacity across the day, reduce the stress of coordinating private and social obligations with fixed start, break and finishing times, and give employees autonomy and control over their working conditions. It can also extend working hours, temporarily to bank hours for later use or permanently as a form of "self-exploitation", especially where more responsibility for results comes with it, and the paper cites earlier studies that attribute negative health effects mainly to the irregularity of such hours. The authors' own estimates for Germany show neutral or slightly negative health effects of small magnitude, mainly for employer-controlled flexibility and flextime.
