Over-qualification describes employed people who have a tertiary level of educational attainment but work in a low- or medium-skilled occupation; Eurostat notes that it may also be referred to as a "vertical" skills mismatch. In Eurostat's statistics the over-qualification rate is the share of employed persons with tertiary education (ISCED 2011 levels 5–8: short-cycle tertiary, bachelor's, master's and doctoral or equivalent) who are employed in ISCO-08 major groups 4–9 — clerical support workers, services and sales workers, skilled agricultural, forestry and fishery workers, craft and related trades workers, plant and machine operators and assemblers, and elementary occupations. Managers, professionals, technicians and associate professionals, and armed forces occupations are not counted as low- or medium-skilled.

## Usage

Eurostat publishes over-qualification rates as part of its migrant integration statistics, using data from the [[Dataset/eu-labour-force-survey]], to show how foreign citizens compare with nationals in terms of skills and successful participation in the labour market. People are grouped either by citizenship (nationals, citizens of another EU country and non-EU citizens) or by country of birth (native-born, born in another EU country and born outside the EU), with further breakdowns by sex and by age (20–34 and 35–64).

Non-EU citizens have consistently had the highest rates. Between 2015 and 2025 their rate fell from 45.2% to 41.4% and that of citizens of other EU countries from 33.8% to 30.2%, while the rate for nationals stayed lowest and broadly stable at about 21–20%. The 2025 rate for non-EU citizens (41.4%) was up from 39.8% in 2024 and the highest since 2020. Women were more affected than men, especially among non-EU citizens. Rates were higher for 35- to 64-year-olds than for 20- to 34-year-olds among non-EU citizens (44.1% against 37.6%) and citizens of other EU countries (31.1% against 28.0%), whereas for nationals the older group's rate was lower (19.3% against 21.8%).

By country of birth the pattern is similar but the gaps are smaller: in 2025 the rate was 35.7% for people born outside the EU, 26.8% for those born in another EU country and 19.6% for the native-born, with women born outside the EU the most affected group (37.7%, against 33.7% for men in the same group).

Rates differ widely between EU countries. Greece recorded the highest rates for non-EU citizens (85.4% for men and 85.3% for women in 2025) and among the highest for people born outside the EU (58.4%), alongside Estonia (52.2%) and Italy (50.4%). Ireland and Cyprus were exceptions to the usual ordering, with citizens of other EU countries recording the highest rates; gender disparities were notably wide in Italy and Cyprus, while Germany had relatively balanced rates for men and women. Even among the native-born, some countries recorded notable levels, such as Spain (32.1%) and Greece (30.7%).

Eurostat places the indicator in the context of skills matching: some mismatches are inevitable, but high and persistent ones may be costly for employers, workers and society, and they are considered especially relevant for vulnerable groups such as older workers, young people moving from education into work, and migrants. One issue affecting non-EU citizens is lower recognition of their professional qualifications, and Eurostat suggests that easier recognition procedures and opportunities for adult migrants to upgrade or equalise their qualifications, including access to lifelong learning, may help enhance their employability. The indicator relates to the wider picture of [[DefinedTerm/labour-market-integration-of-migrants]].
