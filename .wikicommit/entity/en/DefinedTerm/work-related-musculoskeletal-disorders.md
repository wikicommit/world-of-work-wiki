Musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) are impairments of bodily structures such as muscles, joints, tendons, ligaments, nerves, cartilage, bones and the localised blood circulation system; when they are caused or aggravated primarily by work and by the effects of the immediate environment in which work is carried out, they are known as work-related MSDs. The term covers both medically established periarticular diseases of the limbs and spine (specific MSDs, such as tendinopathies and nerve compression syndromes) and multiple or localised pain syndromes that are less well characterised clinically (non-specific MSDs, such as non-specific low back pain). Most work-related MSDs are chronic, cumulative disorders resulting from repeated exposure to loads over a long period, but they can also be acute traumas, such as fractures sustained in an accident. In statistics they are usually grouped by affected area — the back, the upper limbs or neck, and the lower limbs — and in both the EU and Great Britain they are one of the two largest categories of work-related ill health, alongside [[DefinedTerm/work-related-stress]], depression or anxiety.

## Usage

**Measurement.** Because surveys ask about the location of health complaints rather than their clinical nature, self-reported figures can include complaints not caused by musculoskeletal overstrain and are likely to overestimate the prevalence of MSDs; administrative data — recognised occupational diseases, declared accidents at work, hospital discharges — capture only more severe cases. Different surveys also produce different rates: the [[Dataset/european-working-conditions-survey]] asks about backache and muscular pains of any severity, the [[Dataset/european-health-interview-survey]] only about chronic back and neck disorders, and the [[Dataset/eu-labour-force-survey]] ad hoc module only about the most serious work-related health problem. In Great Britain the Health and Safety Executive estimates work-related MSDs from workers' self-reports in the Labour Force Survey.

**European Union.** According to the EU-OSHA report [[Report/work-related-msds-prevalence-costs-and-demographics-in-the-eu]], roughly three out of every five workers in the EU-28 reported MSD complaints in 2015 — backache (43 %) and muscular pains in the upper limbs (41 %) more often than in the lower limbs (29 %) — and 60 % of workers with a work-related health problem named MSDs as their most serious one in 2013. Prevalence was highest in construction, water supply, and agriculture, forestry and fishing and among blue-collar occupations, and was higher among women, older workers and workers with lower education. Work-related MSDs were the most commonly recognised occupational disease in France, Italy and Spain, although national recognition systems differ too much for their figures to be compared. The report found MSD complaints associated with working in tiring or painful positions, carrying or moving heavy loads, repetitive hand or arm movements and vibrations from hand tools, and with organisational and [[DefinedTerm/psychosocial-risks]] such as anxiety, overall fatigue, sleeping problems and verbal abuse at work. Workers with MSDs were more often absent from work and more often worked while sick ([[DefinedTerm/presenteeism]]), and MSDs accounted for about 15 % of the [[DefinedTerm/disability-adjusted-life-year]]s lost to work-related injury and illness. The report argues for integrated prevention that addresses physical, organisational and psychosocial factors together, targeted at the different body areas, and combined with early intervention and support for returning to work.

**Great Britain.** According to [[Report/health-and-safety-at-work-summary-statistics-2025]], 511,000 workers in Great Britain were suffering from a work-related musculoskeletal disorder (new or long-standing) in 2024/25, 27% of all work-related ill health, and 173,000 had a new case that year; 7.1 million working days were lost as a result. By affected area, 43% of cases (221,000) involved the back, 41% (211,000) the upper limbs or neck and 15% (78,000) the lower limbs. Construction, transportation and storage, and administrative and support service activities had higher-than-average rates over 2022/23–2024/25.

Before the coronavirus pandemic, both the rate of self-reported work-related musculoskeletal disorders in Great Britain and the working days lost per worker to them showed a generally downward trend; the rates in each of the latest three years are similar to the 2018/19 pre-pandemic level.
