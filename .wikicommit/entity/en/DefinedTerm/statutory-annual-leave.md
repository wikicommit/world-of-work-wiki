Statutory annual leave is the minimum amount of paid holiday that workers are legally entitled to take each year, as distinct from any extra leave an employer chooses to offer. In the European Union, Article 7 of the [[Legislation/working-time-directive]] requires Member States to ensure that every worker is entitled to paid annual leave of at least four weeks, and Article 31(2) of the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union recognises the right to an annual period of paid leave. In the UK, according to the government's GOV.UK guidance, almost all people classed as workers — including agency workers, workers with irregular hours and part-year workers — are entitled to 5.6 weeks' paid holiday a year, known as statutory leave entitlement or annual leave. Paid annual leave is a legal right that an employer must provide.

## Usage

**Under EU law.** The European Commission's [[Report/interpretative-communication-on-the-working-time-directive]] outlines the Court of Justice's extensive case-law on the right to paid annual leave, which the Court treats as a particularly important principle of EU social law that may not be interpreted restrictively and from which the Directive permits no derogation. The leave has a dual purpose: to let the worker rest and to enjoy a period of relaxation and leisure.

- *Entitlement.* The minimum is four calendar weeks free of work, whether the worker is full-time or part-time, converted into days or hours by reference to those specified in the employment contract. Where a worker's work pattern changes, entitlement is calculated separately for each period; moving from full-time to part-time cannot reduce leave already accrued. During short-time working with no actual work, and during parental leave, no leave accrues, but workers on sick leave or maternity leave accrue it as if they had worked, and a worker unlawfully dismissed and then reinstated is entitled to leave for the period in between. Member States may regulate how the right is exercised — for example the planning of holidays or advance notice — but may not make it subject to preconditions that prevent some workers from benefiting, such as 13 weeks of uninterrupted employment with the same employer.
- *The employer's role.* Employers must not deter workers from taking leave. They must inform workers accurately and in good time of their entitlement and that it will otherwise be lost, and encourage them to take it, though they need not force them to; the burden of proof is on the employer. Leave cannot lapse automatically at the end of the reference period if the employer did not enable the worker to take it, and the Court has held that in that case it can neither lapse at the end of a carry-over period nor become time-barred at the end of a three-year limitation period; it may be lost if the worker deliberately and knowingly chose not to take it.
- *Carry-over.* A worker who could not take leave — because of sick leave, maternity leave or another period of leave guaranteed by EU law — must be able to take it at another time, if necessary in a later period. Member States may limit carry-over after long-term sickness, but the carry-over period must be substantially longer than the reference period to which it relates: the Court rejected a nine-month period and accepted fifteen months.
- *Pay.* Entitlement to leave and to pay for it are two aspects of one right, so the worker must receive normal remuneration, comparable to periods of work. That includes components linked to the worker's personal and professional status, allowances linked to tasks under the contract, and commission on sales, and it includes [[DefinedTerm/overtime]] pay where the contract requires overtime on a broadly regular and predictable basis and that pay forms a significant part of total remuneration. Paying for leave in instalments spread over the year is incompatible with the Directive.
- *Allowance in lieu.* The four-week minimum may not be replaced by a payment except when the employment relationship ends; then, whatever the reason for the ending — including resignation, retirement or death, in which case the right passes to the heirs — an allowance is due for leave not taken, without any prior request.
- *Effect.* Article 7 has direct effect against the State and public employers, and the Court has recognised that Article 31(2) of the Charter can be relied on in disputes between private parties, but only for the four-week minimum.

Member States may grant more than four weeks and set different conditions for the additional leave, for example excluding its carry-over or payment in lieu. Special leave for specific needs, such as marriage or a family death, falls outside the Directive, while for other national forms of leave the purpose of the leave decides how it interacts with annual leave.

**In the UK.** For most workers on a 5-day week, the UK entitlement amounts to at least 28 days' paid leave a year, the equivalent of 5.6 weeks. The statutory entitlement is capped at 28 days, so someone working 6 days a week is still entitled to only 28 days. Part-time workers with regular hours throughout the year are entitled to 5.6 weeks, which comes to fewer than 28 days — for example, at least 16.8 days a year (3 × 5.6) for someone working 3 days a week.

People who work irregular hours (where the number of hours worked in a pay period often or always changes) or only part of the year (with periods of at least a week in a leave year in which they are not required to work and are not paid) are entitled to up to 5.6 weeks, but build up ("accrue") it according to the hours they have already worked rather than receiving a fixed number of days or hours. For leave years beginning on or before 31 March 2024, their entitlement did not have to be accrued in this way.

Bank and public holidays do not have to be given as paid leave, but an employer can choose to count them as part of statutory annual leave. An employer can also offer more leave than the legal minimum and need not apply all the statutory rules to that extra leave — for example, it may require a period of service before a worker becomes entitled to it. Workers also have the right to holiday pay, to build up holiday entitlement while on maternity, paternity or adoption leave and while off sick, and to request holiday at the same time as sick leave. Where a worker believes their rights to leave and pay are not being met, the guidance points to ways of resolving the dispute.

**In Japan.** According to the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare, an employer must give 10 days of annual paid leave, which may be taken continuously or in parts, to a worker who has been continuously employed for six months and has attended at least 80% of all working days in that period. After the first six months, the entitlement increases by one day for each further year of continuous service, and by two days a year from three years and six months of service, up to a maximum of 20 days.
