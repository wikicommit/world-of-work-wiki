Returns to work experience are the increase in earnings — expressed as a percentage increase in wages — that stems from an additional year spent at work. The measure goes back to the labour economist Jacob Mincer, who showed in the 1970s that earnings differentials are influenced by investments in human capital that grow over the life cycle, first in school and later at work. [[Report/world-development-report-2019]] uses it to assess how far work serves as a venue for acquiring skills after school: the higher the return to an additional year of work, the more human capital a worker is gaining on the job.

## Usage

**Measurement.** The report estimates returns to potential work experience for 135 economies from household and labour force survey data in the World Bank's International Income Distribution Data Set. Following earlier work, it groups years of experience into seven bins, estimates wage growth for each bin relative to having no experience, and averages the seven using a geometric mean.

**Differences between countries and occupations.** On average, an additional year of work experience raises monthly wages by 4.0 percent in high-income economies. It raises wages by 5.5 percent in the Netherlands and Sweden but by 0.3 percent in Afghanistan. The report attributes the gap partly to occupations — workers in emerging economies are more likely to hold manual jobs with less scope for learning and a greater risk of automation — and partly to education. An additional year of work in cognitive occupations increases wages by 2.9 percent, against 1.9 percent in manual occupations; elementary occupations such as cleaners, and skilled agricultural workers, have the lowest returns, and professionals, managers and technicians the highest. Poorly educated workers see wage growth of 2 percent per additional year of work, highly educated workers 2.4 percent. Work complements schooling rather than substituting for it: one additional year in school produces, on average, the same increase in wages as four years at work — three years on the job in Germany, five in Malawi and eight in Guatemala.

**Formal and informal work.** Returns to experience are higher in formal than in [[DefinedTerm/informal-employment]]. In emerging economies, the earnings increase for an additional year of work averages 1.4 percent for informal wage workers and 1.8 percent for formal wage workers; in Kenya, a year in the informal sector raises income by 2.7 percent against 4.1 percent in the formal sector, and in India the returns in the formal sector are over twice those in the informal sector.

**Women and men.** Women generally face lower payoffs from work experience (1.9 percent) than men (3.1 percent). In Mali the figures are 1.6 and 3.1 percent, while in Denmark both are 5 percent; in Bangladesh, where the law prescribes no parental leave, nursing breaks or flexible schedules, women's returns are 0.8 percent, almost half of men's, whereas in Portugal, Spain and Sweden, which provide paid leave for both men and women, returns are similar across genders. The report finds that the more legal restrictions women face at work, the lower their payoff from work experience, while cautioning that the laws may not be what causes the difference.

**Urban and rural work.** In emerging economies, an additional year of work experience in a city is worth a 2.2 percent increase in pay, and returns to work in urban areas are 1.7 times those in rural areas where agriculture predominates. The report proposes raising returns to work by creating formal jobs for the poor, removing legal restrictions on women's work, and raising agricultural productivity.
