Labour market integration of migrants concerns how non-nationals, foreign-born people and native-born people with a migrant background fare in the labour market compared with the rest of the population. In its migrant integration statistics, Eurostat measures it with three labour market indicators — the labour force participation rate, the employment rate and the [[DefinedTerm/unemployment-rate]] — compared across citizenship groups (nationals, citizens of another EU country and non-EU citizens) and across categories of [[DefinedTerm/migration-status]], using data from the [[Dataset/eu-labour-force-survey]].

## Usage

Eurostat's main analysis covers people aged 20–64, the age group on which the EU's Action Plan on Integration and Inclusion 2021–2027 focuses its employment analysis; youth unemployment is measured for people aged 15–29. The participation rate is the labour force (employed plus unemployed) as a share of the total population, and the employment rate is the number of employed persons — employees, self-employed and contributing family workers — as a share of the total population.

At EU level, non-EU citizens fare worst of the three citizenship groups on all three measures. In 2025, among people aged 20–64:

- the labour force participation rate was 83.2% for citizens of other EU countries, 81.3% for nationals and 74.3% for non-EU citizens. Participation by non-EU citizens fell between 2008 and 2016 before rising gradually, whereas it rose for the other two groups from 2005;
- the employment rate was 77.3% for citizens of other EU countries, 77.0% for nationals and 65.2% for non-EU citizens, whose rate had been the lowest in 2005 (58.4%) but grew fastest after 2020;
- the unemployment rate was 12.3% for non-EU citizens, 7.0% for citizens of other EU countries and 5.2% for nationals. Non-EU citizens' unemployment fell from 20.0% in 2015 but remained nearly two and a half times that of nationals.

All groups saw a temporary setback during the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020.

Eurostat also compares employment conditions across the same groups, for people aged 20–64. Between 2005 and 2025, citizens of other EU countries and non-EU citizens consistently had lower shares of [[DefinedTerm/self-employment]], and higher shares of temporary contracts and of [[DefinedTerm/part-time-employment]], than nationals. In 2025:

- self-employment made up 13.5% of employment among nationals, 10.9% among citizens of other EU countries and 10.1% among non-EU citizens;
- 22.1% of non-EU citizen employees had a temporary contract, against 12.4% of citizens of other EU countries and 10.7% of nationals; for all three groups this was the lowest share since 2005;
- part-time employment was 21.8% among non-EU citizens, 20.0% among citizens of other EU countries and 16.6% among nationals.

By migration status, native-born people with two foreign-born parents had the lowest self-employment share (9.3%) and the highest part-time share (23.6%), while foreign-born employees (16.1%) and native-born employees with two foreign-born parents (15.9%) were the most likely to hold temporary contracts. Across all groups, men had higher self-employment shares, and women higher shares of temporary contracts and of part-time work.

The national picture varies. Gaps between non-EU citizens and nationals were particularly large in Belgium, Germany and the Netherlands, while in a few countries — Greece and Slovakia among them — non-EU citizens recorded higher participation or employment rates than both other groups. Non-EU citizens' unemployment was highest in Sweden, Finland and Spain, and youth unemployment showed the same ordering of groups as for all ages in most countries.

Education shapes the pattern. Participation and employment generally rise with educational attainment for every migration-status group, but the differences across education levels are smaller for foreign-born people and for the native-born with two foreign-born parents. Among people with low education, foreign-born people had higher employment rates than the native-born, whereas among the tertiary-educated the gap reverses in favour of native-born people with two native-born parents. Unemployment falls with education in all citizenship groups, most markedly among nationals.

National statistics can add measures that the EU indicators above do not cover, such as hours worked. Statistics Norway's [[Report/how-much-do-immigrants-in-norway-work]], based on [[Dataset/register-based-employment-statistics-for-immigrants-norway]], looks at how much immigrants work as well as whether they work. In the fourth quarter of 2025, 68% of immigrants aged 20–66 in Norway were employed, against 80% of the rest of the population, and 74% of employed immigrants worked full time, against 79%. Full-time work was most widespread in the groups with the highest employment rates — immigrants from the Nordic countries and from EU/EFTA countries — and least in those with the lowest, from Asia and Africa, partly because these groups more often work in industries where part-time work is common. The gaps between immigrant men and women were larger than in the rest of the population, and the full-time share rose with length of residence.
