Psychosocial risks are risk factors related to work — how it is designed, organized and managed, and its social context — that are associated with poor mental health, as well as poor physical health. The *WHO guidelines on mental health at work* note that there is reasonable consensus on the influence of certain of these risk factors on mental health at work, and group them into ten categories: work content or task design; workload and work pace; work schedule; control; environment and equipment; organizational culture and function; interpersonal relationships at work; role in the organization; career development; and the home–work interface.

## Usage

WHO's categories, adapted from earlier work, give examples for each: short work cycles, under-use of skills or continuous exposure to people (task design); work overload or under-load, machine pacing and time pressure (workload and pace); shift work, night shifts, unpredictable, long or unsociable hours (work schedule); low participation in decision-making (control); poor equipment, lighting or noise (environment); poor communication and organizational change (culture and function); isolation, conflict, bullying, harassment and lack of social support (interpersonal relationships); role ambiguity and role conflict (role); career stagnation, poor pay and job insecurity (career development); and conflicting demands of work and home, including caregiving (home–work interface). WHO adds that many further risks may be specific to countries or occupations, and that emerging risks appear as work culture changes or after major societal events.

The guidelines summarise evidence linking these risks to mental health outcomes: high workload increases the risk of symptoms of mental health conditions; long working hours are associated with depressive symptoms and with the onset of risky alcohol use; job strain (low decision latitude with high demands) is associated with depression; workplace bullying and violence, role ambiguity and conflict, job insecurity and [[DefinedTerm/effort-reward-imbalance]] are associated with depression or depressive symptoms. On this basis WHO conditionally recommends organizational interventions that assess, mitigate or remove psychosocial risks, with an assessment of such risks built into regular occupational health risk assessment — including whenever changes in work or its organization may harm health.
