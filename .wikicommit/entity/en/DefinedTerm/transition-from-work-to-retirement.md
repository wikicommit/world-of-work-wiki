The transition from work to retirement is the passage of older people out of the labour market and into receipt of an old-age pension. In the EU it typically begins in the late 50s, and most people leave the labour market before the age of 70. For most people the move is abrupt — they stop working shortly after receiving their first old-age pension — but a minority keep working after their pension starts, making the transition gradual.

## Usage

Eurostat measures the transition with the [[Dataset/eu-lfs-module-on-pensions-and-labour-market-participation]], which covers people aged 50–74.

**Timing.** In 2023 the EU employment rate stayed slightly above 80% at ages 50–54, declined gradually to about 70% by the late 50s and then fell steeply from age 60 (63.1%) to 8.7% at age 69, while the share of old-age pensioners rose from 15.1% at 60 to 92.0% at 69. The average age at which people first received an old-age pension was 61.3 years, 2.1 years higher than in 2012, ranging from 58.3 years in Slovenia to 65.7 years in Denmark; around two thirds of pensioners first received it between 60 and 65. Between 2012 and 2023 the whole transition shifted roughly five years later. Because the share of employed people who also received a pension stayed small in both years, Eurostat attributes the shift mainly to higher eligible pension ages (see [[DefinedTerm/statutory-pension-age]]) rather than to people choosing to work longer after their pension starts.

**Working after the first pension.** In the six months after their first old-age pension, 64.7% of EU pensioners stopped working, 22.4% did not work and 13.0% continued, about half of them without changes and half with changes such as switching jobs or working fewer hours. Continuing was most common in the Baltic and Nordic countries — over 40% in Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania and Sweden — and rarest in Romania, Greece and Spain.

**Reasons to continue.** The two main reasons given in the EU were enjoying working and being productive (36.3%) and financial necessity (28.6%), followed by social integration (11.2%), the financial attractiveness of work (9.1%) and a partner still being in work (3.4%). Men more often gave enjoyment and women more often financial necessity. Financial necessity was the most frequent reason in 11 EU countries and enjoyment in 14, and Eurostat found no correlation between the main reason and the share of people continuing to work.

**Reasons to stop.** More than three quarters of those who stopped working did so because they had reached pension eligibility and felt they had worked enough. Smaller shares stopped because of their own health problems (6.8%) or because they had reached a maximum retirement age at which law, contract or agreement required them to leave their job (6.3%). Care responsibilities were the main reason for 2.8% of women and 0.7% of men.

A cluster analysis of pension systems and pensioner behaviour grouped EU countries into five clusters — for example, Ireland, the Netherlands and Sweden, with few statutory-only pensions, high pension ages and pensioners who keep working because they enjoy it, contrasted with Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania, with lower pension ages and pensioners who keep working mainly for financial reasons.

The transition is a policy concern because of population ageing, which puts the financial sustainability of pension, healthcare and long-term care systems at risk. Policymakers' response focuses on extending working lives while giving older people access to adequate social protection, and Principle 15 of the European Pillar of Social Rights sets out rights to a pension that ensures an adequate income and to resources for living in dignity in old age.
