The working time opt-out is the option, under Article 22(1) of the EU [[Legislation/working-time-directive]], for a Member State not to apply the Directive's limit of 48 hours of average weekly working time ([[DefinedTerm/maximum-weekly-working-hours]]) to a worker who has agreed to work longer. A Member State using it must respect the general principles of protecting workers' safety and health and ensure that: no employer requires a worker to work more than 48 hours a week on average without first obtaining the worker's agreement; no worker suffers any detriment for not agreeing; the employer keeps up-to-date records of all workers who do such work; those records are available to the competent authorities, which may prohibit or restrict working beyond the limit for reasons of workers' safety or health; and the employer informs the authorities, on request, of the cases in which workers have agreed.

## Usage

The European Commission's [[Report/interpretative-communication-on-the-working-time-directive]] sets out how the Court of Justice has interpreted the opt-out and where the Commission takes its own view.

**Transposition.** Like the Directive's other derogations, the opt-out applies only if national law gives effect to it; the Court has held that where it has not been transposed, the 48-hour limit, including overtime, continues to apply. Its conditions must all be met together.

**Consent.** The worker's agreement must be individual. The Court has held that consent given by trade union representatives in a collective or other agreement is not equivalent to the worker's own, which is consistent with the maximum weekly working time not being among the provisions that collective agreements may derogate from. A worker who gives up this right must do so freely, with full knowledge of the facts, and expressly; a contract that merely refers to a collective agreement allowing longer hours is not enough, because the worker must be regarded as the weaker party to the employment contract. The Directive does not require written consent, but the Commission considers that a written form setting out the exact extent of the agreement could help prove it. The Commission also considers that, to keep the consent free, the worker must be able to revoke it — possibly subject to a period of notice proportionate to the employer's need to find alternatives — and that unlimited and irrevocable opt-outs would run counter to the Directive.

**No detriment.** The Commission considers that the protection covers not only dismissal but also other forms of harm or disadvantage, such as a compulsory transfer to another department or position, and extends to a worker who withdraws their agreement; Member States must ensure that means of redress are available.

**Effect.** Where transposed, the opt-out allows an individual worker to work more than 48 hours over a seven-day period, calculated as an average over a reference period of up to four months. The Commission considers that a Member State may use it as a full derogation from both the limit and the reference period, or more narrowly — for example by setting a higher weekly maximum, or by allowing the 48-hour average to be respected over a longer reference period. The opt-out does not permit any derogation from other provisions, including the minimum rest periods and the limits on [[DefinedTerm/night-work]], which sets a natural ceiling on its use: since the minimum daily and weekly rest periods alone add up to 90 of the 168 hours in a week (six periods of 11 hours plus 24 hours), average working time could not exceed 78 hours a week even before breaks or tighter night work limits are taken into account. The Commission adds that the limits actually required on health and safety grounds may well be below that theoretical maximum, and the competent authorities can intervene to prevent or restrict working beyond 48 hours even for workers who have opted out.
