Sleep debt is the cumulative effect of missing out on the recommended amount of good-quality sleep over several days. In WorkSafe New Zealand's guidance [[Report/managing-the-risks-of-shift-work]], the negative effects of sleep loss build up across consecutive days into a sleep debt, and the greater the debt, the more likely workers are to experience negative effects, up to falling asleep uncontrollably in microsleeps. The guidance treats missing out on enough good-quality sleep as one of the main risks of [[DefinedTerm/shift-work]] and states that fatigue occurs when a sleep debt builds.

## Usage

The guidance describes how sleep debt develops: for the first few days people feel increasingly sleepy and fatigued, with effects on mood, alertness, reaction times, coordination, communication, thinking speed, situational awareness and creative problem-solving. After the first two or three nights of short sleep they feel increasingly sleepy, but with further short nights they may report feeling no sleepier even though their functioning keeps getting worse — which the guidance calls especially dangerous, because people become less able to judge how impaired they are. Recovery requires at least two consecutive nights of unrestricted, good-quality sleep — sleep in which a person goes to bed and rises when they choose and the sleep period falls at night — for sleep structure to return to normal, and it may take more than two such nights before they can function at their best.

The concept shapes the guidance's advice on rostering. Consecutive 12-hour shifts, too many consecutive night shifts, early-morning starts or late finishes can all build a sleep debt, and the number of rest days a worker needs after a run of shifts depends mostly on the sleep debt accumulated. Employers are advised to consider the sleep debt a work pattern will create when planning rosters and rest days ([[DefinedTerm/rest-breaks-at-work]]). For context on how much sleep is enough, the guidance reproduces the National Sleep Foundation's recommendations of 7–9 hours a night for adults aged 18 to 64 and 7–8 hours for those aged 65 and over ([[DefinedTerm/short-sleep-duration]]).
