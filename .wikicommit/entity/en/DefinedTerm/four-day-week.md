A four-day week is a working-time arrangement in which a full-time working week is spread over four working days instead of five. Eurofound's review [[Report/working-time-in-2021-2022]] notes that progress towards a four-day week in the EU in 2021–2022 was "not necessarily associated with a reduction in working time": some arrangements keep weekly hours unchanged and lengthen the working day, while others cut weekly hours, for example to 32.

## Usage

According to the review, the four-day week was the most debated working-time issue in the EU in 2022, discussed in Austria, Ireland, Latvia, Lithuania, Portugal and Spain.

**Four longer working days.** Belgium's Labour Deal of 3 October 2022 gave full-time private-sector employees the right to request a four-day week, with daily working time of up to 9.5 hours where weekly time is 38 hours or less, and employers must justify a refusal. Greek Law 4808/2021 allows, by agreement, a full-time four-day week of 40 hours with up to 10 hours a day, without reducing working time. In Austria several collective agreements already allow a four-day week through works agreements, with normal daily hours of up to 10 when hours are distributed evenly over four days — an option the review notes may not appeal to workers with care duties.

**With shorter weekly hours.** Lithuania amended its Labour Code, from 1 January 2023, to give parents or guardians in state and municipal institutions and enterprises who return early from parental leave a 32-hour week until the child turns three, without reducing pay. Spain launched a pilot for small and medium-sized industrial companies that reduce working time by at least 10%, to study the effect on productivity, conditional on social dialogue; Portugal planned a six-month pilot from June 2023 with companies that reduce weekly hours without cutting wages. In Ireland, a trial run between February and July 2022 in 12 companies employing 188 people — which the review describes as a compressed working week of 32 hours — reported high employer satisfaction and statistically significant reductions in burnout and anxiety and increases in job satisfaction among employees, and was particularly positive for women.

## When It Applies

In several of the national debates the review describes, support came mainly from trade unions or labour organisations and opposition from employer organisations. Portugal's General Confederation of Portuguese Workers rejected any four-day week that would lengthen the working day beyond eight hours, while tourism and business confederations called the debate premature or ill-timed, citing productivity and sectors operating seven days a week; in Latvia, employers in general did not support a 32-hour week. The examples in the review differ in whether weekly hours are kept or cut, in who can request the arrangement and in how it is introduced — through legislation, collective agreements or company pilots.
