"Reasonably practicable" is the standard that qualifies the duties of care in most Australian work health and safety (WHS) law: what can reasonably be done, in the circumstances, to ensure health and safety. Under the model Work Health and Safety Act, a person conducting a business or undertaking (PCBU) must eliminate risks to health and safety so far as is reasonably practicable and, where that is not reasonably practicable, minimise them so far as is reasonably practicable. Deciding what is reasonably practicable means weighing all relevant matters, including the likelihood of the hazard or risk occurring, the degree of harm that might result, what the person knows or ought reasonably to know about the risk and ways of controlling it, and the availability and suitability of those controls. Only after that may the cost of the available controls be considered, including whether it is grossly disproportionate to the risk.

## Usage

The standard has been generally accepted for some time as the qualifier of duties in most Australian jurisdictions and has been consistently interpreted by the courts; what it requires differs from case to case. It has taken on new questions with the spread of [[DefinedTerm/working-from-home]]. The model WHS laws define a workplace as any place where work is carried out, so employers' duties extend to the home, but, as Australia's Productivity Commission observes in [[Report/working-from-home-research-paper]], employers have less visibility and control over home workspaces and are less able to correct risky environments or activities. Safe Work Australia has described control as an implied element in determining what is reasonably practicable, while employees may have concerns about privacy, autonomy and the cost of modifications if employers seek control over parts of their homes. Of Australia's WHS Acts, only South Australia's explicitly includes control as an element of eliminating or minimising risks.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, Safe Work Australia's guidance suggested that employers meet their duties for home-based workers through measures such as guidance on safe workstation set-up, lending office equipment, workstation self-assessments, regular communication and access to mental health support, with inspection of a home workspace (for example by photo or video) needed only where the risks warranted it.

The Productivity Commission judges that much will depend on how case law develops on what is reasonably practicable at home. Whether an employer is liable for an injury sustained at home is likely to turn on the facts of each case, including whether the employee was following a direction from the employer and whether the injury occurred in the course of a normal working day, and existing case law gives examples of how such factors have been interpreted. On who must supply equipment, the Commission argues that the answer may depend on whether employees work partly or wholly from home and whether they chose to work from home or were directed to by their employer, and it notes that one decision on the supply of a desk suggests a common-sense approach. It found little evidence so far that WHS laws were being applied in an unreasonable or unduly costly way.

New Zealand's [[Legislation/health-and-safety-at-work-act-2015]] uses the same qualifier: section 22 defines it, and certain duties of a PCBU, including the primary duty of care, must be carried out so far as is reasonably practicable. WorkSafe New Zealand's guidance [[Report/managing-psychosocial-risks-at-work]] explains it as doing what is reasonable to ensure health and safety in the business's circumstances — what a reasonable person in its position would be expected to know and do — by first trying to eliminate a risk and, if that is not possible, minimising it. It frames the assessment as questions about what is known about the risk (its likelihood, the severity of the harm and what is or should be known about it) and about possible control measures (what is known about them, what is available, how suitable they are and what they cost). Just because something can be done does not make it reasonably practicable, and cost should only be a reason not to act if it is grossly disproportionate to the risk. WorkSafe adds that a common industry practice may not be the most effective or appropriate control for a business's specific circumstances. The guidance applies the standard to [[DefinedTerm/psychosocial-risks]] as well as physical ones.
