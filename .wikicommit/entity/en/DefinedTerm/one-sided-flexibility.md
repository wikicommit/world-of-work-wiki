One-sided flexibility is the situation in which unpredictable scheduling and income risks are shifted onto workers, leaving them unable to plan their finances or personal lives effectively, while employers retain full control over scheduling decisions. The term is used in UK labour policy, where the government frames it as an imbalance that can lead to exploitative practices and inefficient labour-market outcomes. It is associated above all with [[DefinedTerm/zero-hours-contract]]s and other contracts with no or very few guaranteed hours, but the UK government's analysis holds that workers with more guaranteed hours can also experience it when not all of their working hours are contractually guaranteed.

## Usage

In its 2026 options assessment on the [[DefinedTerm/right-to-guaranteed-hours]] ([[Report/right-to-guaranteed-hours-options-assessment]]), the UK government attributes one-sided flexibility to a combination of market failures: employers' market (monopsony) power over workers who are easily substituted or have few alternative employers; negative externalities, as the stress, anxiety and poor mental health caused by unpredictable hours spill over into public health services and welfare support; and moral hazard, since contracts with few set hours let employers pass business risks such as fluctuating demand onto workers. It expects these failures, and the case for intervention, to be strongest where workers have no or few guaranteed hours and to weaken as guaranteed hours increase. For workers with more guaranteed hours, the assessment suggests the main detriments may relate more to work-life conflict and disruption to personal planning than to income volatility.

The same assessment acknowledges that variable-hours arrangements suit some workers — it cites evidence that 72% of workers in insecure work say their arrangements suit their lifestyle — while reporting that 58% would prefer guaranteed hours matching their usual working patterns and that 53% say variability in their hours directly affects their ability to cover living costs (65% of those with dependent children). It links low employee control over work schedules to depressive symptoms, psychological distress, burnout and accumulated fatigue, and argues that contracts without guaranteed hours let poor workforce planning persist.

The UK's [[Legislation/employment-rights-act-2025]] addresses one-sided flexibility through three rights: the right to guaranteed hours, a right to reasonable notice of shifts, and a right to payment for shifts cancelled, moved or curtailed at short notice. The government rejected an outright ban on variable contracts as ill-targeted, since flexibility is genuinely beneficial to both sides in many cases.
