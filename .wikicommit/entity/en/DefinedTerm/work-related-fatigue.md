Work-related fatigue is, in WorkSafe New Zealand's definition, a state of physical and/or mental exhaustion that can reduce a person's ability to perform work safely and effectively. Because it reduces alertness, it may lead to errors and to more workplace incidents and injuries, so WorkSafe treats it as a potential risk that both businesses and workers are responsible for managing under the [[Legislation/health-and-safety-at-work-act-2015]].

## Usage

WorkSafe's *Fatigue* quick guide (2017) identifies several, often inter-related, causes: work schedules — [[DefinedTerm/long-working-hours]], irregular hours and [[DefinedTerm/night-work]] or shift work, which limit time to recover and disrupt the natural sleeping rhythm; sleep disruption, since people generally need 7.5 to 9 hours of sleep a night, ideally deep, undisturbed and continuous; environmental conditions such as climate extremes, noise and vibrating tools; physically demanding work or tasks requiring intense concentration; and emotional strain from work events, such as regular criticism or deadline pressure, or from events outside work. Alertness also varies through the day, with low points for most people between 3 and 5 am and between 3 and 5 pm.

Businesses must eliminate risks arising from their work so far as is [[DefinedTerm/reasonably-practicable]], or minimise them if they cannot be eliminated. The strategies WorkSafe suggests include ensuring regular, good-quality [[DefinedTerm/rest-breaks-at-work]], keeping working hours from becoming too long, avoiding critical tasks at low-alertness times, negotiating, monitoring and limiting [[DefinedTerm/overtime]] and avoiding incentives to work excessive hours, limiting shift swapping and on-call duties, designing rosters that allow for sleep and recovery, avoid starts before 6 am where possible and limit consecutive night shifts, keeping workloads manageable, and adopting a workplace fatigue policy covering maximum shift length, average weekly hours, work-related travel and procedures for reporting fatigue and managing fatigued workers. Workers in turn must take reasonable care of their own and others' health and safety; they should arrive fit for work, learn to recognise the signs of fatigue — such as constant tiredness, yawning or falling asleep at work, poor concentration and decision-making, and slower reaction times — and report fatigue and fatigue-related incidents. WorkSafe's checklist flags, among other things, regularly working more than 12 hours, breaks of less than 10 hours between shifts, work or travel between midnight and 6 am, and schedules that prevent at least one full day off a week, as signs that fatigue risks may need to be assessed.

WorkSafe's guidance [[Report/managing-the-risks-of-shift-work]] describes fatigue as a physiological state in which someone is unable to function mentally and physically at their best, and names four main causes: missing out on sleep, which accumulates as a [[DefinedTerm/sleep-debt]]; being awake for too long, since after about 16 hours awake the ability to function safely starts to decline; working and sleeping in the wrong parts of the circadian body clock cycle ([[DefinedTerm/circadian-disruption]]); and physical and mental workload, made up of task duration, complexity and intensity and how well the person can meet those demands. Low workload can unmask sleepiness through lack of stimulation, while high workload can cause fatigue through effort and disturb later sleep; time-on-task fatigue from working without stopping on difficult or high-concentration tasks is made worse by sleep loss and at night. The guidance stresses that fatigue may not be obvious from outside: besides visible signs such as excessive yawning or falling asleep at work, memory and concentration problems, poorer judgement, slow reflexes, repeated lateness and more sick leave, fatigued workers may experience drowsiness, headaches, dizziness, blurred vision and a need for extended sleep on days off. It sets fatigue management within a continuous risk management cycle for [[DefinedTerm/shift-work]] built on worker engagement, roster design, fatigue reporting, policies and training.

WorkSafe's web guidance *Fatigue – what's the problem?* sets the quick guide's causes in the context of industries that rely on workers being physically and mentally alert, naming the breaks between shifts as part of the work-schedule cause and construction as an example of physically demanding work. Its examples of ways to minimise the risk where it cannot be eliminated lean towards how work is organised and supported: setting achievable demands in relation to agreed hours of work, matching workers' skills and abilities to job demands, giving workers some control over their pace of work ([[DefinedTerm/job-autonomy]]), developing multi-disciplinary teams, making sure managers and supervisors can identify and support workers who may be feeling stressed, providing access to independent counselling, having agreed policies to prevent or resolve unacceptable behaviour, and consulting workers before changes in a way that genuinely lets them influence the decision. Businesses are to choose the most effective controls proportionate to the risk, and the page stresses involving workers — including contractors and temps — in identifying fatigue risks and how to eliminate or minimise them, and training them on the key risks ([[DefinedTerm/employee-voice]]).
