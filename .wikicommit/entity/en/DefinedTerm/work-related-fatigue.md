Work-related fatigue is, in WorkSafe New Zealand's definition, a state of physical and/or mental exhaustion that can reduce a person's ability to perform work safely and effectively. Because it reduces alertness, it may lead to errors and to more workplace incidents and injuries, so WorkSafe treats it as a potential risk that both businesses and workers are responsible for managing under the [[Legislation/health-and-safety-at-work-act-2015]].

## Usage

WorkSafe's *Fatigue* quick guide (2017) identifies several, often inter-related, causes: work schedules — [[DefinedTerm/long-working-hours]], irregular hours and [[DefinedTerm/night-work]] or shift work, which limit time to recover and disrupt the natural sleeping rhythm; sleep disruption, since people generally need 7.5 to 9 hours of sleep a night, ideally deep, undisturbed and continuous; environmental conditions such as climate extremes, noise and vibrating tools; physically demanding work or tasks requiring intense concentration; and emotional strain from work events, such as regular criticism or deadline pressure, or from events outside work. Alertness also varies through the day, with low points for most people between 3 and 5 am and between 3 and 5 pm.

Businesses must eliminate risks arising from their work so far as is [[DefinedTerm/reasonably-practicable]], or minimise them if they cannot be eliminated. The strategies WorkSafe suggests include ensuring regular, good-quality [[DefinedTerm/rest-breaks-at-work]], keeping working hours from becoming too long, avoiding critical tasks at low-alertness times, negotiating, monitoring and limiting [[DefinedTerm/overtime]] and avoiding incentives to work excessive hours, limiting shift swapping and on-call duties, designing rosters that allow for sleep and recovery, avoid starts before 6 am where possible and limit consecutive night shifts, keeping workloads manageable, and adopting a workplace fatigue policy covering maximum shift length, average weekly hours, work-related travel and procedures for reporting fatigue and managing fatigued workers. Workers in turn must take reasonable care of their own and others' health and safety; they should arrive fit for work, learn to recognise the signs of fatigue — such as constant tiredness, yawning or falling asleep at work, poor concentration and decision-making, and slower reaction times — and report fatigue and fatigue-related incidents. WorkSafe's checklist flags, among other things, regularly working more than 12 hours, breaks of less than 10 hours between shifts, work or travel between midnight and 6 am, and schedules that prevent at least one full day off a week, as signs that fatigue risks may need to be assessed.
