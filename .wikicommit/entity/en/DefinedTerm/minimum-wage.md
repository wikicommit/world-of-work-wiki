A national minimum wage, as Eurostat uses the term in its statistics, is a basic wage floor fixed at an hourly, weekly or monthly rate that usually applies to all employees, or at least to a large majority of employees, in a country. It is enforced by law (the government), often after consultation with social partners, or set directly by a national intersectoral agreement. In the European countries that have no national minimum wage, minimum wages are laid down by collective agreements for a range of specific sectors.

## Usage

Eurostat publishes national minimum wages twice a year, reflecting the situation on 1 January and 1 July, so changes made between those dates appear only in the next release. Wages are presented as gross monthly rates, before income tax and the employee's social security contributions are deducted. Where a minimum wage is fixed hourly or weekly it is converted to a monthly figure with factors supplied by the country (for example, hourly rate × 35 hours × 52 weeks ÷ 12 for France); where it is fixed in net terms, as in Montenegro and Serbia, it is grossed up to cover applicable taxes; and where it is paid for 14 months a year, as in Greece, Spain and Portugal, the data are adjusted accordingly. Non-euro amounts are converted at the exchange rate at the end of the previous month, and purchasing power standards (PPS), based on purchasing power parities for household final consumption expenditure, are used to remove differences in price levels.

On 1 July 2026, 22 of the 27 EU countries had a national minimum wage; Denmark, Italy, Austria, Finland and Sweden did not, and neither did the EFTA countries Iceland, Norway and Switzerland. Cyprus introduced its minimum wage on 1 January 2023. Monthly minimum wages ranged from €620 in Bulgaria to €2 771 in Luxembourg, the highest being 4.5 times the lowest; in PPS terms the range narrowed to PPS 935 in Estonia to PPS 2 164 in Germany, a ratio of 2.3. Between July 2016 and July 2026 average annual growth was highest in Lithuania (+11.7%), Romania (+11.6%) and Bulgaria (+11.2%), and lowest in France (+2.4%) and Malta (+3.2%).

Eurostat also relates the minimum wage to the wider wage distribution. Expressed as a share of median gross earnings — the [[DefinedTerm/kaitz-index]] — it ranged in 2024 from 44% in Estonia to 69% in Portugal, and was above 60% in eight EU countries. Using microdata from the [[Dataset/structure-of-earnings-survey]] 2022, Eurostat estimates the share of employees paid less than 105% of the minimum wage (the 5% mark-up allows for differences between the earnings concepts of the two data sources): in 2022 it was above 10% in seven countries, highest in Bulgaria (13.0%), France (12.7%) and Slovenia (12.6%), and lowest in Portugal (3.1%) and Czechia (2.6%).

At EU level, Principle 6 "Wages" of the European Pillar of Social Rights, proclaimed in November 2017, calls for adequate minimum wages and transparent, predictable wage setting in accordance with national practices and respecting the autonomy of social partners. Directive (EU) 2022/2041 on adequate minimum wages, adopted on 19 October 2022, sets a framework for the adequacy of statutory minimum wages, promotes collective bargaining on wage setting and aims to improve workers' effective access to minimum wage protection.
