Maximum weekly working hours is a legal limit on how many hours a person may work in a week, measured as an average over a reference period rather than week by week. In the European Union, Article 6 of the [[Legislation/working-time-directive]] requires Member States to ensure that average working time for each seven-day period, including overtime, does not exceed 48 hours. In the UK, according to the government's GOV.UK guidance, a worker cannot work more than 48 hours a week on average, normally averaged over 17 weeks; the guidance notes that this law is sometimes called the "working time directive" or "working time regulations". Workers under 18 face a stricter limit and cannot work more than 8 hours a day or 40 hours a week.

## Usage

**The EU limit.** The European Commission's [[Report/interpretative-communication-on-the-working-time-directive]] explains how the Court of Justice has interpreted the 48-hour limit. All working time in the Directive's sense ([[DefinedTerm/working-time-and-rest-period]]) counts towards it, including periods that national law may treat differently for pay purposes, such as [[DefinedTerm/overtime]] or on-call time. The limit is a minimum standard that Member States may make more protective, but they cannot attach conditions or restrictions to a worker's right not to work more than 48 hours a week; exceeding the limit is in itself an infringement, without any need to show a specific detriment, and the Court has held that compulsorily transferring a worker for having asked for the limit to be respected deprives the rule of its effectiveness.

The limit can be averaged. Member States may set a reference period of up to four months for calculating the average in any sector. Periods of paid annual leave and sick leave are not included, or are neutral, in the calculation, so absence during them cannot offset weeks above the limit; the Commission considers that Member States remain free to decide whether additional national leave beyond the four-week minimum is included. By derogation, the reference period can be extended to six months by law for certain activities or to twelve months by collective agreement, and the Court has said that it can in no circumstances exceed twelve months. National law may use reference periods that start and end on fixed calendar dates, provided it has mechanisms ensuring that the 48-hour average is also respected over each six-month period straddling two consecutive fixed periods. The Court has recognised Article 6(2), read with the reference-period rules, as having direct effect, so it can be relied on against the State and public employers, though not directly between private parties. The limit itself can be set aside only for "autonomous workers" whose working time is not measured or predetermined, and for individual workers who agree to a [[DefinedTerm/working-time-opt-out]] where a Member State allows it.

**The UK limit.** The UK limit is not absolute. An individual worker can choose to work more by opting out of the 48-hour week, and people in certain jobs may have to work more than 48 hours a week on average: jobs where 24-hour staffing is required; the armed forces, emergency services and police; security and surveillance; domestic service in a private household; seafarers, sea-fishermen and workers on vessels on inland waterways; and jobs where working time is not measured and the worker is in control of it, such as a managing executive with control over their own decisions.

Employers need to keep some records of working hours, and the Advisory, Conciliation and Arbitration Service (Acas) provides further advice on working hours and on the records employers must keep.
