Workplace discrimination is defined, in the literature drawn on by the EU-OSHA report [[Report/workforce-diversity-and-musculoskeletal-disorders]], as the perception that a worker has experienced unfair or negative treatment based on membership of a particular social group. In this view it is the individual's perception that matters, because discrimination can affect outcomes such as health only when the person is aware of a discriminatory action or event. The report links the concept to justice: discriminatory behaviours violate perceptions of justice in the distribution of rewards, in decision-making procedures and in personal interactions.

The report distinguishes two pairs of forms. **Formal discrimination** shows up in job-related decisions such as hiring, promotion and pay, and stems from the way work is organised; **interpersonal discrimination** shows up in negative verbal and non-verbal behaviour in everyday workplace interactions, such as avoidance, refusing eye contact, unwillingness to help or unfriendly communication. Other authors separate **subtle** from **overt** discrimination; subtle forms are harder to detect, address and remedy, and are described as much more pervasive in the modern workplace because strong contemporary norms promote egalitarianism.

## Usage

EU-OSHA counts discrimination, alongside harassment and bullying (see [[DefinedTerm/workplace-harassment]]), among the [[DefinedTerm/psychosocial-risks]] that can lead to anxiety, fatigue, sleep problems and job-related stress, and so to [[DefinedTerm/work-related-musculoskeletal-disorders]]. The report cites evidence that perceived discrimination at work adds job stress, worsens job attitudes and reduces mental and physical health, and that it may harm those who observe it as well as those who experience it.

In the 2015 [[Dataset/european-working-conditions-survey]], 17 % of first-generation migrant workers reported discrimination in the past year, compared with 8 % of second-generation migrants and 6 % of native workers. Because each individual ground of discrimination was reported by fewer than 3.5 % of respondents, the report's regressions used a general indicator of discrimination on any ground, reported by 7 %. It was associated with a higher likelihood of anxiety, fatigue and sleep problems and of back, upper-limb and lower-limb MSDs; the link with MSDs shrank when mental-health indicators were added, which the report reads as support for mediation through mental health. The report also describes discrimination against LGBTI workers in recruitment, employment conditions and promotion, which it connects to "prejudice-based segregation" into jobs where less intolerance is expected (see [[DefinedTerm/occupational-segregation]]).
