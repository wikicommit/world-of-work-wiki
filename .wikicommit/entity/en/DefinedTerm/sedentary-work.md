Sedentary work is work that involves prolonged sitting — sitting without moving or standing up for a long time. The EU-OSHA report [[Report/work-related-msds-prevalence-costs-and-demographics-in-the-eu]] treats sedentary behaviour at work as an occupational risk that needs to be prevented, because it can be hazardous to health (cardiovascular pathologies, cancer, diabetes and so on), and stresses this all the more as sitting at the workplace is increasing. A later EU-OSHA report on prolonged static sitting, [[Report/prolonged-static-sitting-at-work]], defines prolonged sitting as being sedentary for 2 hours or longer at a time, with three main characteristics: low energy expenditure, a seated body posture and static loading — the physical exertion needed to hold the same position.

## Usage

In the 2015 [[Dataset/european-working-conditions-survey]], 60 % of workers said their job involved sitting at least a quarter of the time, and the share working with computers, laptops or smartphones for at least a quarter of the working day rose from 47 % in 2000 to 53 % in 2010 and 58 % in 2015; the two are positively correlated. In the [[Dataset/esener]], 74 % of employees worked in establishments where prolonged sitting was present in 2019. The prolonged-sitting report adds that 28 % of workers in the 2015 survey reported sitting almost all the time — 31 % of women against 25 % of men — and that prolonged sitting was the second most frequently reported risk factor in the 2019 ESENER, named by 61 % of establishments in the EU-27.

That report names office workers as most at risk, along with drivers, pilots, crane operators, sewing machine operators, assembly line workers and staff at service desks, in laboratories, call centres and control rooms; people working from home may work longer without a break in poorer ergonomic conditions. It points out that women are over-represented in predominantly seated, low-grade jobs such as office work and micro-assembly, where they may have little control over when they can get up and move, and that older workers and workers with chronic conditions such as back pain are particularly affected.

In the Netherlands, the CBS article [[Report/sitting-behaviour-on-working-days]] found that in 2024 employees sat on average 4.5 hours per working day while working, 1.1 hours while commuting and 3.3 hours in their free time; only free-time sitting had risen in recent years, slightly and mainly between 2017 and 2022. Sitting at work differed between occupations and was associated with working from home, screen work and working part-time or full-time, while free-time sitting on working days was associated with age. CBS found no pronounced link between sitting at work and sitting in free time on a working day. It frames sitting a lot and moving little as going together with health risks that can bring societal costs.

### Health effects

The prolonged-sitting report, based on a short literature review, links prolonged sitting to low back pain, neck and shoulder complaints, type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease, obesity, certain cancers (in particular breast and colon cancer), mental health issues and premature death, while noting that the cause-effect relationship between sitting and health effects is not fully understood.

Its link with [[DefinedTerm/work-related-musculoskeletal-disorders]] is unclear. The 2019 EU-OSHA report notes that previous research indicates self-reported time spent sitting is positively related to chronic diseases and mortality, but that the evidence linking sitting at work to MSDs is less clear, and that the amount of time spent sitting is very difficult to measure reliably with self-report questionnaires. Short periods of sitting can also be beneficial, relieving the load on the hips and legs after long standing, which suggests a threshold beyond which sitting raises health risks — a threshold that has not yet been established.

The 2019 report found in 2015 survey data that the more time workers sat, the less likely they were to report lower-limb MSDs, with no relationship to back or upper-limb complaints. The authors caution that the survey measured the share of working time spent sitting rather than prolonged sitting, and that it had no data on standing — which in 2010 was the most prevalent physical risk factor, affecting 69 % of workers — so the negative relationship could reflect an unmeasured effect of standing. They call for more research, while recommending that sedentary behaviour at work be prevented regardless of its specific relationship to MSDs.

### Prevention

The prolonged-sitting report recommends spending 50 % or less of the working day sitting, getting up at least every 20–30 minutes, getting up for at least 10 minutes after 2 hours of sitting and not exceeding 5 hours of sitting at work a day, and treats 2 hours as the maximum for continuous sitting. It replaces the ideal of an "as upright as possible" posture with "dynamic sitting", in which sitting positions are continuously altered, and argues that the opposite of sitting is moving rather than standing, so prolonged sitting should not simply be replaced with prolonged standing. It frames prevention as a systematic strategy combining ergonomic workstations, work organisation that limits sitting (task rotation, minibreaks, control over breaks — see [[DefinedTerm/rest-breaks-at-work]]), a culture that encourages movement, worker participation and health promotion.
