Cross-border telework refers to situations in which workers use information and communication technologies to work while residing in a different Member State from their employer. The European Commission notes that it became more common as telework rose during the COVID-19 pandemic, and that it covers diverse arrangements: occasional cross-border telework (for example because of temporary care responsibilities, to optimise holiday schedules or to work from a second residence for a limited time), workers living in another country who regularly alternate between the office and home-based work in their country of residence, and people who work from multiple locations, including "digital nomads", who are often self-employed or freelance.

## Usage

According to the Commission's 2024 consultation document [[Report/first-phase-consultation-on-telework-and-the-right-to-disconnect]], there is no specific legal status for workers who work remotely from another Member State, and the application of EU rules on free movement and cross-border work to telework can be challenging. For social security, the rules on people working in two or more Member States apply: under Article 13 of Regulation (EC) No 883/2004 a worker is subject to the legislation of their Member State of residence if they pursue a "substantial part" of their activity there, and under the implementing Regulation (EC) No 987/2009 at least 25% of working time or remuneration over 12 months indicates a substantial part. A Framework Agreement concluded by interested countries on the application of Article 16(1) of Regulation 883/2004 to habitual cross-border telework, in force since 1 July 2023, raises that threshold so that workers can telework less than 50% of their total working time in their state of residence without changing their social security affiliation; the document lists 20 signatory countries. A non-binding guidance note on telework from the Administrative Commission for the Coordination of Social Security Systems has applied from the same date.

For employment contracts, the Rome I Regulation provides that, absent a choice of law, the applicable law is that of the Member State where the employee usually works, and the Brussels I Regulation determines jurisdiction. No EU-wide rules regulate the taxation of people who work in several countries, beyond bilateral double taxation treaties. The Commission notes that workers, employers and public administrations can find it difficult to determine rights and obligations on social security, taxes and labour law, and that cross-border telework also raises organisational questions on insurance and responsibility for health and safety.
