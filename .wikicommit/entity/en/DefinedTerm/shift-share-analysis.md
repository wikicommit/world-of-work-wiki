Shift-share analysis is a technique that aims to break down the total change in an economic indicator into various components, to identify the underlying sources of growth or decline. Applied to average hours worked, it separates two kinds of contribution. **Shift effects** are the weighted contribution of a particular employment group: the change in that group's own average hours, weighted by its share in total employment. **Share effects** capture compositional change: even if every group's hours stay the same, average hours overall will fall if groups that work fewer hours make up a growing share of employment.

## Usage

The UK Office for National Statistics used the technique in [[Report/average-hours-worked-and-economic-growth-uk-1998-to-2022]] to account for the 1.3-hour fall in average weekly hours worked between 1998 and 2022, and the 0.3-hour fall since 2019, using two breakdowns of employment. Broken down by sex and labour market status (full-time, part-time and second jobs), nearly all of the 1998–2022 fall came from a shift effect — shorter hours among men working full-time (negative 1.2 hours) — while the share effect (negative 0.3 hours) mainly reflected fewer men and more women working full-time; between 2019 and 2022 that share effect was positive. Broken down by sex and age, the share effect accounted for over half of the 1998–2022 fall (negative 0.7 hours), reflecting the growing proportions of women and older people in employment, both groups that on average work fewer hours.

## When It Applies

The result depends on how employment is broken down: in the ONS analysis the same fall in average hours is attributed mostly to a shift effect in one breakdown and more than half to a share effect in another. The decomposition separates contributions but does not by itself explain them; in that analysis, explanations such as greater participation in further and higher education or the rising state pension age for women are offered as possibilities ("probably", "perhaps").
