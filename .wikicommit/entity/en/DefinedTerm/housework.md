Housework is the range of unpaid chores geared toward maintaining household members, their home and the property on which it sits, and their vehicles. In the General Social Survey on Time Use (the [[Dataset/canadian-time-use-survey]]), as used in [[Report/time-use-total-work-burden-unpaid-work-and-leisure]], it covers all unpaid household activities except caregiving: preparing meals and snacks and preserving food; indoor cleaning, dish washing and tidying; taking out garbage and recycling; laundry, ironing and sewing; repair, painting and renovation; organizing, planning and paying bills; unpacking groceries and packing for travel or a move; outdoor maintenance such as car repair, snow removal or cutting grass; gardening and caring for house plants; pet care; and shopping for and researching goods and services. Together with caregiving it makes up [[DefinedTerm/unpaid-work]].

## Usage

Housework is a standard category in analyses of the gendered division of labour. [[Report/time-use-total-work-burden-unpaid-work-and-leisure]] describes three theoretical explanations from earlier research for why women spend more time on it than men. The gender perspective treats the gendered division of housework as a repeated expression of "appropriate" gender roles. The time availability perspective holds that housework is allocated rationally according to partners' relative paid-work hours and how much housework needs doing. The relative resources perspective holds that the partner with more education and income relative to the other has more power to avoid it. Gender specialization also contributes to the gap: research cited in the report finds that women tend to do routine, repetitive tasks such as cooking, cleaning, laundry and shopping, and men more episodic or discretionary ones such as taking out the garbage, house and car repairs, mowing the lawn and gardening.

In Canada, women aged 25 to 54 spent an average of 2.8 hours a day on housework as a primary activity in 2015 and men 1.9 hours; 89.9% of women and 76.2% of men did some. Between 1986 and 2015 women's average time fell from 3.5 to 2.8 hours a day while men's rose from 1.5 to 1.9 hours. Consistent with the time availability and relative resources perspectives, women employed full-time spent 2.5 hours a day on housework, against 3.0 hours for women employed part-time and 3.9 hours for women not employed; women with a university degree and women in the top quarter of the income distribution also spent less time on it. Men's housework time did not vary significantly with their paid-work hours and was similar across the income distribution. Counting housework done at the same time as another activity (see [[DefinedTerm/simultaneous-activity]]) widened the gap, to 3.4 hours a day for women against 2.0 for men in 2010.
