Informal employment (labour informality) is work that takes place outside the social security system and the protections that come with formal employment. Sources measure it in different ways. In Argentine labour statistics, an employee is informal when the employer does not register them with social security, and an independent worker is informal when they make no contribution to a pension scheme. In [[Report/world-development-report-2019]], based on household and labour force survey data, a person is identified as an informal worker if they have no employment contract, social security or health insurance and are not a member of a labour union. Both sources associate informality with low incomes and with a lack of protection: Argentina's national survey of working conditions links it to poverty and to precarious access to labour rights such as paid holidays, health and unemployment insurance, disability pensions and a future retirement pension, and the World Development Report describes informal workers as having neither health insurance nor social protection and as earning less and being less productive than formal workers.

## Usage

### In Argentine labour statistics

In the [[Dataset/ecetss]], an employee counts as informal when the employer makes no pension deduction from their pay, the same operational definition Argentina's Permanent Household Survey uses to calculate the rate of unregistered employment. For independent workers, informality is determined by the absence of any pension payment under any of its forms (the simplified *monotributo* or social *monotributo*, the self-employed scheme, or a provincial or professional fund). A productive unit is in turn classed as formal when at least one of its employees is registered, and informal when none is; an unregistered employee working in an informal unit is described as being in "double informality", which further reduces their prospects of improving their income and social security coverage. Because informality affects workers' access to protection, the survey treats it as a dimension of workers' health and safety as well as of employment.

On this basis the 2018 survey found informality among 42.8% of workers in urban Argentina, with very different levels by category: 28.5% of employees in productive units, 76.8% of domestic workers in private homes, 68.3% of own-account workers and 23% of employers. Among employees it fell as education and establishment size rose and was highest among young people, and unregistered employees had low coverage of rights such as health insurance (*obra social*), the year-end bonus, paid holidays and accident insurance; the survey notes that lacking cover by an occupational risk insurer (ART) also means lacking the prevention and health and safety care that such cover involves. The survey authors stress that the category is highly heterogeneous, spanning situations as different as professionals and street vendors. Detailed results are in [[Report/ecetss-2018]].

### In the World Development Report 2019

The World Bank's report treats persistent informality as the greatest challenge for emerging economies, irrespective of technological progress. About two out of three workers in emerging economies are informal, and the share reaches 90 percent in some; informal employment stands at more than 70 percent in Sub-Saharan Africa, 60 percent in South Asia and more than 50 percent in Latin America. It has proved remarkably stable: it has stayed at about 75 percent in Peru over the last 30 years and at around 90 percent in India despite fast economic growth, and it has remained high over two decades despite improvements in the business regulatory environment. In emerging economies, informal workers are on average only 15 percent as productive as formal workers, and they gain less from experience: the earnings increase for an additional year of work averages 1.4 percent for informal wage workers against 1.8 percent for formal wage workers (see [[DefinedTerm/returns-to-work-experience]]).

The report argues that technology is blurring the divide between formal and informal work, so that the challenges faced by short-term or temporary workers in advanced economies are mostly the same as those faced by informal workers, and it questions the fit of social insurance financed by contributions from formal wage employment, which excludes informal workers. Among measures that have reduced informality it cites Mexico's Rapid Business Opening System, introduced in 2002, which cut the average time to register a business from 30 days to 2, after which informal business owners similar in profile to formal wage workers were 25 percent more likely to move into formal employment; Brazil's Individual Micro-Entrepreneur Program of 2009, which reduced taxes and red tape for entrepreneurs employing at most one person; Peru's e-Payroll, a factor in the fall of nonagricultural informal employment from 75 percent in 2004 to 68 percent in 2012; and skills training for young people, which raised the probability of securing a formal job in the Dominican Republic.

### In platform work

The International Labour Office's report [[Report/realizing-decent-work-in-the-platform-economy-2024]] (2024) considers three types of informality — of activities, of economic units and of jobs — and their potential negative impacts on the resources collected by the State, on workers' access to labour protection and social security, and on competition among enterprises. It argues that the relatively high proportion of self-employed workers on platforms may contribute to greater informality than in non-platform work, since globally 86.7 per cent of self-employed workers are estimated to be in informal employment against 39.8 per cent of employees. Disguised self-employment, undeclared work, the lack of tax frameworks capable of imposing declaratory obligations on platforms and workers, unintentional non-compliance with contribution and tax obligations, and the risk that platform earnings from a secondary job go undeclared add to the risk.

The report acknowledges that quantified evidence is limited, but finds that results, especially from countries with relatively low overall informality, tend to point to high levels among platform workers. In France, just 15 per cent of platform workers said they planned to report the previous year's income from platform work; in the United Kingdom, only 35 per cent had notified or intended to notify the tax authority, and 54 per cent did not intend to report their earnings. Some 76 per cent of platform workers in Ukraine and two thirds in Serbia reported working informally, without registering as self-employed or paying social security contributions. In Italy, where between 11 and 13 per cent of all workers were estimated to be in informal employment in recent years, almost 40 per cent of platform workers had no formal agreement, and 44 per cent of digital platforms operating in the country were not registered with the national social security institute. All 138 passenger transport platform workers surveyed in Lagos, Nigeria were informal self-employed workers, and in Chile, National Labour Survey data show informal employment among workers on digital platforms falling from 93.5 per cent in the first quarter of 2020 to around 75 per cent in September 2023.

At the same time, the report sees the traceability of platform activity — digital contracts and digitized transactions — as an opportunity to formalize activities that are frequently hidden from the authorities, using data held by platforms, complaint-reporting tools and banks, provided platforms and the competent national authorities collaborate and share information. It also records that some participants in the 2022 Meeting of Experts on Decent Work in the Platform Economy noted the potential for well-regulated platform work to provide pathways to formalization, and the relevance in this respect of the Transition from the Informal to the Formal Economy Recommendation, 2015 (No. 204), which applies to all workers and economic units in the informal economy, including own-account workers.

### In Latin America and the Caribbean

The ILO's regional formalisation strategy for 2024–2030, [[Report/strategy-for-the-promotion-of-formalization-in-latin-america-and-the-caribbean-2024-2030]] (FORLAC 2.0), starts from the fact that half of all working people in Latin America and the Caribbean work in the informal economy. It describes the problem as structural, multidimensional and persistent, mainly affecting women, youth, people belonging to certain ethnic groups and migrants, and falling more heavily on the less educated, low value-added agriculture, the working poor, smaller enterprises, the self-employed and people in new forms of employment; informality increased during the recovery from the pandemic. Following Recommendation No. 204, the ILO treats high informality as an obstacle to guaranteeing workers' rights, to the development of sustainable enterprises and to the financial sustainability of the State, and it relaunches a regional programme of knowledge generation, technical assistance, training and social dialogue first run by its Regional Office in 2013–2017.
