A digital labour platform is an entity that uses digital technologies and automated decision-making to organize or facilitate work that people perform for pay. The draft Convention concerning decent work in the platform economy, set out in the International Labour Office's report [[Report/decent-work-in-the-platform-economy]], defines it as "a legal person or, where applicable under national law, natural person that, through digital technologies, using automated decision-making systems: (i) organizes and/or facilitates work performed by persons for remuneration or payment, for the provision of service, upon request of the recipient or requestor; (ii) regardless of whether that work is performed online or in a specific geographic location". The definition reproduces point 3(a) of the Conclusions agreed at the International Labour Conference's first discussion in 2025, and the revised draft in the Office's fourth report, [[Report/realizing-decent-work-in-the-platform-economy-2026]], keeps it unchanged.

## Usage

The definition determines which platforms the proposed instruments would cover, since the draft Convention applies to all digital labour platforms. In the third report, the Office pointed to the ambiguity of "organizes and/or facilitates work". Read as "or", facilitating work alone would suffice, bringing within scope a wide range of digital platforms — including social media, online advertising and search engines, which usually fall outside the regulation of platform work — as well as private employment agencies that use automated decision-making systems, raising questions about the link with the Private Employment Agencies Convention, 1997 (No. 181). Read as "and", both conditions would be needed, excluding platforms that merely offer a matching function. Some Committee members argued that coverage should be limited to platforms that organize, assign or manage work, not those that merely publish online advertising such as online job billboards. The Office noted that this would be the first time "and/or" is used in a definition in a Convention and invited constituents' views on it.

The replies, summarized in the fourth report, were divided. A number of governments preferred "organizes and facilitates"; others wanted to keep "and/or" or not to reopen agreed text. A majority of employers' organizations opposed reopening the definition, arguing that "organize" implies an employment relationship in some jurisdictions and that most platform workers are independent, and said that if it were reopened they would prefer "facilitate" alone. A majority of workers' organizations considered "and/or" a source of legal uncertainty and wanted the definition to cover only platforms that organize work — that is, that play an active role in structuring and governing it rather than simply offering a matching function — while being open to "organizes and facilitates". The Office explained that, legally, "and/or" is equivalent to "or", so a platform that either organizes or facilitates work falls within the definition. It left the text unamended on the understanding that respondents intend a broad definition covering digital platforms that employ or engage workers, but not one so broad as to include platforms that merely offer intermediation spaces connecting clients and workers, such as social networks, online advertising, search engines or job boards. Some governments and a majority of workers' organizations also considered that the Convention should not cover platforms whose main purpose is to exploit or share assets, or through which goods are sold.

**Earlier formulations.** The questionnaire appended to the Office's first report on the standard-setting item, [[Report/realizing-decent-work-in-the-platform-economy-2024]] (ILC.113/Report V(1), 2024), asked governments whether the term should mean "a natural or legal person that provides, through digital tools such as a website or an application, a service involving the performance of work by a person for remuneration, irrespective of whether that work is performed online (online digital labour platforms) or in a specific geographic location (location-based digital labour platforms)". For its own analysis, that report adopted a description of a digital platform from a measurement handbook published jointly by the OECD, the ILO and Eurostat — "a 'digital interface' or an 'online service provider' … positioned between the providers of the services or goods and their clients" — and concentrated on platforms that connect businesses and clients to workers, either by acting as an intermediating entity or by directly engaging workers to provide particular services, leaving aside online intermediaries in the sale of goods and platforms that coordinate the temporary use of physical assets.

**Location-based and online platforms.** The same report distinguishes location-based platforms, where services are provided in a specific location by individuals (for example deliveries, passenger transport or domestic work), from online platforms, where workers provide services remotely (for example software programming, translation or content moderation); online platforms can be further divided into crowdwork (or microwork), freelance and competitive programming platforms, with some researchers adding content creation. Using the Crunchbase database, it estimates that the number of platforms connecting businesses and clients to workers grew from 193 in 2010 to 1,070 in 2023: 357 online platforms, 334 in delivery, 121 in care work, 119 in individual passenger transport, 117 in domestic work and 22 hybrid platforms providing various services. Because the database is self-reporting and covered 98 countries, some active platforms, particularly in low-income countries, may not be listed. Investment is concentrated: Asia, North America and Europe account for just over 96 per cent of total investment in these platforms, and over 60 per cent of online platforms have their headquarters in the European Union or the United States.

**National legal definitions.** Reviewing national legislation, the report finds that the term most commonly used in Europe is "digital platform" (France, Greece, Italy and Portugal) or "digital labour platform" (Croatia and Malta), while laws elsewhere speak of a "digital service platform company" (Chile), a "labour platform operator" (Republic of Korea), an "internet platform" (Kazakhstan), an "aggregator" (India) or a "transportation network company" (US State of Washington). Despite the range of terms, the definitions share common elements: several describe the platform as an entity that connects clients or users with service providers (Washington State, India, Greece, France); several as a provider of services (Croatia, Kazakhstan, Portugal); and Belgium, Italy, Malta and Portugal refer to the control the platform exercises over the organization of work or the way services are provided — Belgium's law, for example, covers a supplier who, "by means of an algorithm or any other equivalent method or technology, is able to exercise decision-making or control over the manner in which the services are to be provided". Some laws exclude particular activities: Chile excludes companies that merely publish advertisements for services or for the sale or lease of property, Croatia excludes platforms whose primary purpose is to share resources or resell goods or services, and Belgium excludes those whose main purpose is to exploit or share assets or resell goods or services, or that provide a service on a not-for-profit basis. Some legislation also reaches intermediaries: Malta's regulations extend to workers placed at a platform's disposal by a work agency, Croatia's revised Labour Act includes subcontractors (termed "aggregators") as an employer of platform workers, and Portugal's 2023 amendments clarify that platform workers can be hired by intermediaries. The report concludes that legislative definitions focus on the execution of work through or on a platform, and that the intermediating role of technology, by means of an application or online programme, differentiates platform work from work undertaken by individuals for digital platform companies.
