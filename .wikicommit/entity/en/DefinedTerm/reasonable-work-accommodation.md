A reasonable work accommodation is a change to work made to accommodate an individual worker's needs, with the aim of fostering sustainable participation in work by providing favourable, adapted working conditions. In the *WHO guidelines on mental health at work*, such accommodations are organizational interventions focused on an individual, and WHO strongly recommends that they be implemented for workers with mental health conditions, including psychosocial disabilities, in line with international human rights principles — a recommendation made despite very low-certainty evidence and critically influenced by Article 27 of the United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, which calls for reasonable accommodation to be provided to persons with disabilities in the workplace.

## Usage

The review underlying WHO's recommendation identified four types of accommodation: communication accommodations, such as regular supportive meetings with supervisors or receiving information in a preferred written or verbal form; scheduling accommodations, such as frequent breaks or extra time to complete tasks; job description accommodations, such as gradual reintroduction to tasks or job- or task-sharing; and physical environment accommodations, such as access to private space for rest or to refrigeration for medication storage.

Observational studies point to positive associations between accommodations, length of employment and improved mental health; workers receiving accommodations were reported to have job tenures 7–24 months longer than those who did not. Accommodations can apply to workers returning from absence and to those who have not been absent or are newly joining work, and should be person-centred, according to the worker's needs and preferences.

## When It Applies

WHO notes that workers with mental health conditions are, in most countries, legally entitled to reasonable accommodations under disability, equality or discrimination law. The main barrier is disclosure: stigma and fear of repercussions keep workers from disclosing their mental health status and so from accessing accommodations, and accommodations were often not implemented to the level workers needed — in one study only 30.5% of workers received all the accommodations they required. WHO therefore stresses organizational measures against stigma, protecting the worker's privacy (accommodations can be implemented without identifying the worker to colleagues), training and resources for managers, and guidance from public services for small enterprises and lower-resourced settings. Undesirable effects reported in one qualitative study included workers feeling overprotected, patronised and under-challenged. The evidence base is limited: no direct evidence was found, all studies in the review were from high-income countries, chiefly the USA, and there was insufficient evidence to say whether any one type of accommodation is superior.
