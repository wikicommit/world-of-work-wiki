Low pay, as measured by the UK Office for National Statistics (ONS) in its job quality analysis, describes an employee job whose hourly pay is below two-thirds of median hourly pay. The ONS applies the threshold in two ways: against the median hourly pay of the UK as a whole, and against the median hourly pay of all employees living in the same subnational authority (unitary authority or county) as the employee. In 2018 two-thirds of the UK median was £7.90 an hour, according to the Labour Force Survey, compared with a National Minimum Wage for those aged 25 and over of £7.50 until March 2018 and £7.83 from April 2018.

## Usage

In [[Report/job-quality-indicators-in-the-uk-hours-pay-and-contracts-2018]], not being in low pay is one of three components of the ONS's composite measure of [[DefinedTerm/job-quality]], alongside satisfactory hours and a desired contract. The choice of threshold changes the picture at local level. Against the UK median, 7% of UK employees were in low pay in 2018, and employees living in the Greater London Authority were the least likely to be (3%). Against local medians, areas with a wider earnings distribution show more people in low pay, so employees living in England were more likely to be in low pay (14%) than those in Scotland, Wales or Northern Ireland, and the Greater London Authority became one of three city regions with significantly more employees in low pay.

## When It Applies

The ONS presents the local measure as a way for combined authorities, devolved administrations and others to take account of local relative costs, since household spending differs by region of residence; it describes it as imperfect. It notes the measure's flip side: in an area where many employees earn the National Minimum Wage, two-thirds of local median pay could fall below the minimum wage, so the local definition could show nobody in low pay. It also cautions that hourly pay from a single job is often not the same as household income, which can include several income sources.
