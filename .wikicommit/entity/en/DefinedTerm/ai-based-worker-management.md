AI-based worker management (AIWM) is an umbrella term, used in EU-OSHA research, for a worker management system that gathers data — often in real time — on the workspace, the workers, the work they do and the (digital) tools they use, and feeds it into an AI-based model that makes automated or semi-automated decisions, or provides information to decision-makers, on worker management questions. Such decisions and recommendations may include setting shifts, allocating tasks, evaluating performance, monitoring workers' activities and recommending how to prevent health risks. The report [[Report/artificial-intelligence-for-worker-management-an-overview]] treats [[DefinedTerm/algorithmic-management]] as falling under this umbrella, characterised in the same way by the use of algorithms to allocate, monitor and evaluate work and workers' behaviour and performance and by the (semi-)automatic implementation of decisions.

## Usage

**Control and support.** The report frames worker management as combining control and support mechanisms, which are not mutually exclusive. Drawing on the academic literature, it describes three control mechanisms — direction, evaluation and discipline — split into six sub-mechanisms, the so-called "6Rs" model, each of which can be automated or semi-automated: direction through recommending and restricting, evaluation through recording and rating, and discipline through replacing and rewarding. Support mechanisms include tools that improve communication and cooperation between workers, or that aim to prevent conflict, bullying and favouritism, for example through emotional distress identification.

**Goals.** According to the report, organisations implement AIWM to increase efficiency and productivity (automated scheduling and task allocation, gamification, real-time direction), to improve decision-making through people analytics and prediction models (for instance predicting which staff are likely to leave), and to improve workers' health, safety and well-being, for example by monitoring biometric signs of fatigue or offering mental health chatbots that estimate the probability of psychosocial issues from communication patterns. It notes that automated scheduling can also benefit workers by letting them change shifts without contacting a manager.

**Uptake.** The report finds uptake hard to measure, as there is no single database, organisations may not know whether the tools they buy or rent are AI-based, and some are unwilling to discuss their use. Uptake is described as higher in manual sectors with routine tasks, among blue-collar and lower-skilled white-collar workers such as call-centre staff, and in larger organisations; ESENER-3 data show around 23% of manufacturing businesses using machines, systems or computers to monitor workers' performance, against around 14% in information and communication and 11% in financial and insurance activities.

**Risks.** The report identifies risks of dehumanisation and the "datafication" of workers, manipulative nudging based on personal data, lack of transparency about whether and how AIWM is used, intrusive monitoring that blurs work–life balance and threatens privacy, a faster pace of work and performance pressure that can lead to [[DefinedTerm/work-related-stress]], physical harm and accidents, and the amplification of biases present in training data. It argues that, if built and implemented in a trustworthy, transparent and participatory way with minimal data collection, AIWM may also offer opportunities to improve OSH.
