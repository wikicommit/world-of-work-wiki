The unadjusted gender pay gap (GPG) is the difference between the average gross hourly earnings of male and female employees, expressed as a percentage of the average gross hourly earnings of male employees. Eurostat calculates it for enterprises with 10 or more employees and uses it to monitor imbalances in earnings between men and women. "Unadjusted" means that it is computed without correcting for differences in the individual and job characteristics of employed men and women, so a negative value simply means that women's average gross hourly earnings are higher than men's.

## Usage

In the EU, women's gross hourly earnings were on average 11.1% below men's in 2024 (11.4% in the euro area), for the economy as a whole defined as industry, construction and services except public administration, defence and compulsory social security. The gap varied by 19.6 percentage points between countries, from -0.8% in Luxembourg — the only EU country where men earned less than women on average — to 18.8% in Estonia.

Eurostat breaks the indicator down further where national data allow:

- **Working time** — in 2024 the gap for part-time workers ([[DefinedTerm/part-time-employment]]) ranged from -8.3% in Bulgaria to 23.8% in Slovenia, and for full-time workers from -8.1% in Belgium to 19.9% in Latvia. Eurostat cautions that negative gaps should be read against the characteristics of employees: women who take part in the labour market may have comparatively higher skills and education than men.
- **Age** — the gap is generally much lower for new entrants to the labour market and tends to widen with age, which may reflect career interruptions that women experience more often than men during their working life.
- **Economic activity** — in the countries with data, the gap in financial and insurance activities was higher than in the business economy as a whole everywhere except Spain, ranging in 2024 from 12.2% in Spain to 40.3% in Hungary. Many countries recorded negative gaps in water supply and waste management (13 countries) and construction (14 countries).
- **Public versus private sector** — every EU country with data except Slovenia had a larger gap in the private sector than in the public sector in 2024, possibly because public-sector pay in most countries follows transparent wage grids that apply equally to men and women.

The figures come from the [[Dataset/structure-of-earnings-survey]]: from reference year 2006 the indicator follows that survey's methodology, with Eurostat computing the gap directly for survey years and Member States supplying estimates benchmarked on the survey for the years in between.

Because it is unadjusted, the gender pay gap is a broader concept than pay discrimination in the sense of "equal pay for work of equal value". Part of the difference can be explained by differences in the average characteristics of male and female employees, and part by differences in the financial returns to the same characteristics. Reducing the gap is a priority of EU gender-equality policy, and the Pay Transparency Directive (Directive (EU) 2023/970), being transposed into national law, aims to strengthen the principle of equal pay for equal work or work of equal value between men and women.

**France.** Insee measures the gap on annual net pay, distinguishing the effect of working time from that of full-time-equivalent salaries. Its study [[Report/pay-gap-between-women-and-men-in-2023]] found that in France's private sector in 2023 women's average annual salary income was 22.2% below men's, partly because their annual volume of work was 9.3% lower, while at equal working time their average full-time-equivalent net salary was 14.2% lower — and 3.8% lower for the same job in the same establishment. The salary income gap has narrowed by a third since 1995, faster since 2019. Insee links the remaining gap to age (the full-time-equivalent gap rises from 4.3% among under-25s to 24.9% at 60 or over), to [[DefinedTerm/occupational-segregation]], to women's under-representation among the best-paid 1% of employees, without whom the gap falls from 13.5% to 9.7%, and to parenthood: in 2022 mothers' salary income was 29.9% below fathers', and the full-time-equivalent gap rose from 5.8% among employees without children to 28.2% between mothers and fathers of three or more.
