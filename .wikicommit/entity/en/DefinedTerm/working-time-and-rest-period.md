In EU law, working time and rest period are the two categories into which the [[Legislation/working-time-directive]] divides all of a worker's time. Article 2 defines "working time" as any period during which the worker is working, at the employer's disposal and carrying out their activity or duties, in accordance with national laws and/or practice, and a "rest period" as any period which is not working time. The Court of Justice of the European Union has held that the two are mutually exclusive, with no intermediate category between them, and that they are autonomous concepts of EU law to be defined by objective characteristics by reference to the Directive's aim of improving workers' living and working conditions — not by the legislation of each Member State, which cannot unilaterally determine their scope and may not derogate from them. The definitions determine what counts towards every limit in the Directive, including the [[DefinedTerm/maximum-weekly-working-hours]] and the minimum [[DefinedTerm/rest-breaks-at-work]].

## Usage

The European Commission's [[Report/interpretative-communication-on-the-working-time-directive]] draws from the wording of Article 2, the Court's case-law and the Directive's legislative history three cumulative criteria for working time:

- *The worker is working.* The Commission reads this as a spatial criterion — being at the workplace or at a place determined by the employer — noting that several language versions of the Directive say the worker is "at work" rather than "working", and that the Court has repeatedly treated the obligation to be physically present at a place determined by the employer as decisive. That place need not be the usual workplace.
- *The worker is at the employer's disposal.* The decisive factor is that the worker is available to provide services immediately if needed, being legally obliged to follow the employer's instructions; where workers can manage their time without major constraints and pursue their own interests, that points against working time.
- *The worker is carrying out their activity or duties.* The intensity of the work and any interruptions in it are irrelevant: being obliged to be present and available at the workplace to provide services is carrying out one's duties, even if the activity actually performed varies, and output is not a defining characteristic of working time.

National courts deciding whether a period is working time must look at the conditions that apply in practice, not only at national law, and a period that does not meet the criteria is a rest period. The Directive does not govern how either is paid.

The Court has applied these criteria to specific kinds of time. [[DefinedTerm/on-call-and-stand-by-time]] at a place determined by the employer away from home is working time in its entirety, while stand-by during which the worker can choose where to be depends on how far the employer's constraints restrict the use of that time; a break during which a worker must be ready to respond within two minutes has been held to be working time. For workers without a fixed or habitual place of work, such as technicians travelling to customers assigned by their employer, the Court held in 2015 (C-266/14, Tyco) that time spent travelling between home and the first and last customers of the day is working time, since the journeys are necessary to provide the services, the workers follow the employer's instructions and cannot use the time freely, and their place of work cannot be reduced to their customers' premises. Vocational training that the employer requires, even when it takes place away from the usual workplace and wholly or partly outside normal hours, is also working time.

Where the Court has not ruled, the Commission gives its own view. It considers that journeys between appointments during the working day, and irregular journeys that workers make at the employer's request directly to an appointment or to a workplace other than their usual one, would qualify as working time on the same reasoning. By contrast, it sees no indication that daily travel between home and a fixed place of work is working time, since workers with a fixed workplace can determine the distance they live from it and use the journey freely.
