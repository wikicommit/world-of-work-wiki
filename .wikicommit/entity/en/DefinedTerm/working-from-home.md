Working from home is an arrangement in which someone works either part or all of their regular work time in their primary place of residence, as defined by Australia's Productivity Commission, which adapted definitions from the International Labour Organization and from academic research. In doing so, workers reduce their time spent commuting and may gain access to more flexible working hours, and they typically rely on telecommunications technology to engage with co-workers who may themselves be working remotely or in a central workplace. Under this definition, someone who works their standard hours in a central workplace and then does extra overtime at home is not working from home, and nor is someone doing [[DefinedTerm/unpaid-work]] in the home, such as housework, caring or volunteering.

## Usage

Several overlapping terms are used for the same idea, including remote working, teleworking and telecommuting. The Productivity Commission's research paper [[Report/working-from-home-research-paper]] uses the definition above to measure how common the arrangement was in Australia and how it changed during the COVID-19 pandemic. Before the pandemic, about 8% of Australian employees had a formal work-from-home arrangement and around 2% of total hours were worked from home; people who did so were more likely to be women, older, part-time workers, carers and residents of regional or remote areas. The share of people working from home at least some of the time rose to around 40% during the pandemic.

The Commission distinguishes several models by how much work is done at home: fully remote work, fully centralised (office-based) work, and [[DefinedTerm/hybrid-work]], which combines the two and includes "work-from-anywhere" policies. The main benefit reported by workers is the avoided commute — in 2019, full-time workers in Australia's major cities spent an average of about 67 minutes a day commuting — followed by a more flexible schedule. Reported costs include fewer opportunities for collaboration, networking and face-to-face contact with managers, possible effects on career progression, reduced incidental exercise, and a blurring of the boundary between work and home that can make it harder to switch off (see [[DefinedTerm/work-life-balance]] and [[DefinedTerm/right-to-disconnect]]).

Whether a given job can be done from home depends mainly on its tasks; this is estimated as the [[DefinedTerm/potential-to-work-from-home]]. In Australia, employer duties under work health and safety law apply wherever work is carried out, including in the home, qualified by what is [[DefinedTerm/reasonably-practicable]].

French law frames the arrangement more broadly, as telework (*télétravail*). According to the French government's Service Public guidance for the private sector, telework is a form of work organisation in which work that could have been done on the employer's premises is done by an employee away from them, using information and communication technologies; the employee must be a volunteer, and a teleworker is not the same as a homeworker (*travailleur à domicile*). Telework may take place at home, in a telecentre or shared office, or — for employees who travel a lot — anywhere else permitted by a collective agreement, an employer charter or the employer. It may be regular (for example one or two days a week) or occasional (a few days or weeks a year).

Telework is set up by a collective agreement or an employer charter, which must cover matters such as the conditions for moving to and returning from telework, how working time or workload is monitored, the hours during which the employer may usually contact the teleworker, and access to telework for disabled workers, pregnant employees and carers; failing either, employer and employee may agree on it at any time, preferably in writing. In companies with more than 50 employees, the social and economic committee (CSE), where one exists, must be consulted first. An employee may refuse telework, and refusal is not grounds for ending the employment contract, but in exceptional circumstances such as a threatened epidemic, or in cases of force majeure, it can be imposed without the employee's agreement. Where telework is covered by a collective agreement or charter, an employer that refuses it to an employee whose job allows it must give reasons; without one, the employer need only give reasons when the request comes from a disabled worker or a carer.

The teleworker has the same rights and obligations as other employees. The employer must provide, install and maintain the equipment needed for telework at home, protect the data teleworkers use, set workload and deadlines on the same basis as for work on the premises so that rules on maximum working time and rest can be respected, hold an annual interview on the teleworker's working conditions and workload, give the teleworker priority for any job without telework matching their skills, and cover the costs arising from telework, either on the basis of receipts or through a flat-rate allowance. Telework may be included in the employer's occupational risk assessment, which can cover risks linked to distance from the work community and the use of digital tools, and an accident at the place of telework during working activity is presumed to be a work accident.
