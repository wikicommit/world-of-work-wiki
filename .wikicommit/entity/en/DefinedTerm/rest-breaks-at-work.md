Rest breaks at work are periods during or between working days in which a worker is entitled to be free from work. The EU [[Legislation/working-time-directive]] defines three kinds of minimum rest period — daily rest, breaks and weekly rest — which, together with paid annual leave, give concrete expression to the right to daily and weekly rest periods in Article 31(2) of the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union. In the UK, according to GOV.UK's guidance, workers over 18 are usually entitled to three types of break: rest breaks at work, daily rest and weekly rest. A worker's employment contract may give them more or different rights to breaks.

## Usage

**Under EU law.** The European Commission's [[Report/interpretative-communication-on-the-working-time-directive]] sets out how these minimum periods have been interpreted. The Directive's Recital 5 requires rest to be expressed in units of time — days, hours or fractions of them — and any period that is not working time is a rest period ([[DefinedTerm/working-time-and-rest-period]]).

- *Daily rest.* Every worker is entitled to a minimum of 11 consecutive hours of rest per 24-hour period. The 24-hour period is not a calendar day, but the Court of Justice has held that work must as a general rule regularly alternate with rest, and that daily rest must directly follow a period of work so that the worker can relax and dispel the fatigue it caused. The Communication concludes that, where no derogation applies, a worker cannot work for more than 13 consecutive hours, from which the break must also be deducted. Where a worker has several contracts with the same employer, the minimum daily rest applies to the contracts taken as a whole.
- *Breaks.* Where the working day is longer than six hours, every worker is entitled to a rest break, whose duration and terms are left to collective agreements or national legislation. National transposition may not leave them to individual agreement between worker and employer, and Member States must ensure that workers not covered by a collective agreement are also entitled to one. The Commission considers that the break must be defined in units of time, that excessively short breaks would be contrary to the Directive, and that the break should take place at the latest after six hours of work. A break is a rest period, so workers should not be obliged to remain at their workstation or at the employer's disposal; the Court has held that a break during which a worker must be ready to respond to a call-out within two minutes can constitute working time. National law may, as a more favourable provision, count breaks as working time.
- *Weekly rest.* Per each seven-day period, every worker is entitled to a minimum uninterrupted rest period of 24 hours plus the 11 hours of daily rest — a continuous period of 35 hours, from which the daily rest cannot be subtracted. The Court has held that the weekly rest may be given at any time within each seven-day period, not necessarily on the same day each week. Member States may average weekly rest over a 14-day reference period, allowing either two periods of 35 hours or one double period in each 14 days. Where objective, technical or work-organisation conditions justify it, weekly rest may be reduced to 24 hours; the Communication notes this was introduced mainly for shift workers who work the late shift on Saturday and the early shift on Monday, and that its use must be considered case by case. The 1993 version of the Directive provided that weekly rest should in principle include Sunday, but the Court annulled that sentence as not justified on health and safety grounds.

These rules are minimum standards, and national law often provides more, for example longer daily rest or a maximum daily working time. Member States may derogate from all three, in most cases only if equivalent compensatory rest is granted.

**In the UK.** Workers have the right to one uninterrupted 20-minute rest break during their working day if they work more than 6 hours a day; this could be a tea or lunch break. Whether the break is paid depends on the employment contract. Workers have the right to 11 hours' rest between working days — for example, a worker who finishes at 8pm should not start again until 7am the next day. Workers also have the right to either an uninterrupted 24 hours without any work each week, or an uninterrupted 48 hours without any work each fortnight.

Where work is monotonous, such as work on a production line, and so puts health and safety at risk, an employer should give an employee enough breaks to make sure their health and safety is not at risk. Domestic workers in a private house, such as a cleaner or au pair, are not entitled to rest breaks for health and safety reasons.

**In Japan.** According to the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare, an employer must give a break of at least 45 minutes when working hours exceed 6 hours, and of at least 1 hour when they exceed 8 hours. Employers must also give at least one day off every week, or at least four days off over a four-week period.

**In fatigue risk management.** Beyond legal minimums, occupational health and safety guidance treats breaks and rest as fatigue controls. WorkSafe New Zealand's guidance [[Report/managing-the-risks-of-shift-work]], which notes that New Zealand has minimum legal break requirements of its own, says that frequent short breaks can reduce fatigue, improve productivity and may reduce errors and accidents, that breaks are better taken away from the workstation, and that the time needed to reach the break area, toilets or facilities should be added to the break rather than counted in it; businesses must provide rest facilities, including somewhere to prepare food. Breaks between consecutive shifts should leave enough time to recover, commute, eat well, sleep and take part in social and domestic life. Rest days are treated as opportunities for unrestricted night-time sleep: the number needed after a run of shifts depends mostly on the [[DefinedTerm/sleep-debt]] built up, the guidance suggests a general limit of five to seven consecutive working days for standard shifts, and it advises allowing at least two nights of full unrestricted sleep when switching between day and night shifts and building regular weekend breaks into schedules where reasonably practicable ([[DefinedTerm/shift-work]]).
