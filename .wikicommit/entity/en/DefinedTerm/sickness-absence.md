Sickness absence is absence from work because of one's own health problems. How it is counted depends on the statistic: in its international comparison of sickness absence based on the Labour Force Survey, Statistics Norway (SSB) defines it as at least one week's absence from work because of one's own health problems, as reported by survey respondents — so the absence need not be medically certified, and the person need not be formally classed as on sick leave under their country's legislation. SSB distinguishes this from Norway's statistics on medically certified sickness absence, which use other definitions and collection methods.

## Usage

**As a cross-country measure.** Because Labour Force Surveys in all European countries use the same variable definitions, SSB argues that a survey-based measure is not affected by differences in sick-pay schemes: everyone away from work because of their own illness is counted, however national laws and social schemes classify them. SSB also notes that the survey is not designed to measure sickness absence and does so only as a by-product of measuring employment and working time, though its large sample and long-run stability make it reasonable to assume it shows a realistic level.

**Who is counted.** Only people in work can be absent from work, so the measured rate depends on who is employed as well as on how often they fall ill. SSB's analysis [[Report/norway-sickness-absence-overtook-sweden-and-netherlands]] illustrates both sides. Groups such as people with disabilities and people in physically heavy work have higher absence than others, so employing more of them raises a country's rate even if they would otherwise be at home rather than working. Conversely, where people who become ill more often lose their job, the rate falls, because they drop out of the statistics rather than recover. Looking at the absence rate in isolation can therefore lead to wrong conclusions about public health and public spending.

**Comparing countries.** Comparing Norway with Sweden, Denmark, Finland, Iceland, the United Kingdom and the Netherlands over 1983–2017, SSB found that Norway has had the highest sickness absence since 2006 because absence fell in Sweden and the Netherlands rather than because it rose in Norway. Differences in the composition of the employed — age, sex, occupation and disability — explained little, and SSB concludes that sick people in Norway tend to remain on sickness benefit, while in the other countries they are more exposed to becoming unemployed while sick. After the Netherlands' 2002 reform (*Wet Verbetering Poortwachter*), which made company doctors responsible for sick notes and employers responsible for sick pay and reintegration, sickness absence fell while unemployment rose.
