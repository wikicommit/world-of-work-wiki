Shift work intolerance — also called "shift maladaptation syndrome" — describes workers who have trouble adjusting to [[DefinedTerm/shift-work]] right from the start, show symptoms of illness such as sleep disturbance, gastrointestinal complaints and mood disturbance from the outset, and transfer to a regular daytime schedule after a short period. As background on why shift work can affect health more generally, disruption of circadian rhythms is one of three pathways researchers have proposed, alongside unhealthy behaviour and stress: biological functions such as body temperature, cognitive performance and hormonal secretions normally follow a 24-hour cycle, while shift workers must sleep when their body rhythms favour activity and be alert when their bodies are preparing for sleep, and because most return to normal hours on their days off, the circadian system never fully adapts.

## Usage

Statistics Canada's [[ScholarlyArticle/shift-work-and-health]] (2002) uses these terms to explain why it found no cross-sectional link between shift work and chronic conditions or psychological distress, but did find longitudinal ones: workers who cannot tolerate shift work tend to stop doing it relatively soon, so their physical and psychological problems may not be visible in a snapshot. In its National Population Health Survey data, fewer than one in five people on evening, rotating or irregular shifts in 1994/95 kept that schedule through 1996/97 and 1998/99. The article reads this turnover as consistent with earlier studies suggesting a "healthy survivor effect": those who continue in shift work are a more robust group, willing and able to tolerate its stress.

The article also links tolerance to age and circumstance. Drawing on earlier research, it notes that it is more difficult for older people to deal with shift work and that many switch to daytime jobs; this progressive intolerance with ageing may be due to the flattening of circadian rhythms that occurs as people reach their forties and fifties, or to a decline in the ability to cope with stress. It has also been suggested that commitment to shift work may be the most important individual factor in the ability to tolerate it — which the article offers as one possible reason why women, who more often work shifts to care for family or to attend school, showed no increased risk of new chronic conditions.
