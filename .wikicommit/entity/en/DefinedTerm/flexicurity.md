Flexicurity is an approach to labour market policy that combines labour market flexibility with strong social security and active labour market programmes. [[Report/world-development-report-2019]] cites Denmark's adoption of flexicurity, which it says has its roots in the 19th century, as an example of a substantially new social contract, alongside the market reforms begun in China in 1978, Poland's Balcerowicz Plan of 1989 and Germany's Hartz reforms of 2003.

## When It Applies

The report's case for balancing security and flexibility arises in its discussion of labour regulation. It argues that as people become better protected through enhanced social assistance and social insurance, labour regulation could, where appropriate, be made more flexible to facilitate movement between jobs — for example by providing income support for the unemployed through unemployment benefits rather than [[DefinedTerm/severance-pay]]. The approach assumes that flexibility for firms goes hand in hand with stronger social protection, intermediation and job search assistance, and arrangements for strengthening the voice of workers; the report stresses that effective representation of both formal and informal sector workers is what preserves the "security" element of flexicurity. It identifies the typical way this goes wrong: many governments have made their labour markets more flexible, but only a few have made corresponding investments in income support and reemployment assistance, and reducing restrictions on hiring and dismissal without increased protection outside the work contract and active reemployment support would shift an unmanageable risk burden onto workers. The report offers Denmark as an example rather than presenting evaluated results of the approach.
