The child penalty (Dutch *babyboete*, "baby fine") is the change in a parent's income from work after the birth of their first child, expressed relative to their income in the year before the child was born. Statistics Netherlands (CBS) estimates it separately for mothers and fathers who were in work before the birth, using an event-study method: changes in income around the birth are estimated with a linear regression that takes account of career differences (through the parent's age) and of the business cycle, and the income estimated for the years after the birth is related to the income estimated for the year before it.

## Usage

In the Emancipation Monitor 2024 chapter [[Report/working-and-caring-emancipation-monitor-2024]], CBS used its income statistics for 1994–2022, based on tax data, for men and women who had their first child (possibly a multiple birth) in 1996–2016 and worked before the birth. It looked at income from work in the two years before the birth (because of anticipation effects), the year of the birth and the six years after; a six-year window captures the largest changes, since women adjust their working hours mainly in the first years after a birth, and also covers the effect of most second children.

For women who had their first child in 2014–2016, income six years after the birth was 35% lower than in the year before; for those who kept working after the birth it was 28% lower. Men experienced no child penalty: their income was 1% higher. Women's income falls most in the year of the birth and the year after, and continues to decline less steeply in later years. The penalty for mothers has shrunk over time, from 57% for women whose first child was born in 1996–2001, mainly because women's working hours have risen; CBS also points to new mothers less often stopping work and to a smaller hourly pay gap between men and women. For fathers the figure has varied only between a fall of almost 2% and a rise of almost 2% since the mid-1990s. The penalty is smallest for women with higher professional or university education (28%, against 47% for women with a vmbo or comparable diploma), mainly because they cut their working hours least after the birth.
