Progressive universalism is a principle for expanding social protection that deliberately aims at higher levels of coverage while ensuring that the poor benefit more and before others. [[Report/world-development-report-2019]] proposes it as a guiding principle for strengthening social assistance, and for covering more people, especially in the informal economy: coverage is expanded from the bottom up, giving priority to the poorest, while navigating the fiscal, practical and political trade-offs that each increment of coverage involves. Where exactly in the income distribution a person becomes a net beneficiary rather than a net payer is, in the report's words, a choice that countries and governments would make themselves.

## Usage

The report proposes a guaranteed social minimum — with social assistance at its core, and including the set of social assistance programmes that provide financial support to a large share of the population, or even all of it — as one of three components of social protection alongside social insurance and labour market regulation. Progressive universalism is its guiding principle for strengthening social assistance, and the report says it may guide the expansion of such a minimum from the bottom up. The need it responds to is low coverage: in low-income countries, only 18 percent of people in the poorest quintile are covered by social assistance and 2 percent by social insurance, against 77 and 28 percent in upper-middle-income settings. The report argues that the desirable level of spending on such a minimum may in many developing countries be significantly higher than current average spending on social assistance of 1.5 percent of GDP, and that programmes, whether old or new, should share the notion of progressive universalism. As evidence of the headway low- and middle-income countries have already made in social assistance, it cites the growth of conditional cash transfers in Tanzania and in the Philippines, where the Pantawid programme covers 20 percent of the population at a cost of 0.5 percent of GDP, and of South Africa's Child Support Grant from 1 million beneficiaries in 2001 to 11 million in 2014.

## When It Applies

The report presents the principle for countries where social protection coverage is low and informality is widespread, so that social insurance financed by contributions from formal wage employment leaves most workers out. It assumes that revenue can be mobilised to pay for wider coverage: expansion is to be gradual, in line with the prevailing fiscal space, and should proceed at the same pace as the mobilisation of the resources it requires. More universal approaches, the report notes, reduce or eliminate hurdles around programme fragmentation, eligibility determination and social tensions but require significant additional resources, and the choice of larger or smaller tax-transfer policies has distributional effects and draws on different bases of political support. The report puts progressive universalism forward as a guiding principle for policy rather than as a measured result.
