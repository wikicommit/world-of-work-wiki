A zero-hours contract is a contract or arrangement under which a worker has no contractually guaranteed hours of work, so the hours they work — and what they earn — depend on the work the employer chooses to offer. In UK policy analysis a distinction is drawn between a zero-hours *contract*, under which the worker is obliged to accept the work offered, and a zero-hours *arrangement*, under which they are not. Because no hours are guaranteed, every hour such a worker works is above their contracted hours.

## Usage

Zero-hours contracts are a central case of [[DefinedTerm/one-sided-flexibility]]. The UK government's 2026 options assessment on the [[DefinedTerm/right-to-guaranteed-hours]] ([[Report/right-to-guaranteed-hours-options-assessment]]) estimates that about 2.6 million employee jobs in the UK were zero-hours contracts or arrangements in April 2025, according to the Annual Survey of Hours and Earnings, and notes that an average zero-hours worker in the Labour Force Survey usually works around 23 hours a week. It cites evidence that zero-hours workers are more likely than other workers to be paid close to the National Minimum Wage and to report a long-term health condition, being almost twice as likely to report mental ill health, and that 28% are underemployed. On the employer side, managing fluctuating demand is a key reason for using them.

Used in the right way, according to the same assessment, zero-hours contracts give employers workforce flexibility and offer employment to people who might otherwise not enter the labour market at all — students, people with fluctuating health conditions and older workers downshifting towards retirement are among those who can benefit most. Other evidence it cites points to "constrained choice": many people take them because no other work is available.

Countries have regulated them in different ways. New Zealand and Norway have banned them outright and Ireland prohibits them with limited exceptions, while in the Netherlands and Finland an employer must offer a contract on the terms of a set shift pattern the worker consistently works. The UK's [[Legislation/employment-rights-act-2025]] instead keeps them lawful but requires employers to offer qualifying workers a contract guaranteeing the hours they regularly work; workers who value the flexibility can decline the offer and stay on their existing contract.
