Severance pay is a payment made by an employer to a worker who is dismissed. [[Report/world-development-report-2019]] describes it as the most prevalent form of financial protection for workers whose livelihood has been disrupted in most low- and middle-income economies, and as a practice left over from a time when governments were unable to offer unemployment income support. The report also notes that a formal wage employment contract is still the most common basis for regulations such as those specifying a minimum wage or severance pay.

## Usage

Some countries have, on paper, extremely generous statutory severance pay: after 10 years of continuous employment it amounts to 132 weeks of salary in Sierra Leone, 130 weeks in Mauritius and 120 weeks in Bahrain. The report nonetheless calls it an ineffective instrument for income protection, for two reasons: it pools risk at the level of the firm or industry, where shocks and losses are correlated, and employees face a high risk of not being paid if their employer has liquidity constraints or goes out of business.

As an alternative, the report proposes placing greater reliance on unemployment benefits organised nationally, which would give workers more reliable options and open this form of protection to everyone, no matter where or how they work. To preserve work incentives, such systems could combine individual savings with redistribution: savings could be drawn on for unemployment or retraining, with any remainder available on retirement, while workers without enough savings could rely on a minimum income guarantee financed from general revenues. Chile and Jordan have individual savings accounts for unemployment. The report links this shift to a wider rebalancing of labour regulation in which firms gain flexibility as protection outside the work contract improves — the kind of balance associated with [[DefinedTerm/flexicurity]].
