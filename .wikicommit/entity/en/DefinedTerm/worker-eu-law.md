In EU law, the concept of a "worker" rests on what the Court of Justice of the European Union calls the essential feature of an employment relationship: that for a certain period of time a person performs services for and under the direction of another person, in return for which they receive remuneration. The EU [[Legislation/working-time-directive]] applies to "workers" without defining the term, and the Court has held that it has an autonomous meaning specific to EU law that cannot be interpreted differently according to the law of each Member State or narrowly. The Court rejected both national definitions and the definition in Directive 89/391/EEC on measures to improve the safety and health of workers at work, and instead linked the concept to the one it has developed for the free movement of workers laid down in Article 45 of the Treaty: a worker must be identified by objective criteria that distinguish the employment relationship by the rights and duties of the persons concerned.

## Usage

According to the European Commission's [[Report/interpretative-communication-on-the-working-time-directive]], it is for national courts to classify a person, on the basis of objective criteria and an overall assessment of all the circumstances, including the nature of the activities and the relationship between the parties. What matters is not status under national law but the person's concrete working arrangements — in particular whether they perform effective and genuine activities under the direction and supervision of another person, for remuneration. The Court has accordingly treated as workers employees of a body governed by public law regardless of their civil servant status, casual and seasonal staff on fixed-term contracts who were not covered by parts of the national labour code, and a volunteer firefighter under Belgian law, and has ruled that the concept could cover a person admitted to a work rehabilitation centre regardless of their productivity or the source and amount of their pay.

The same reasoning means that someone classed as self-employed under national law may be a worker for the purposes of the Directive if their independence is merely notional, disguising an employment relationship. The Court has pointed to indicators such as whether the person acts under the employer's direction as regards the freedom to choose the time, place and content of their work, whether they share the employer's commercial risks, and whether they form an integral part of the employer's undertaking for the duration of the relationship; by contrast, choosing the type of work and tasks and how they are performed, choosing when and where to work, and being freer to recruit one's own staff are typical of an independent service provider. To decide whether a self-employed person is a worker, the Court examines, first, whether their independence appears fictitious and, second, whether a relationship of subordination with the putative employer can be established. The Commission observes that this assessment could lead people in any form of contractual relationship to be treated as workers, and that, conversely, volunteers or self-employed people who do not qualify as workers are not covered by the Directive's limits on working time.
