A working-time mismatch (German: Arbeitszeitdiskrepanz) is a gap between the number of hours a worker would like to work and the number they actually work. It runs in both directions: a worker who wants more hours than they work is underemployed, and one who wants fewer is overemployed. In the IAB report [[Report/working-time-trends-preferences-and-reality]], desired hours are taken from a survey question asking how many hours a week people would most like to work if they could choose, bearing in mind that their earnings would change accordingly, and a difference of more than 2.5 hours a week between desired and actual hours counts as a mismatch.

## Usage

The report describes mismatches between desired and actual hours as observed in many industrialised countries and documented in numerous studies, and summarises the research as finding that the fewer hours people work, the more their desired hours exceed their actual ones, and that the same holds in reverse for long hours. Many part-time workers and especially [[DefinedTerm/minijob]] holders therefore want to work clearly more, while overemployed workers — mostly full-time — want to work less. According to the literature it reviews, mismatches impair satisfaction with life, health and work; they depend strongly on personal characteristics such as age, sex, family background, qualifications and income, with children a decisive factor in the differences between women and men; they arise and dissolve with transitions between life phases; and the economic cycle affects them, the financial crisis having increased them. The measured share of workers with a mismatch can differ between surveys for the same year because of different concepts and question wording, overemployment depending on this far more than underemployment. Wishes to reduce hours are harder to realise than wishes to increase them, often because of financial reasons or a heavy workload.

Using the [[Dataset/socio-economic-panel]], the report finds that in Germany the actual hours of two-thirds of employees did not match their wishes in 2021, and that the basic pattern has hardly changed since 1985: clearly more men than women are overemployed and more women than men underemployed. Overemployed workers form the largest group — around half of men and around 40% of women before the pandemic — and in 2021, 49% of women and 58% of men wanted to work less, the highest shares in the period, while about 15% of women and 9% of men wanted to work more. Employees would on average have liked to work 3.8 hours less in 2021 than they did.

Because mismatches are measured against hours actually worked, including overtime, they are considerably larger than gaps between desired and contractually agreed hours. The report presents realising people's wishes for longer hours as one possible lever for stabilising the total volume of work, though one that employers' requirements and regional differences prevent from being fully used, and notes that a simple arithmetic netting of wishes for longer and shorter hours ignores how people and households react to one another.
