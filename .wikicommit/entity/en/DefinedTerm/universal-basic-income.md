A universal basic income (UBI) is a way of building a guaranteed social minimum through a single programme with three design features, as [[Report/world-development-report-2019]] sets them out: it is aimed at every individual, independent of income or employment status; participants do not have to fulfil any conditions or reciprocal co-responsibilities; and assistance is provided in cash rather than as in-kind transfers or services. A UBI is not an alternative to health, education or other social services; it may supplement existing social assistance and more likely replace some of the programmes that provide income support. Although it pays everyone the same benefit, the money may be recovered from the rich, for example through a progressive income tax.

## Usage

**Experience.** The report finds that little is known about how a UBI works in practice. Only Mongolia has had one covering its entire population, from 2010 to 2012, before it was dismantled because of fiscal constraints when mineral prices collapsed; in 2011 the Islamic Republic of Iran replaced energy subsidies with cash transfers to 96 percent of its population, for one year. Local variants exist as resource dividend schemes: in 2016 the Alaska Permanent Fund in the United States distributed about US$2,000 each to 660,000 Alaskans. Several small-scale schemes and experiments called UBI programmes, in China, Kenya, the Netherlands, Scotland and the United States, are in some instances versions of targeted programmes.

**Cost.** Estimates cited in the report point to significant additional spending. With transfers set equal to those of existing cash transfer programmes, the additional cost was estimated at 13.8 percent of GDP in Finland, 10.1 percent in France, 8.9 percent in the United Kingdom and 3.3 percent in Italy. In a handful of emerging economies, a UBI set at 25 percent of median income would cost about 3.8 percent of GDP, against the 1.5 percent of GDP that low- and middle-income countries spend on social assistance on average. A UBI for adults set at the average poverty gap would cost 9.6 percent of GDP in low-income countries and 3.5 percent in upper-middle-income countries, and the cost would nearly double if it covered the entire population rather than adults only.

**Distribution and work incentives.** Simulations of replacing some existing schemes with a UBI found that most people in Nepal would gain; that in Indonesia a UBI of the same average amount as current programmes would leave about 40 percent of the poor with less; and that in South Africa most of the elderly and the poor would be worse off, with a similar effect on about 40 percent of senior citizens in Chile. On the recurring concern about work disincentives, the report finds that the available evidence shows both a UBI and other social assistance to have a limited impact on work incentives: a study of the Alaskan dividend found no impact on employment but a 1.8 percentage point rise in part-time employment, and a study of the Iranian quasi-UBI found no effect on overall labour supply.

**Alternatives.** The report sets the UBI against job guarantee schemes such as India's National Rural Employment Guarantee Act, which offers 100 days of work a year at the minimum wage. It suggests that a UBI may be an alternative to public works when their function is mere income support, while public works complement it where more meaningful activity is envisioned; "participation income", universal cash transfers tied to some form of civil engagement, is a hybrid of the two. In its overview, the report calls a UBI "untested and fiscally prohibitive for emerging economies".
