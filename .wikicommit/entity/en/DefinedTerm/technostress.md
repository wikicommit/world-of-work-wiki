Technostress is a negative psychological state related to the use of information and communication technologies (ICT), or to the threat of their use in the future. Spain's National Institute for Safety and Health at Work (INSST) describes it as arising from a perceived mismatch between demands and the resources available in relation to using technology: ICT can increase the inflow of work, sometimes causing overload, can require particular resources because they are complex to use, and their continual development and introduction can increase mental effort and the need to adapt.

## Usage

INSST's guidance on telework, [[Report/guidance-on-managing-ergonomic-and-psychosocial-aspects-of-telework]], treats technostress as one of the specific consequences of the intensive ICT use that defines telework under Spain's [[Legislation/spain-remote-work-act]]. The guidance distinguishes several forms:

- **Technoanxiety** — high levels of unpleasant physiological activation, tension and discomfort from using ICT, with scepticism about technology and negative thoughts about one's own competence. **Technophobia** is a specific form centred on fear and anxiety towards ICT.
- **Technofatigue** — high levels of mental and cognitive tiredness from continued ICT use, with sceptical attitudes and beliefs of ineffectiveness. Specific forms include "information fatigue", or information overload from Internet use, and computer fatigue from intensive and continuous ICT use, such as continual videoconferencing ("Zoom fatigue"). Unaddressed, fatigue can lead to general exhaustion, loss of interest, cynicism and difficulty concentrating or remembering.
- **Technoaddiction** — an uncontrollable compulsion to use ICT at all times and in all places, through which technology invades personal, working and social life; the continuous connectivity it allows can foster work addiction.

According to the guidance, technostress can also affect social and behavioural health, influencing the balance between working and personal time and encouraging unhealthy behaviour such as physical inactivity, poor diet or poor sleep. The preventive measures it proposes fall on both sides: the employer should plan how ICT is introduced, provide specific, good-quality training and adequate technical support, and allow workers control over their own tasks, while teleworkers should organise their time, set hours for work communications, guarantee their period of digital disconnection, avoid multitasking across several tools at once and take enough breaks.
