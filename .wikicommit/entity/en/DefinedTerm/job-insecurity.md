Job insecurity (Swedish *anställningsotrygghet*), the opposite of employment security, is characterised by the perception that one's own job is threatened. As cited in the Swedish knowledge compilation [[Report/organisation-of-work-and-its-significance-for-health-and-well-being]], De Witte, Vander Elst and De Cuyper define it as "an overall concern about the continued existence of the job in the future". They treat the experience as subjective, so that the same situation can be experienced differently and employees in the same workplace may feel more or less insecure regardless of the objective risk, while arguing that subjective and objective risk are generally correlated. They also distinguish **quantitative** job insecurity, a concern about losing the job as such, from **qualitative** job insecurity, a concern about aspects of the job such as working conditions, pay and career development opportunities.

## Usage

The compilation treats employment security as a temporal aspect of how work is organised and summarises research linking job insecurity to poorer health and well-being. Among the studies it reviews, a meta-analysis of 56 studies with just over 53,000 participants found a significant correlation between subjective job insecurity and mental health outcomes such as depression, anxiety and emotional exhaustion; an analysis of European Working Conditions Survey data linked job insecurity to sleep problems; a Finnish study of permanent employees associated high quantitative insecurity with lower job satisfaction and high qualitative insecurity with poorer self-rated health; and a Swedish study found that job insecurity had the same negative effect on health whether employees held permanent or temporary contracts. Job insecurity is reported more often by temporary employees and by workers employed through [[DefinedTerm/temporary-agency-work]].

Institutional context can also matter. The compilation cites a study of hotel cleaners in Denmark showing that the design of social insurance systems can weaken the link between job insecurity and economic worry: under the [[DefinedTerm/flexicurity]] model, what might otherwise be experienced as insecurity with negative consequences was not experienced in the same way. It also cites a European study finding that countries whose welfare regimes support balancing work with leisure and family are less vulnerable to mental-health problems arising from low employment quality.
