Circadian disruption is the disturbance of the body's circadian rhythms — biochemical, behavioural and biological processes that run on an approximately 24-hour cycle — that occurs when people work and sleep in the wrong parts of their circadian body clock cycle. WorkSafe New Zealand's guidance [[Report/managing-the-risks-of-shift-work]] names it as one of the four main causes of [[DefinedTerm/work-related-fatigue]], alongside missing out on sleep, being awake for too long and workload, and explains that because the circadian body clock cannot fully adapt to the changes [[DefinedTerm/shift-work]] brings, circadian rhythms fall out of sync with the world and with each other, resulting in poor sleep, poor health and lower functioning.

## Usage

According to the guidance, people are programmed to be active and alert during the day and to sleep at night because of an internal clock in the brain, which controls daily cycles of core temperature, hormones, falling and staying asleep, mood and the ability to work. The clock is kept in step with the 24-hour day mainly through exposure to daylight, with meal times, social contact, physical activity and work hours also playing a part. It makes people sleepiest in the earliest hours of the morning — when they are most error-prone — and in the late afternoon, and most alert in the few hours before normal bedtime and across the morning. The guidance places the lowest level of functioning usually between 3 and 5 am.

This rhythm explains why shift workers' sleep is often short and of poorer quality. Night workers sleep in the day, when sleep is usually lighter and shorter, and their sleep is limited by the clock making them more alert around lunchtime or early afternoon; early starters find it hard to fall asleep earlier than usual because they are naturally alert before their normal bedtime; late finishers get to bed later but may still have to wake early ([[DefinedTerm/night-work]]). In rostering, the guidance notes that slow, forward-rotating shifts (morning to afternoon to night) may be easier to adjust to, but the clock still has to re-adjust with every change of shift type and on days off. People also vary in their tolerance: being an older adult, having poor sleep habits, poor general health, stress, some medical conditions and sleep disorders such as insomnia and sleep apnoea, and using some medications and drugs can make the disruption harder to cope with.
