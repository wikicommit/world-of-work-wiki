Work potential is a concept Statistics Canada uses to assess the potential size of the labour force with disabilities in an inclusive, accessible and accommodating labour market. It applies to persons with disabilities who are not currently working and who might be likely to enter paid employment under a best-case scenario — a labour market without discrimination, with full accessibility and accommodation. As [[Report/examining-work-potential-and-overqualification-among-persons-with-disabilities]] puts it, the concept is not an attempt to measure individuals' capacity or ability to work, but a way to examine how the labour market might change under more inclusive conditions.

## Usage

In the [[Dataset/canadian-survey-on-disability]], people who are officially unemployed, or who are not in the labour force but say they will be looking for work in the next 12 months, are classified as potential workers. Among the others outside the labour force, those who say they are completely retired, or that their condition completely prevents them from working and that no workplace accommodation exists that would enable them to work, are classified as not being potential workers; those who are not completely retired and either are not prevented from working by their condition or say that workplace accommodations exist that would enable them to work are classified as potential workers. Work potential is not determined for people whose answers are too incomplete to place them in any of these categories.

Statistics Canada treats non-working persons with disabilities as a possibly underutilized resource within the labour market, alongside persons with disabilities who are employed but overqualified for their jobs ([[DefinedTerm/over-qualification]]). With 2022 survey data, 42.0% of persons with disabilities who were unemployed or not in the labour force had work potential — 741,280 people in Canada. The share was highest among youth aged 15 to 24 (88.7%), who are often new to the workforce or still in school, and lowest among seniors aged 65 and over (6.0%), who are typically at the end of their working lives. Among those aged 25 to 64 it was lower for people with more severe disabilities or more disability types, and differed by age, region, place of residence, household arrangement, education and racialized or 2SLGBTQ+ identity, but not significantly by gender.
