Job polarization is the expansion of high- and low-skill jobs coupled with the decline of middle-skill jobs. [[Report/world-development-report-2019]] describes it as the pattern that evidence from developed countries points to: demand is rising for workers who can undertake nonroutine cognitive tasks, such as high-skilled research, and for workers able to handle nonroutine tasks that cannot easily be automated, such as food preparation, while demand for procedural routine tasks often performed in middle-skill jobs, such as data entry, is declining because of automation.

## Usage

**Advanced economies.** In advanced economies, the report finds, employment has grown fastest in high-skill cognitive occupations and in low-skill occupations that require dexterity, and has shifted away from middle-skill occupations such as machine operators — the replacement of labour by automation appearing to be concentrated in middle-skill jobs. It names this as one of the factors that may translate into rising inequality: middle-skill workers could see falling wages because of automation, and low-skill workers because of increased competition.

**Developing countries.** Asked whether the same pattern is emerging in low- and middle-income countries, the report answers "not quite". In many developing countries the share of employment in high-skill occupations has increased — by 8 percentage points or more in Bolivia, Ethiopia and South Africa from 2000 to 2014 — but changes in low- and middle-skill employment are heterogeneous: the middle-skill share rose by 7.5 percentage points in Jordan between 2000 and 2016 and fell by almost 20 percentage points in Bangladesh over the same period. The report attributes this to the competing forces of automation, whose adoption varies considerably across developing countries, and globalization, which is bringing developed countries' low- and medium-skill jobs to some developing countries but not all; in many countries middle-skill jobs continue to grow in importance.

**Occupational groups.** For this comparison the report classes managers, professionals, technicians and associate professionals as high-skill occupations; clerical support workers, sales and services workers, craft and related trades workers, skilled agricultural, forestry and fishery workers, and plant and machine operators and assemblers as middle-skill; and elementary occupations — such as cleaners and helpers, labourers, food preparation assistants and street sales and service workers — as low-skill.
