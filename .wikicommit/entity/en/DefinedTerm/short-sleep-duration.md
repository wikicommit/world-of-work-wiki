Short sleep duration is sleeping fewer than 7 hours per day. The threshold follows the determination by the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Sleep Research Society that adults require at least 7 hours of sleep per day to promote optimal health; in the CDC analysis [[ScholarlyArticle/short-sleep-duration-by-occupation-group-29-states-2013-2014]], survey answers to the question of how many hours of sleep a person gets in a 24-hour period were split into at least 7 hours (sufficient sleep) and fewer than 7 hours (short sleep duration).

## Usage

According to that analysis, short sleep duration has been linked to adverse health outcomes including cardiovascular disease, obesity, diabetes, depression and anxiety, and to safety problems such as drowsy driving and injuries. Work-related factors such as job stress, work hours, [[DefinedTerm/shift-work]] and physically demanding work are associated with sleep duration and quality; these factors vary by occupation, and the prevalence of short sleep has also been shown to vary by occupation, which the analysis suggests warrants further evaluation of how work-related factors might relate to sleep. In 2013–2014 data from the [[Dataset/behavioral-risk-factor-surveillance-system]] for 29 US states, 36.5% of currently employed adults reported short sleep duration, ranging from 21.4% among air transportation workers to 58.2% among communications equipment operators.

The same article cites an estimate that short sleep among the US working population costs the economy $411 billion a year, equivalent to 2.28% of gross domestic product, and that 1.2 million working days are lost each year in the United States because of sleep deprivation.

WorkSafe New Zealand's guidance [[Report/managing-the-risks-of-shift-work]] uses a similar benchmark. It reproduces the National Sleep Foundation's recommendations of 7–9 hours of sleep in 24 hours for adults aged 18 to 64 and 7–8 hours for those aged 65 and over, notes that people who regularly miss out on the recommended amount are at higher risk of poor health and lower functioning, and states that the likelihood of a car crash increases when drivers have had less than seven hours of sleep in 24 hours. Repeated short sleep accumulates into a [[DefinedTerm/sleep-debt]].
