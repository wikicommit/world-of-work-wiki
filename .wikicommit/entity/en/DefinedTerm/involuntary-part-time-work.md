Involuntary part-time work is part-time work that a person does not because they prefer it but because of economic or business conditions, or because they could not find full-time work. Statistics Canada treats it as an indicator of underemployment that may be associated with financial stress. In the Canadian [[Dataset/canadian-labour-force-survey]], respondents working part-time are asked whether they want to work more or less than 30 hours a week at a single job or business, and the main reason for working part-time is recorded; those whose reason for working part-time in their main job is business conditions or not being able to find work of 30 or more hours are counted as involuntary part-time workers, whether or not they looked for full-time work in the previous month.

## Usage

Two indicators are built on the concept. Under the UNECE quality of employment framework, the *involuntary part-time work rate* expresses involuntary part-time workers as a proportion of all workers and measures how prevalent the situation is overall. The alternative — the share of people in [[DefinedTerm/part-time-employment]] who work part-time involuntarily — describes the nature of part-time work in different groups. The two can point in different directions: in Canada in 2022, employed women were more likely than men to work part-time involuntarily (3.6% versus 2.2% of workers), but involuntary work made up a larger share of men's part-time employment (17.4% versus 14.5%).

Statistics Canada reports that the rate moves with the economy: when unemployment is low and vacancies plentiful, workers find jobs with their preferred hours more easily, and when unemployment is high they are more likely to accept or stay in jobs with fewer hours than they want. The Canadian rate rose after the 2008/2009 recession and during the COVID-19 pandemic, and fell to 2.8% in 2022, its lowest level since 1997, as reported in [[Report/involuntary-part-time-work-1997-to-2022]].
