Shift work is a method of organising work in which workers take turns at the same work stations. The EU [[Legislation/working-time-directive]] defines it (Article 2(5)) as any method of organising work in shifts whereby workers succeed each other at the same work stations according to a certain pattern, including a rotating pattern, which may be continuous or discontinuous, entailing the need for workers to work at different times over a given period of days or weeks; a shift worker is any worker whose work schedule is part of shift work (Article 2(6)).

## Usage

According to the European Commission's [[Report/interpretative-communication-on-the-working-time-directive]], the Court of Justice has interpreted these definitions only to a very limited extent. In its Simap ruling (C-303/98) on doctors in public health services, who normally worked from 8 a.m. to 3 p.m. and were on call from 3 p.m. to 8 a.m. every eleven days, the Court held that the on-call duty met the definition, because the doctors were assigned successively to the same posts on a rotational basis and, although the duty recurred at regular intervals, were called on to work at different times over a given period of days or weeks; it did not treat their normal day schedule as shift work. The Commission notes that shift work is not limited to the familiar rotating pattern of three eight-hour periods a day. Shift workers may also be night workers — the Court has held that a worker whose shift work is only partly performed at night is a night worker — and a worker covered by more than one definition benefits from the protection attached to each ([[DefinedTerm/night-work]]).

The Directive gives shift workers, together with night workers, the right to safety and health protection appropriate to the nature of their work, and to prevention and protection services or facilities equivalent to those for other workers and available at all times (Article 12). The Commission links this to Recital 7, which notes that the human body is more sensitive at night to environmental disturbances and to certain burdensome forms of work organisation, and to its original proposal's reference to the more demanding nature of these forms of work and the problems that usually occur when shift cycles change. Article 13 requires employers who organise work according to a certain pattern to take account of the general principle of adapting work to the worker, in particular to alleviate monotonous work and work at a predetermined rate, and of safety and health requirements, especially as regards breaks; the Commission describes this as particularly relevant to shift work, though not limited to it.

Several of the Directive's flexibilities were designed with shift work in mind. Member States may derogate from daily and weekly rest when a worker changes shift and cannot take rest between the end of one shift and the start of the next, provided equivalent compensatory rest is granted (Article 17(4)); and the possibility of reducing weekly rest to 24 hours was introduced mainly for shift workers working the late shift on Saturday and the early shift on Monday ([[DefinedTerm/rest-breaks-at-work]]). For offshore work, the option of a twelve-month reference period for averaging weekly working time was intended in particular to allow shift systems of two twelve-hour shifts over fourteen days to continue.

## Coping with shift work

The UK Health and Safety Executive (HSE) notes that people vary in how they cope with shift work depending on their health, fitness, age, lifestyle and domestic responsibilities — some adapt well, others do not. It identifies sleep loss and fatigue as among the most significant problems for shift workers: daytime sleep is usually lighter, shorter and of poorer quality than night-time sleep, and is more often disturbed by warmer temperatures and daytime noise. Digestive problems are common because of disruption of the body clock and poor diet; working times that differ from the routines of family and friends can leave shift workers feeling isolated; and on night and very early morning shifts it can be hard to stay alert, which may raise the risk of errors, injuries and accidents. Driving to and from work can be risky, particularly after a long shift or a night shift or before an early start.

While inbuilt characteristics cannot be changed, HSE's hints and tips for shift workers — drawn from commonly available advice and best practice — suggest behaviour and lifestyle changes that may make shift work more tolerable, improve sleep quality, increase alertness and reduce health risks. They include:

- **Sleep**: finding a sleep schedule that suits the shift pattern and keeping to it (for example, a short sleep before the first night shift, and a short sleep and an earlier bedtime when coming off nights), keeping a sleep diary, and making the bedroom dark, quiet and cool.
- **Diet and stimulants**: regular light meals rather than a single heavy one, avoiding fatty, spicy or heavy and sugary food, drinking plenty of fluid, using caffeine only occasionally, and avoiding alcohol and sleeping pills as sleep aids because tolerance develops and persistent use risks dependence.
- **Fitness and social life**: regular physical activity, sharing the shift schedule with family and friends and planning time off together.
- **Travel and alertness**: considering public transport or taxis rather than driving and stopping to nap when sleepy; at work, keeping the light bright, taking regular short breaks, moving around and scheduling more stimulating tasks for the times of greatest drowsiness.
