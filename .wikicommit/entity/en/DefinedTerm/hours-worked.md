Under the US [[Legislation/fair-labor-standards-act]] (FLSA), hours worked is the time an employer must count and pay for as work. Because the Act requires at least the minimum wage and overtime pay at one and one-half times the regular rate for hours over 40 in a week, the amount an employee is owed cannot be determined without knowing the number of hours worked. The concept rests on the statutory definition of "employ", which includes "to suffer or permit to work": work that was not requested but that the employer suffers or permits — for example, staying on at the end of a shift to finish a task or correct errors — is work time and must be paid, whatever the reason for it. The workweek ordinarily includes all time during which an employee is necessarily required to be on the employer's premises, on duty or at a prescribed workplace, and the workday runs from the start to the end of the employee's "principal activity", so it may be longer than the scheduled shift.

## Usage

The US Department of Labor's Wage and Hour Division sets out how the principle applies to common situations in its Fact Sheet #22 (revised July 2008), which it describes as general information that does not have the force of law:

- **Waiting time.** An employee "engaged to wait" — a secretary reading while waiting for dictation, a firefighter playing checkers while waiting for an alarm — is working; an employee "waiting to be engaged" is not. Which applies depends on the circumstances.
- **On-call time.** Being required to remain on call on the employer's premises is work. Remaining on call at home, or leaving word where one can be reached, is in most cases not, although additional constraints on the employee's freedom could make it compensable.
- **Rest and meal periods.** Short rest breaks, usually 20 minutes or less, are customarily paid and must be counted as hours worked; unauthorised extensions need not be, provided the employer has clearly communicated the permitted length, that extensions are against the rules and that they will be punished. Bona fide meal periods, typically 30 minutes or more, generally need not be paid, but only if the employee is completely relieved from duty.
- **Sleeping time.** An employee on duty for less than 24 hours is working even if allowed to sleep when not busy. For duty of 24 hours or more, the parties may agree to exclude bona fide regularly scheduled sleeping periods of up to 8 hours, provided adequate sleeping facilities are furnished and the employee can usually enjoy an uninterrupted night's sleep; no reduction is permitted unless at least 5 hours of sleep is taken.
- **Lectures, meetings and training.** Attendance need not be counted only if it is outside normal hours, voluntary, not job related, and no other work is done at the same time.
- **Travel.** Ordinary home-to-work travel is not work time. Travel to and from another city on a special one-day assignment is work time, less the employee's normal commute; travel between job sites during the workday is work time; and overnight travel away from home is work time when it cuts across the employee's workday, including the corresponding hours on non-working days. As an enforcement policy, the Division does not count time spent travelling away from home outside regular working hours as a passenger on an airplane, train, boat, bus or automobile.

The Division identifies failure to recognise certain time as hours worked as a typical compliance problem — for example, an employee who eats lunch at the desk while regularly answering the telephone is not completely relieved from duty, so that time must be counted and paid.
