Occupational segregation (*ségrégation professionnelle*) is, in Insee's definition, the de facto distribution of employees across occupations on the basis of demographic characteristics, which may be sex, age group, immigration status and so on. Applied to sex, it describes the fact that women and men do not do the same jobs and do not work in the same firms or sectors, and it also reflects hierarchical inequalities, since women and men are unequally represented in senior positions.

## Usage

Insee uses the concept in [[Report/pay-gap-between-women-and-men-in-2023]] as one of the factors behind the [[DefinedTerm/gender-pay-gap]] in France's private sector. In 2023, of the twenty most common occupations for each sex — accounting for about 38% of women's employment (secretaries, administrative employees, cleaners and others) and 30% of men's (road haulage drivers, IT engineers, delivery drivers and others) — only four were common to both. Women made up just 0.6% of skilled masons and 3.0% of road haulage drivers, while only 4.7% of secretaries were men, and fewer than a third of senior executive (*cadres dirigeants*) positions were held by women. According to Insee, occupational segregation explains most of the 13.5% gap between women's and men's full-time-equivalent net salaries: comparing women and men in the same job in the same establishment, the gap is estimated at 3.8%. That estimate covers only the part of the private sector (40% of full-time equivalents) where at least one woman and one man hold the same job in the same establishment, and Insee cautions that it cannot be read strictly as a measure of pay discrimination, since it is not corrected for unobserved differences such as experience, tenure or qualifications.
