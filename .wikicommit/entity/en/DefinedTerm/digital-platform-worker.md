A digital platform worker is a person who performs paid work organized or facilitated by a [[DefinedTerm/digital-labour-platform]]. The draft Convention concerning decent work in the platform economy, set out in [[Report/decent-work-in-the-platform-economy]] and retained unchanged in the revised draft of [[Report/realizing-decent-work-in-the-platform-economy-2026]], defines the term as "a person employed or engaged to work: (i) for the provision of service organized and/or facilitated by a digital labour platform; (ii) for remuneration or payment; (iii) regardless of their classification of status in employment". The definition therefore covers employees and self-employed workers alike, and the draft Convention would apply to all digital platform workers, whether in the formal or informal economy, unless otherwise specified.

## Usage

Because the category spans different employment statuses, the draft instruments distinguish between what is due to different workers. "Remuneration" or "payment" is defined as the amount due to a digital platform worker under national laws and regulations, collective agreements or contractual obligations, according to their classification of status in employment, in exchange for the work performed, and excludes compensation for expenses or other costs incurred in carrying out the work; the Committee added "or 'payment'" to distinguish the labour income of employees from that of the self-employed. In the third report, the Office noted that while a principle in the Convention may apply to different categories of workers, the rights, levels of protection and ways of implementing them may differ between those categories, and it proposed qualifying several provisions with "according to the nature of work arrangements and the classification of digital platform workers' status in employment".

According to the fourth report, a majority of workers' and employers' organizations and some governments preferred to refer only to the classification of status in employment, and several governments stressed differentiating between platform workers in an employment relationship and the self-employed. The revised drafts accordingly separate the two groups in several places. Under the remuneration Article, principles such as payment in full and on time apply to all digital platform workers, while adequate remuneration no lower than any applicable minimum wage and reasonable compensation for expenses are required for those in an employment relationship, to be extended to other platform workers "to the extent possible and as appropriate". The draft Recommendation would prohibit charging fees or costs to platform workers who are employees, and permit fees to other platform workers only to compensate for specific services the platform provides to them; its working-time guidance — protection in relation to maximum hours and minimum rest, compensation for time spent waiting for work, and the ability to decline a task or disconnect without retaliation — applies as appropriate and considering the worker's classification. Social security protection and the general guarantee of no less favourable treatment are measured against other workers with the same classification of status in employment.

The draft Convention would require Member States to ensure the correct classification of digital platform workers in respect of the existence of an employment relationship, guided primarily by the facts relating to the performance of work and the worker's remuneration. The third report's draft also said this should take into account the Employment Relationship Recommendation, 2006 (No. 198); the fourth report's revision drops that reference for streamlining, which the Office said would not change the Article's meaning, and removes a separate paragraph on not interfering with true civil and commercial relationships. Workers' organizations sought a reference to a legal presumption of employment, which the Office regarded as one method among others that could be adopted nationally. The draft also defines an "intermediary" as a person that makes the work of a digital platform worker available through contractual relationships with both the platform and the worker, or as part of a subcontracting chain between them; the Office notes that the Convention would apply to intermediaries to the extent that their use is permitted nationally and that they provide services between a platform worker and a platform within its scope.

**Earlier formulation.** In the questionnaire appended to the Office's first report on the standard-setting item, [[Report/realizing-decent-work-in-the-platform-economy-2024]] (ILC.113/Report V(1), 2024), governments were asked whether the term should mean "a person who is employed or engaged to work on or through a digital labour platform, regardless of their employment status or whether they work formally or informally".

**National legal definitions.** The same report compares how national legislation defines platform workers. Belgium's law of 3 October 2022 covers "any individual carrying out work via an ordering digital platform, notwithstanding the nature of the contractual relations or the qualification by the concerned parties". Chile's amended Labour Code refers to a person who performs personal services, "either on his own account or on behalf of others", requested by users of an application managed by a digital service platform company. China's guidelines for workers in new forms of employment cover workers who accept delivery, travel, transportation, domestic services and other assignments issued online by internet platforms and obtain remuneration for their work. India's Code on Social Security, 2020 defines "platform work" as "a work arrangement outside of a traditional employer–employee relationship" in which organizations or individuals use an online platform to access others to solve specific problems or provide specific services in exchange for payment, and a platform worker as a person engaged in such work. Malta's regulations cover any person performing delivery work for one or more digital labour platforms, directly or through a work agency, "irrespective of the contractual designation"; Kenya's transport regulations define a "transport network driver"; and the Republic of Korea's Industrial Accident Compensation Insurance Act defines a "platform worker" as a labour provider who provides labour through an online platform. The report finds that, while the definitions vary with the scope of each law, most define a platform worker as a person who provides services on an online platform, recognized as such irrespective of the nature of the employment relationship (Belgium), the contractual relationship (Malta) or whether they work on their own account or on behalf of others (Chile).

**Who works on platforms.** The report gathers evidence on the size and profile of the workforce on platforms that connect businesses and clients to workers. Official surveys put its prevalence in the low single digits in most countries, from 0.5 per cent of employment in Chile to 4 per cent in the Philippines, while World Bank estimates of active workers on online platforms range from 154 million to 435 million depending on the method. Most such workers are in delivery and passenger transport. For many, platform work supplements other income: according to ILO estimates, only around 30 per cent of workers on online platforms rely on them as their main source of income, against an estimated 84 per cent of passenger transport and 90 per cent of delivery platform workers. ILO surveys found that online platform workers work on average 27 hours in a typical week (32 hours in developing and 20 in developed countries), while average weekly hours reach 67 in passenger transport and 59 in delivery, with 41 and 38 per cent of workers in those sectors respectively working seven days a week; on microtask platforms, about 20 minutes of unpaid work accompany every hour of paid work, rising to 23 minutes on freelance platforms. Platform workers are typically younger than the working population — 33.9 years on average in the European Union against 42.4 for all employed persons — and, in some countries, more highly educated: 63 per cent of online platform workers surveyed by the ILO had a higher level of education, against 25 per cent of workers as a whole. Occupational segregation by gender tends to mirror the traditional labour market, with women under-represented in transport and delivery and on microtask platforms (about one in three workers) with a significant presence in care, domestic and beauty work; migrants are generally over-represented, with close to 17 per cent of freelance platform work carried out by migrant workers.

**Classification.** Most platforms characterize themselves as technological intermediaries and classify most of their workers as self-employed, a classification that shapes their access to labour and social protection. The report finds a significant body of case law on the question but no consensus on whether platform workers are employees or self-employed, reflecting diverse facts and regulatory frameworks; courts have largely applied the principle of the primacy of facts rather than the contractual terms, and some jurisdictions recognize an intermediate category between employment and self-employment. Several countries have legislated on the question, including through statutory presumptions (see [[DefinedTerm/presumption-of-employment-relationship]]).
