Eldercare is, in the [[Dataset/american-time-use-survey]] (ATUS), assisting or caring for an adult who needed help because of a condition related to aging — an ongoing ailment or physical or emotional limitation that typically affects older people, such as dementia or Parkinson's disease. The definition is not limited to care recipients of a particular age. It is narrower than adult care in general, which also covers temporary care such as helping a friend with a broken leg or a spouse with the flu. Unlike the ATUS measures of adult care and childcare used in [[Report/older-women-and-unpaid-caregiving-in-the-us]], which count only care given as a primary activity, its eldercare measures also count secondary care given while doing something else, such as keeping an eye on an older relative with dementia while watching TV.

## Usage

The U.S. Department of Labor's Women's Bureau presents eldercare as a growing need as the U.S. population ages, and one that care from friends and family meets alongside professional caregiving. According to [[Report/older-women-and-unpaid-caregiving-in-the-us]], women aged 55 or older account for 35.3% of all unpaid eldercare providers in the U.S. on any given day. On an average day, 8.8% of these women support a friend or family member with a chronic, age-related condition, compared with 6.2% of men of the same age. Eldercare can be sporadic, but for many of the women who give it, it is frequent: 28.3% provide it daily and a further 38.6% at least weekly.

Definitions differ between surveys. Canada's 2005 General Social Survey identified elder care with questions about hours spent in the previous week providing unpaid care or assistance to seniors — defined as people aged 65 or older — living inside or outside the respondent's household. Using these questions, [[Report/work-life-balance-of-older-workers]] found that 20% of workers aged 55 and over provided care to seniors, compared with 16% of workers aged 25 to 54, mostly outside the household. Older workers who provided four or more hours of elder care a week were over 14 percentage points more likely to be dissatisfied with their [[DefinedTerm/work-life-balance]] than those with no elder care responsibilities; 8% of older workers gave that much care in 2005, a share the authors expect to grow as Canada's population ages.
