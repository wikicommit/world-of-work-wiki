Night work is work performed during a legally defined night period, and a night worker is a person whose working pattern regularly falls within it. Under the EU [[Legislation/working-time-directive]], "night time" is any period of not less than seven hours, defined by national law, which must include the period between midnight and 5 a.m.; any work carried out during that period, however long, is night work. A "night worker" is a worker who, as a normal course, works at least three hours of daily working time during night time, or who is likely to work a certain proportion of annual working time during night time as defined by national legislation or by collective agreement. In the UK, according to GOV.UK's guidance for employers, staff who regularly work at least 3 hours during the "night period" are night workers. The night period is 11pm to 6am unless the worker and employer agree a different one in writing, in which case it must be 7 hours long and include midnight to 5am. Staff may also be night workers where a collective agreement, such as a trade union agreement, states that their work is night work.

## Usage

**Under EU law.** The European Commission's [[Report/interpretative-communication-on-the-working-time-directive]] sets out how the Directive's night work provisions are interpreted. Because the English text links the two criteria for being a night worker with "and", while other language versions and the Court's case-law suggest otherwise, the Commission considers them alternatives: meeting either one is enough. The categories are not mutually exclusive — the Court has held that a worker doing [[DefinedTerm/shift-work]] of which only part is performed at night is a night worker, and a worker covered by more than one definition benefits from the protection attached to each. The Directive's Recital 7 grounds these rules in research showing that the human body is more sensitive at night to environmental disturbances and to certain burdensome forms of work organisation, and that long periods of night work can harm workers' health and endanger safety.

The Directive sets two limits. Normal hours of work for night workers must not exceed an average of eight hours in any 24-hour period; this covers all their working time, including overtime, not only the hours at night. Member States or the social partners set the reference period for the average without an express limit, but the Commission considers it should be substantially shorter than the one used for the 48-hour week, since an equal period would make the night work limit pointless; weekly rest falling within the period is left out of the calculation, while daily rest is included. Night workers whose work involves special hazards or heavy physical or mental strain may not work more than eight hours in any 24-hour period in which they perform night work — an absolute limit that cannot be averaged. What counts as such work is defined by national law, practice or collective agreement, taking account of the specific effects and hazards of night work; the Commission's original proposal gave piece work, assembly-line work and work at a pre-established pace as examples.

Night workers are entitled to a free health assessment before they are assigned to night work and at regular intervals afterwards, which must respect medical confidentiality and may be carried out within the national health system; the Directive leaves the intervals, and who pays, to Member States. Night workers with health problems recognised as connected with night work must be transferred whenever possible to day work to which they are suited. Unlike the ILO Night Work Convention (No. 171), with which the Commission compares these provisions, the Directive gives the right to an assessment to all night workers rather than on request. Member States must also ensure that night workers and shift workers have safety and health protection appropriate to the nature of their work, with prevention and protection services equivalent to those for other workers and available at all times; the Court has held that night workers must benefit from measures on working hours, pay, allowances or similar benefits that compensate for the particular hardship of their work. Member States may add guarantees for particular categories of night workers, and employers who regularly use night workers must inform the competent authorities if asked. The limits on the length of night work are among the provisions from which Member States may derogate under the Directive's derogations, subject in most cases to equivalent compensatory rest; an individual opt-out from the 48-hour week does not extend to them.

**In the UK.** Additional rules apply to night workers on top of the general rules on [[DefinedTerm/maximum-weekly-working-hours]] and rest breaks. Night workers must not work more than an average of 8 hours in a 24-hour period. The average is usually calculated over 17 weeks, but can be taken over a longer period of up to 52 weeks if workers and the employer agree, for example by collective agreement; regular overtime counts towards the average but occasional overtime does not, and workers cannot opt out of the limit. Night workers who deal with special hazards, or whose work involves mental or physical strain, cannot work longer than 8 hours in any 24-hour period; a risk assessment must be carried out to identify such hazards and strain, which may also be set out in collective or workforce agreements.

Young workers are restricted further. Staff aged 16 or 17 cannot work between midnight and 4am, and usually cannot work between 10pm and 6am (which can be changed by contract to 11pm to 7am), with exceptions for work in agriculture; cultural, sporting, artistic or advertising activities; hospitals; hotels or catering; retail; and post or newspaper delivery. In exceptional circumstances they can work at night if no adult is available and they are needed to handle a sudden increase in demand or to maintain the continuity of a service or production, such as filming; the employer must then give them a rest period of the same length as the extended shift.

The National Minimum Wage applies to night workers, but there is no higher night working rate. On sleep-in shifts, workers who are expected to sleep for most of the shift (for example, a care worker) and are provided with suitable sleeping facilities are paid the minimum wage only for the periods when they are awake to perform tasks, while workers expected to work for most of a shift get it for the whole shift even if they are allowed to sleep between tasks.

Employers must keep records of night workers' working hours to show that the limits are not exceeded, and keep them for at least 2 years. A worker who does not want to work nights must not be discriminated against.
