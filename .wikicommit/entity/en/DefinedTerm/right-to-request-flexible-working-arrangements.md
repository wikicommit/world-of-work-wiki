The right to request flexible working arrangements is a procedural entitlement under which eligible employees may ask their employer to change their working arrangements — such as their hours, pattern or location of work, including [[DefinedTerm/working-from-home]] — and the employer may refuse only on reasonable grounds. It obliges the employer to consider the request; it does not give the employee a substantive right to the arrangement itself.

## Usage

In Australia, as described by the Productivity Commission in [[Report/working-from-home-research-paper]], the minimum right sits in section 65 of the Fair Work Act 2009 (Cth), whose provisions are minimum standards. It applies to employees with at least 12 months' service (or casual employees engaged on a long-term, regular and systematic basis) who are parents of school-age or younger children or carers, have a disability, are aged 55 or older, or are experiencing, or caring for someone experiencing, family violence, and the request must arise from those circumstances. Reasonable business grounds for refusal include that the arrangement would be too costly, that other employees' arrangements cannot practicably be changed to accommodate it, or that it would cause a significant loss of efficiency or productivity or a significant negative impact on customer service.

Other instruments build on this minimum:

- **Modern awards.** Since 2018, all modern awards contain a provision that keeps the same eligible groups but requires the employer to discuss the request and genuinely try to reach an agreement that reasonably accommodates the employee's circumstances, which the Commission says gives those employees a strong basis for negotiating a work-from-home arrangement.
- **Enterprise agreements.** In the December quarter of 2019, about 350 agreements — some 3% of agreements, covering just over 20% of employees — allowed some or all work to be done at home or away from the office, and some grant a broader right to request.
- **State laws and awards.** Queensland's Industrial Relations Act 2016 gives employees a general right to request flexible working arrangements; Victoria's Equal Opportunity Act 2010 requires employers not to unreasonably refuse to accommodate parental or caring responsibilities, without a minimum service requirement; and the Tasmanian State Service Award 2020 provides that requests for work–life balance arrangements will not be unreasonably refused.
- **Workplace policies.** Firm and public-sector policies, which are not enforceable rights, often go further — for example, flexible-by-default policies under which, if a role cannot have one type of flexibility, managers and employees must look for a type that will work.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the Fair Work Commission temporarily varied some awards to ease remote working (for example, a variation to the Clerks — Private Sector Award that, among other things, extended the span of ordinary hours for employees working remotely and expired on 30 June 2021), and in 2020 it released a draft Award Flexibility Schedule under which employees could request, among other things, a compressed working week or changed hours when working from home. None of these changes created an entitlement to work from home.

In the United Kingdom, the government's GOV.UK guidance describes a broader version of the right. All employees have the legal right to request flexible working — defined there as a way of working that suits an employee's needs, such as flexible start and finish times or working from home — and can do so from their first day in a job; a request of this kind is known as a "statutory application". An employee can ask to change the number of hours they work, when they start or finish, the days they work, or where they work. Employers must deal with requests in a "reasonable manner", for example by assessing the advantages and disadvantages of the application, discussing possible alternatives and offering an appeal process, with further guidance in the Advisory, Conciliation and Arbitration Service (Acas) code of practice on flexible working requests. An employer can refuse an application if it has a good business reason, and an employee whose request is not handled in a reasonable manner can take the employer to an employment tribunal. Northern Ireland has different rules.

The [[Legislation/employment-rights-act-2025]] raises the burden of justification on employers. Its Explanatory Notes describe the pre-existing position as a day-one right to request that employers could reject for several reasons, and state that under the Act employers must accept a request except where it is not reasonably feasible.

In the European Union, Eurofound's report [[Report/telework-in-the-eu-regulatory-frameworks-and-recent-updates]] describes a right to request telework specifically. In every country with telework legislation, access rests on agreement between employer and employee, in line with the voluntary principle of the [[Legislation/eu-framework-agreement-on-telework]], so any employee may ask but the employer may refuse. Only in France, Lithuania and Portugal (the last since 2021) does legislation require the employer's decision on a telework request to be justified or motivated, particularly where the request comes from a worker with care needs, and the report treats these three as recognising a right to request telework. It distinguishes this from individual agreements: under a right to request, the request can in principle be rejected only if the employee does not meet certain conditions. Some countries go further for particular groups. In Lithuania, pregnant workers, new parents, parents of young children, single parents, workers with a medical condition and workers caring for others owing to illness have a right to telework; in Poland, the employer must grant a request to switch to telework from workers with a complicated pregnancy and parents of disabled children; and in Portugal, workers who are victims of domestic violence, and workers with children up to three years old (or up to eight in specific situations), have a right the employer cannot oppose where telework is compatible with the activity and the employer has the means. The report also points to the EU Work–Life Balance Directive, which includes telework among the flexible working arrangements to which working parents and carers are entitled, and which extends the right to request flexible working to all working parents with children up to the age of eight and to all carers.

As of the second quarter of 2022, two further countries were debating a right to request telework. In Germany, a draft Mobile Work Act proposed by the Ministry of Labour in late 2020 would first have given a right to telework 24 working days a year and, in a second draft, a right to request under which the employer could reject a request for any factual reason but had to explain the refusal; neither draft reached consensus within the governing coalition. In Ireland, the government published the Scheme of the Right to Request Remote Working Bill 2022, under which employees with more than six months' service could submit a request, employers would have up to 12 weeks to respond, the bill set out 13 potential grounds for refusal, and an appeals procedure would be available if a request was rejected.

## When It Applies

The Productivity Commission judges that the formal workplace relations system will play only a limited role in working-from-home arrangements, most of which will be negotiated individually or set by workplace policy. It regards the procedural character of the right as appropriate, arguing that a substantive entitlement to work from home would be blunt, costly and contrary to the spirit of experimentation. The absence of a right to request does not stop other workers from asking; it only means the employer is not legally obliged to consider or agree.
