The right to guaranteed hours is a right created in the United Kingdom by the [[Legislation/employment-rights-act-2025]] under which an employer must offer a qualifying worker a new or varied contract guaranteeing a number of hours that reflects the hours the worker worked for that employer during a reference period. Workers in scope are those working on a [[DefinedTerm/zero-hours-contract]] or arrangement, or on a contract guaranteeing hours at or below an "hours threshold" that they exceeded during the reference period; their hours must also meet conditions on number or regularity. The worker can accept the offer or decline it and stay on their existing contract. The duty arises after an initial reference period and again after each subsequent reference period.

## Usage

The right is one of three measures in the Act aimed at [[DefinedTerm/one-sided-flexibility]], alongside a right to reasonable notice of shifts and a right to payment for shifts cancelled, moved or curtailed at short notice. It applies to agency workers too: by default the end hirer must make the offer, and an agency worker who accepts becomes directly engaged by the hirer. Regulations may place the duty on the agency or another intermediary for agency workers of a specified description.

Much of the detail is left to secondary legislation. In its 2026 consultation the UK government stated a preference for an initial reference period of 12 weeks and an hours threshold of between 8 and 20 hours a week, and consulted on the other parameters — regularity tests, how the offer is calculated and the length of subsequent reference periods; see [[Report/right-to-guaranteed-hours-options-assessment]]. Under the Act, an offer should be for a permanent contract unless a limited-term contract is reasonable — where the worker is needed only for a specific task, until a particular event, or for a "temporary need" to be described in regulations. Subsequent reference periods cannot be used to reduce hours already guaranteed, and a worker can complain to an employment tribunal if the employer limited the hours made available for the sole or main purpose of reducing the offer or avoiding one. The right is enforced through Acas early conciliation and employment tribunals rather than by the [[Organization/fair-work-agency]].

The UK government presents the right as a departure from earlier proposals for a right for workers to *request* a more predictable contract: those placed the initiative on the worker, whereas this right places the obligation on the employer, which the government argues removes the imbalance of power involved in having to ask. Its options assessment sets it against other countries' approaches: New Zealand and Norway ban zero-hours contracts outright, Ireland prohibits them with limited exceptions, and in the Netherlands and Finland employers must offer a contract on the terms of a set shift pattern that a worker consistently works.
