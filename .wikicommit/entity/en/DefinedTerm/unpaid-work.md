Unpaid work is productive activity done without pay — domestic work for one's own household, caring for others, and volunteering — which time-use surveys measure alongside, and separately from, paid work. The surveys draw its boundaries differently. In the [[Dataset/abs-time-use-survey]], unpaid work covers domestic activities, looking after children, caring for adults and volunteering. Domestic activities are the unpaid work done to maintain and manage the household, excluding caring responsibilities — cooking and food or drink preparation, housework, shopping, gardening, pet care, household administration, and home and vehicle maintenance. Looking after children under 15 covers activities such as a child's personal care, playing with a child or helping with homework; [[DefinedTerm/supervisory-care-of-children]] while doing something else is measured separately. Unpaid work is one of four broad types of time use, alongside personal care for self, employment and education, and free time. In Mexico's [[Dataset/mexico-national-time-use-survey]] (ENUT), unpaid household work is work done without pay to produce goods or services for the household's own use, for its capital formation or for others. It comprises producing goods for the household's own consumption, unpaid domestic work for one's own household, unpaid care for household members, unpaid support to other family and non-family households, and community and volunteer work. Together with paid work for the market it makes up [[DefinedTerm/total-work-time]]. Statistics Canada, in [[Report/time-use-total-work-burden-unpaid-work-and-leisure]], treats unpaid work as [[DefinedTerm/housework]] and caregiving, and measures it both as a primary activity and as a [[DefinedTerm/simultaneous-activity]] done alongside something else.

## Usage

In 2024, people who did unpaid work spent an average of 4 hours 25 minutes a day on it, according to [[Report/how-australians-use-their-time-2024]]. Females were more likely to do it than males (90% compared with 81%) and spent about an hour more on it (4 hours 53 minutes compared with 3 hours 52 minutes). Parents of children under 15 spent well above the average, and people did less unpaid work on days they also did paid work, but more on workdays spent working from home than on workdays spent elsewhere.

In Mexico, according to [[Report/enut-2024-results-report]], unpaid work made up just over half of the total work time of people aged 12 and over in 2024, and 98.3% of them did some unpaid domestic, care, community or volunteer work. Women spent 66.8% of their total work time on unpaid work and men 33.2%, and women spent 21.5 hours a week more than men on unpaid domestic, care, community and volunteer work — a gap widest in domestic work. The ENUT reports [[DefinedTerm/passive-care]] separately from active care.

In the United States, unpaid care given by women aged 55 or older is the subject of [[Report/older-women-and-unpaid-caregiving-in-the-us]], which draws on the [[Dataset/american-time-use-survey]]. On an average day 23.5% of these women provide unpaid care to a child or an adult, for about 2 hours 11 minutes, and over a year the average woman in this age group gives 182 hours of unpaid care to family and friends. They account for 35.3% of all people providing unpaid [[DefinedTerm/eldercare]] on a given day. The brief argues that this care allows others to stay in paid work, but that the reduced hours, earnings and retirement benefits that come with it weigh on the caregivers themselves; see [[DefinedTerm/caregiving-penalty]].

In Canada, women aged 25 to 54 spent an average of 3.9 hours a day on unpaid work as a primary activity in 2015 and men 2.4 hours, according to [[Report/time-use-total-work-burden-unpaid-work-and-leisure]]. The gap is larger when unpaid work done at the same time as other activities is counted: 60.8% of women and 40.2% of men did unpaid work as a simultaneous activity in 2015, and in 2010 women spent 5.4 hours a day on all unpaid work against men's 2.9. The report notes that the unpaid work women disproportionately do for their families facilitates men's higher labour force participation and longer paid-work hours, and that the mental and emotional work of coordinating children's lives and the household goes unmeasured in time-use surveys.

In Japan, unpaid work falls disproportionately on women and paid work on men, according to [[Report/international-comparison-of-time-use-gender-equality-white-paper-2023]], a column in Japan's White Paper on Gender Equality that compares 11 countries using OECD time-use data. Japan's unpaid working time was the second shortest of the 11 after South Korea's, mainly because Japanese men's was exceptionally short; Japanese women's was not necessarily long by international standards. Paid work leaned towards men and unpaid work towards women in every country compared, but women accounted for 84.6% of unpaid working time in Japan, the largest share of the 11, while in Sweden, Finland and Norway women's shares of paid and unpaid work were close to half. The column argues that unless Japanese men's long paid working hours are reduced it will be difficult for them to do more unpaid work.
