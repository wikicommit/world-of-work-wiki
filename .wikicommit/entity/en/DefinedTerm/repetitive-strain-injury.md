Repetitive strain injury (RSI) is a label for physical complaints for which there is no universally accepted definition. On one hand it is considered an upper-extremity musculoskeletal disorder; on the other, the complaints it covers vary widely in location and background. They can involve static workloads and — sometimes in the same person — repetitive or dynamic workloads, tendon problems such as lateral epicondylitis or carpal tunnel syndrome, and cold environments, and may include inflammatory symptoms in the forearm muscles or problems with local blood flow, especially in the neck and shoulder area. There is consensus that when several of these symptoms occur at the same time in combination with work activities known to cause them, they are called RSI.

## Usage

Because the complaints have multiple causes and effects, many names and classification systems are in use. A well-known classification is CANS — musculoskeletal complaints of the arm, neck and/or shoulder not caused by acute trauma or by any systemic disease. The EU-OSHA report [[Report/work-related-msds-prevalence-costs-and-demographics-in-the-eu]] cites RSI as an example of collective terms being used to classify [[DefinedTerm/work-related-musculoskeletal-disorders]] whose causes can be multiple; syndromes such as RSI/CANS, vibration-induced white finger syndrome, de Quervain's disease and carpal tunnel syndrome are usually used to describe complaints in the upper part of the body.

National data in the report show the term in use: in the Netherlands' register of occupational diseases, repetitive strain injury of the shoulder or upper arm was the most common of the 1,791 MSD-related occupational diseases recorded in 2016 (around 20 %), and the Health Council of the Netherlands has found repetitive movements at work to be a health risk for specific upper-extremity disorders such as carpal tunnel syndrome and lateral epicondylitis ("tennis elbow"). Awareness of RSI has also been put forward as one individual factor that may partly explain why the prevalence of disabling arm and back pain differs between countries among workers doing similar tasks.
