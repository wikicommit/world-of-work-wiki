The potential to work from home is an estimate of whether a job could feasibly be done at home, judged from the tasks its occupation involves rather than from whether the person actually works from home. Occupations whose work typically uses computers and involves little interaction with the public are classed as able to be done from home; those involving outdoor work, physical activity, handling or moving objects, or inspecting equipment, structures or materials are classed as not. Summed across the workforce, the measure indicates how much [[DefinedTerm/working-from-home]] is possible, as distinct from how much takes place.

## Usage

Australia's Productivity Commission, in [[Report/working-from-home-research-paper]], applied a classification developed by Dingel and Neiman — who used detailed United States data describing the tasks involved in around 1000 occupations — to Australian 2016 Census data by matching US occupation codes to the Australian and New Zealand Standard Classification of Occupations. On this basis it estimated that about 35% of Australian workers had jobs amenable to working from home, including about 75% of clerical and administrative workers, 53% of professionals and 52% of managers. The potential was higher among full-time workers and among women, and rose with income and educational attainment; many women work in lower-paid clerical and administrative jobs that can also be done from home. By comparison, only about 5% of workers worked from home on census day in 2016, and about 8% of employees had a formal work-from-home arrangement in 2019.

The Commission used the measure to bound the likely scale of change: if everyone with the potential to work from home did so on a hybrid basis, about 13% of all hours would be worked remotely. It also mapped where people with this potential live, finding them concentrated close to city centres.

## When It Applies

The measure is deliberately blunt. It classifies each occupation as either able or not able to be done from home, whereas many jobs contain some tasks that could be done remotely, so it tends to understate the true potential. It relies on an algorithm that looks only at tasks, which can misclassify some occupations — Dingel and Neiman noted cases, such as preschool and primary teachers, where the result conflicted with their own judgement, and the Commission treated those occupations as not able to be done from home. Applying it outside the United States also assumes that each occupation involves similar tasks in both countries.
