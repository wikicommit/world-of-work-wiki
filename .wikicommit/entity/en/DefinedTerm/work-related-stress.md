Work-related stress can result when pressure at work becomes excessive and a person feels unable to cope. HSE distinguishes this from ordinary pressure, which may motivate performance. Its leaflet [[Report/how-to-tackle-work-related-stress]] treats prevention as a matter of improving working conditions as well as supporting individuals.

## Usage

HSE's [[DefinedTerm/management-standards-for-work-related-stress]] identify six areas of work that can contribute to stress when poorly managed: demands, control, support, relationships, role and change. The leaflet emphasizes assessing these conditions with workers and their representatives, then acting on the findings. It gives line managers a central role because they often see problems first and may be the first contact for a worker experiencing stress.

**EU statistics.** The [[Dataset/eu-lfs-ad-hoc-module-on-accidents-at-work-and-work-related-health-problems]] counts stress, depression or anxiety as one type of self-reported [[DefinedTerm/work-related-health-problem]]. In its 2020 round it was the second most common type in the EU after musculoskeletal disorders, reported by 1.9 % of employed or previously employed people aged 15-64 (1.5 % of men and 2.2 % of women), at a rate of around 2 % across age groups. Eurostat reports the highest share in Sweden (8.6 %), followed by Luxembourg (3.6 %) and Finland (3.5 %), and rates below 0.5 % in several southern and eastern Member States; in most countries the share was higher for women than for men. The same survey found that 44.6 % of the employed were exposed to risk factors for their mental well-being at work, most often time pressure or work overload (19.5 %), dealing with difficult customers, patients or pupils (10.4 %) and job insecurity (6.1 %).

**Great Britain statistics.** For its official statistics HSE defines work-related stress as a harmful reaction people have to undue pressures and demands placed on them at work, and measures it, together with depression and anxiety, from workers' self-reports in the Labour Force Survey. According to [[Report/work-related-stress-depression-or-anxiety-statistics-in-great-britain-2025]], 964,000 workers were suffering from work-related stress, depression or anxiety in 2024/25 (2,770 per 100,000 workers, 409,000 of them new cases), which accounted for 52% of all work-related ill health and 62% of the working days it caused to be lost — 22.1 million days, or 22.9 per case. The rate had shown signs of increasing in the years before the coronavirus pandemic and in each of the latest three years has been above its 2018/19 level. Over 2022/23–2024/25, rates were significantly higher in public administration and defence, human health and social work, and education, in professional and associate professional occupations, among women, and in medium and large workplaces. Workers most often attributed their condition to workload — tight deadlines, too much work or too much pressure or responsibility — followed by a lack of managerial support, violence and bullying, organisational change and role uncertainty.
