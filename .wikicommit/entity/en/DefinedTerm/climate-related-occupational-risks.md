Climate-related occupational risks are the risks to workers' safety and health that climate change makes worse or brings about. The European Agency for Safety and Health at Work (EU-OSHA) describes higher temperatures, UV radiation, air pollution and extreme weather events as affecting occupational safety and health across almost all economic sectors, both by increasing existing risks and by creating new ones — heat-related illnesses, infectious diseases, accidents, allergies and cancer — and notes that climate change can also affect workers' mental health. The consequences it names are higher health costs, lower job quality and lost productivity. EU-OSHA states that one third of EU workers are exposed to risks such as extreme heat, extreme weather events or poor air quality and, citing its 2025 OSH Pulse survey, that 31% of workers are concerned about the impact of environmental risks on their safety and health at work ([[Report/osh-pulse-2025-occupational-safety-and-health-in-the-era-of-climate-and-digital-change]]).

## Usage

EU-OSHA groups the climate-driven impacts into three kinds:

- **Rising temperatures**, a concern for both indoor and outdoor workers. Extreme heat can cause fatigue, dehydration, worsening of chronic diseases and severe heat illness; physical work raises body temperature, and heat stress impairs judgement and increases the risk of accidents. Poor recovery between shifts adds to the burden, especially for workers living in homes that are not adequately cooled.
- **Extreme weather events** such as floods and wildfires, projected to become more frequent and severe across Europe, which cause injuries and deaths and can raise the risk of drowning, burns or frostbite; emergency workers face additional risks from toxic gases, explosions and fire.
- **Air pollution** and rising levels of aeroallergens such as pollen, which raise the risk of respiratory and other illnesses indoors and outdoors.

**Who is at risk.** Outdoor workers are exposed to heat, extreme weather, pollutants and vector-borne diseases: sun exposure raises the risk of skin cancer and impairs cognitive performance, and diseases such as Lyme disease and dengue are spreading geographically. EU-OSHA names farmers and forestry workers (heat stress, tick-borne diseases, pollutants, allergens and the injury and musculoskeletal risks of post-disaster clean-up), construction workers working in urban heat islands, and emergency services such as firefighters, police and medical responders. Indoor workers are vulnerable during heatwaves in poorly ventilated buildings or heat-intensive industries; healthcare workers wearing personal protective equipment in hot conditions may suffer exhaustion and breathing difficulties, and heatwaves increase demand for medical care, creating high stress and workloads, particularly for ageing staff in urban hospitals subject to urban heat islands.

**Prevention and policy.** In EU Member States employers must assess workplace risks and set preventive measures that follow the hierarchy of control, giving priority to technical and organisational measures over personal ones. Member States address climate-related risks at different paces; some set recommended temperature limits in regulations or collective agreements, and some have guidance on heat and UV exposure. Measures named by EU-OSHA include adjusting work schedules, rest areas, hydration, cooling systems and protective clothing, heat action plans, digital tools such as early warning systems and heat alert devices, and consulting and training workers. EU-OSHA also points out that climate change mitigation can itself introduce new risks, for example in renewable energy, green building and recycling.
