Casual employment, as used in Australian labour statistics, is a form of employment for which there is no single statistical measure: the Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS) uses employees who are not entitled to paid holiday or paid sick leave in their main job as a commonly used proxy, and separately asks employees whether they consider their job to be casual. Other characteristics often associated with casual jobs are earnings that vary from one pay period to the next, not usually working the same number of hours each week, having no guaranteed minimum hours, and not expecting to be with the current employer in 12 months' time.

## Usage

The two measures give similar but not identical counts. In August 2024, 2.6 million employees — 22% of employees and 18% of all employed people — had no paid leave entitlements, while 2.4 million (20% of employees) considered their job casual. The proxy measure has been published since 1984: it rose from about 16% of employees in 1984 to around a quarter in the 2000s and 2010s, with women consistently more likely than men to lack paid leave. The absence of paid leave is concentrated among part-time and low-paid employees: 49% of part-time employees had no paid leave entitlements, and only 47% of employees in the lowest-earning quarter had paid sick or holiday leave, compared with 92% of those earning the median wage or more.

Since 2022-23 the ABS has also asked casual employees about their preferences. In 2023-24, 75% said they currently preferred casual employment, most often for flexibility (53%) or a higher hourly pay rate (22%); those who did not prefer it most wanted paid leave entitlements (31%) or more secure employment (21%). Most casual employees (75%) had not discussed converting to non-casual employment with their employer, and the ABS notes that provisions of the Fair Work Act may influence decisions about changing between casual and non-casual employment.
