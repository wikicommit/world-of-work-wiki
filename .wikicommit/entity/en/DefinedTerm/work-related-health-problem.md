In the statistics Eurostat produces from the [[Dataset/eu-lfs-ad-hoc-module-on-accidents-at-work-and-work-related-health-problems]], a work-related health problem is any illness, disability or physical or mental health problem from which a person suffered during the 12 months before the survey and which, in that person's own opinion, was caused or made worse by their current or previous work. The onset may lie further back, provided the person still suffered from the problem during the reference period. Injuries resulting from accidents at work are excluded. The problem need not have been reported, complained about or recognised by the authorities, and need not have led to sick leave or medical treatment; its consequences may be private as well as work-related. Where a person has more than one such problem, the survey records only the most serious. The concept is therefore broader than a recognised [[DefinedTerm/occupational-disease]] and differs from an [[DefinedTerm/accident-at-work]].

## Usage

The term is used to measure the self-reported burden of work-related ill health across EU and EFTA countries, broken down by type of problem, sex, age, country, sector and occupation, and complemented by measures of how far the problem limits daily activities and how long it keeps people off work. In 2020, 10.3 % of people in the EU aged 15-64 who had worked reported a work-related health problem in the previous year (10.8 % of women and 9.8 % of men), rising with age from 6.5 % among 15-34-year-olds to 14.1 % among 55-64-year-olds. More than two-thirds (71.0 %) of those affected had some or severe limitations in daily activities as a result. Half had less than a day or no time off work, while 10 % were absent for more than six months. By type, [[DefinedTerm/work-related-musculoskeletal-disorders]] were by far the most common (6.0 %), followed by [[DefinedTerm/work-related-stress]], depression or anxiety (1.9 %) and headaches, eye-strain or migraine (0.7 %). Agriculture, forestry and fishing (15.8 %) and skilled agricultural and fishery workers (16.9 %) had the highest rates by sector and occupation.

Because the concept rests on self-perception, Eurostat cautions that differences between countries — in 2020 the share ranged from 1.8 % in Lithuania to 25.7 % in Finland, with Poland's higher 36.6 % not fully comparable because of a different national implementation of the questionnaire — may reflect differences in awareness, treatment, compensation, reporting and prevention, and cultural differences in how people perceive their health and are willing to share information on it, as well as differences in working conditions and economic structures.
