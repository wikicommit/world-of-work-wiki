An Article 36 agreement (時間外労働協定, commonly called a "36 agreement" or *saburoku kyōtei*) is the labour–management agreement that, in Japan, makes overtime and holiday work lawful. Employers may not, as a rule, have employees work more than 8 hours a day or 40 hours a week, and must give at least one day off a week or four days off over four weeks; where the employer concludes an agreement setting out overtime and holiday work with either a trade union organised by a majority of the workers or a person representing a majority of the workers, and files it with the government authority, overtime beyond the statutory hours and work on statutory days off are permitted.

## Usage

The name comes from Article 36 of the Labour Standards Act, which provides for the agreement. The agreement does not remove all limits: the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare notes that limits are set on overtime hours, and it presents the upper limit on overtime work as a separate policy.

## Limit standards and premium pay (as described in 2015)

A fiscal year 2015 seminar pamphlet on preventing overwork, produced under a commission from the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare, describes the rules then attached to the agreement. Article 32 of the Labour Standards Act sets the statutory working hours at 40 hours a week and 8 hours a day, excluding breaks, and the pamphlet stresses that Article 36 is not meant to allow unlimited overtime and holiday work: both should be kept to the minimum necessary, and labour and management should conclude the agreement with this in mind.

Under the standard on limits to the extension of working hours (Ministry of Labour Notice No. 154 of 28 December 1998, last amended in 2009), labour and management must make the agreement conform to the standard (Article 36(3)). The agreement must set limits both for a period of more than one day and up to three months and for one year, and the extension it allows may not exceed the following, which do not include work on statutory days off:

| Period | General workers | Workers on a one-year variable working-hours system with a reference period over three months |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 week | 15 hours | 14 hours |
| 2 weeks | 27 hours | 25 hours |
| 4 weeks | 43 hours | 40 hours |
| 1 month | 45 hours | 42 hours |
| 2 months | 81 hours | 75 hours |
| 3 months | 120 hours | 110 hours |
| 1 year | 360 hours | 320 hours |

Overtime and holiday work must be paid at a premium: at least 25% for overtime (at least 50% for overtime beyond 60 hours a month, with application deferred for small and medium-sized enterprises) and at least 35% for holiday work. Like the rules of employment and other labour–management agreements, the agreement must be made known to workers, for example by posting it in a conspicuous place in each workplace or handing out copies.

The pamphlet treats these limits as a reference point for preventing overwork: its example of a workplace policy on overwork sets overtime at, in principle, no more than 45 hours a month, and it links hours above that level to health measures such as [[DefinedTerm/physician-interview-guidance-for-long-working-hours]] ([[DefinedTerm/karoshi]]).
