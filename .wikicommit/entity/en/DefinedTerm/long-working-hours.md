In the WHO/ILO Joint Estimates of the Work-related Burden of Disease and Injury, exposure to long working hours is defined as working 55 hours or more per week. It is treated as an occupational risk factor in its own right — one of two recently added to the joint estimates, linked to the health outcomes of stroke and ischaemic heart disease on the basis of WHO/ILO systematic reviews and meta-analyses — and it is the occupational risk factor with the largest attributable burden of deaths.

## Usage

**Measured burden.** The joint estimates attribute 744 924 deaths and 23.26 million disability-adjusted life years in 2016 to exposure to long working hours — 398 306 deaths from stroke and 346 618 from ischaemic heart disease. These represent 6.9% of all stroke deaths and 3.7% of all ischaemic heart disease deaths worldwide. Deaths attributable to long working hours rose between 2000 and 2016 — by 19.0% for stroke and 41.6% for ischaemic heart disease — and long working hours drove most of the increase in the total number of work-related deaths over that period. The burden falls disproportionately on males, who accounted for about 69% of the stroke deaths and 76% of the ischaemic heart disease deaths, on people of older working age, and on the WHO South-East Asia and Western Pacific regions. Exposure was modelled from 2324 surveys, mostly Labour Force Surveys, from 154 countries, areas and territories, together with 1742 quarterly Labour Force Survey datasets from 46 countries.

**Prevention.** WHO and the ILO point to ILO Conventions No. 1 and No. 30, which set maximum limits on working hours in industry and in commerce and offices, with a weekly average that should not exceed 48 hours apart from specific exceptions, and to other ILO standards on working time, holidays and weekly rest. They also point to human resources and work organization management, an adequate balance between working and personal life — particularly in teleworking, self-employment and freelancing — occupational health assessments that consider working hours alongside other cardiovascular risk factors, and social protection floors that provide income so that workers can stop working unhealthy long hours.
