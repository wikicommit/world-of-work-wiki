Occupational exposure to generative AI (GenAI) is the extent to which the tasks making up an occupation could potentially be performed with GenAI technology. In the ILO's global index, set out in [[ScholarlyArticle/generative-ai-and-jobs-a-refined-global-index-of-occupational-exposure]], each task receives a score from 0 (cannot be automated) to 1 (could be fully automated, without a human operator), and an occupation's exposure is judged from the mean and the standard deviation of its task scores. The measure captures potential exposure, not actual employment effects: the ILO presents its estimates as an upper threshold of the employment that could be affected if the technology were fully implemented.

## Usage

**Exposure gradients.** The 2025 version of the ILO index classifies occupations into four progressively increasing gradients of exposure, alongside two lower categories:

- **Gradient 4** (highest exposure, low task variability): mean task score of at least 0.6 and mean minus one standard deviation of at least 0.5 — most current tasks have high automation potential.
- **Gradient 3** (significant exposure, high task variability): mean of at least 0.5 but below 0.6, with mean plus one standard deviation of at least 0.5.
- **Gradient 2** (moderate exposure, high task variability): mean of at least 0.4 but below 0.5, with mean plus one standard deviation of at least 0.5.
- **Gradient 1** (low exposure, high task variability): mean below 0.4, with mean plus one standard deviation of at least 0.5 — some tasks have elevated automation potential, but the occupation remains reliant on tasks with low potential.
- **Minimal exposure**: mean below 0.5 and mean plus one standard deviation above 0.4.
- **Not exposed**: occupations meeting none of these conditions.

This scheme replaced the ILO's 2023 framework, which used "automation" and "augmentation" potential as two extreme categories with a "big unknown" in between; gradient 4 corresponds closely to the earlier notion of automation risk and gradient 1 to augmentation. The ILO argues that the gradients make results easier to interpret, allow employment in exposed occupations to be added together, and permit tracking of occupations over time and comparison across national classifications.

**Interpretation.** Because nearly all occupations contain some tasks requiring human input, the ILO treats the "bundle of tasks" that makes up an occupation as a buffer against full automation and regards transformation of jobs as the most likely impact. It cautions nonetheless that efficiency gains may reduce demand for workers in more exposed occupations, and that adoption depends on factors the index does not capture, such as infrastructure, digital skills, cost and the institutional and social acceptance of automating tasks. Under the 2025 index, about one in four workers worldwide are in an exposed occupation, with higher shares among women and in high-income countries.
