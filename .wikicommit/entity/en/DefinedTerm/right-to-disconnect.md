The right to disconnect is an entitlement for workers to switch off from work outside their scheduled hours — not to be contacted, and not to be required to read or respond to work emails, telephone calls or other forms of contact, during leave, rest days and other non-working time. A corollary is that workers should not be encouraged to be, or rewarded for being, constantly connected to work systems. Eurofound defines it as the right of workers "to be able to disengage from work and refrain from engaging in work-related electronic communications, such as emails or other messages, during non-work hours".

## Usage

The right did not originate with remote or hybrid work, but it has become particularly relevant to [[DefinedTerm/working-from-home]], where the absence of a commute removes the cues that mark the end of the working day and the idea of being "finished for the day" is harder to enforce. Australia's Productivity Commission, in [[Report/working-from-home-research-paper]], links the loss of that boundary to longer working days, difficulty switching off, stress, strained relationships at home and, in some cases, burnout, and cites research finding that senders of after-hours work emails underestimate how compelled recipients feel to respond straight away.

According to the Commission, a right to disconnect has been part of general French labour law since 2016, while in Australia the idea gained prominence in the union movement only recently. The Australian Council of Trade Unions (ACTU), which has proposed a Working from Home Charter of rights and protections for people working from home, has argued for legal and reasonable limits on working time, including a right to disconnect from work emails, calls and other contact outside scheduled hours; unions have also proposed the right as part of employment agreements. The 2019 Victoria Police enterprise agreement, approved in March 2020, makes it a mandatory condition of employment: supervisors must respect employees' leave and rest days; outside emergencies or genuine welfare matters, employees must not be contacted outside their hours of work unless they receive an availability allowance; and employees are not required to read or respond to emails or calls outside their working hours.

The Commission also notes an economic dimension: workers who are explicitly on call are usually paid an allowance, and if work hours routinely bleed into recreation time when working from home, the distinction on which that premium rests could fade, transferring welfare from workers to employers.

In Europe, Eurofound's report [[Report/right-to-disconnect-implementation-and-impact-at-company-level]] finds that, as of spring 2023, there was no EU legislation specifically on the right, although several EU directives, notably the Working Time Directive, contain relevant provisions. By June 2023 nine Member States had legislation providing a right to disconnect — Belgium, Croatia, France, Greece, Italy, Luxembourg, Portugal, Slovakia and Spain — and Ireland had adopted a code of practice in 2021. According to the report, France was the first European country to introduce the right, through a provision in its labour code in 2016 that came into force on 1 January 2017. National provisions differ in whom they cover (in some countries only teleworkers or workers using digital tools), in how they are implemented — directly applicable, as in Portugal, where the law imposes a duty on employers to refrain from contacting employees during rest periods, and in Slovakia, or through negotiations at company or sectoral level with a company policy or charter as a fall-back, as in Belgium, France, Luxembourg and Spain — and in the sanctions attached, which are rarely specific.

At company level, Eurofound distinguishes "soft" approaches, such as awareness raising, training, agreed hours of availability and managing out-of-hours communication, from "hard" approaches that sever the connection between message systems and devices. In its December 2022 survey of employees in Belgium, France, Italy and Spain, the measures employees in companies with a policy most often reported were the automatic deletion of emails received during holidays (40%) and stopping work email delivery at certain times (32%), while training for managers (19%) and employees (12%) was least often reported; only half of respondents in companies with a policy were aware of any action taken to implement it.

## When It Applies

The Commission's view is that a separate legal right would be needed in Australia only as a preventative measure if evidence showed employees facing unreasonable pressure to stay available despite existing protections, because the model work health and safety laws already cover psychological health and employers' duty to address stress and burnout risks is well established in case law. It emphasises workplace culture — clear expectations about when and how business communication happens, and employees setting their own boundaries — and says it will be important for regulators and workers' compensation insurers to monitor whether more working from home is associated with more psychological injury. This is the Commission's assessment; the ACTU, by contrast, argues that legal limits on working time are needed.

Eurofound's survey evidence points in a related direction. Having a company policy did not appear to reduce how often workers were contacted out of hours or how often they responded, and only 17% of respondents said they worked additional hours mainly because they were contacted out of hours. But workers in companies with a policy less often worked additional hours because of out-of-hours contact (14% against 19%), were more likely to be compensated for additional hours, more often reported a good fit between working hours and private life (92% against 80%), reported fewer health problems and were more often very satisfied with their working conditions; they also reported more autonomy over their working hours, suggesting the right does not curb desired flexibility. Eurofound concludes that a policy on its own is insufficient to change workplace culture: it needs awareness raising, effective implementation measures, joint monitoring by management and employee representatives, and action on other causes of over-connection such as high workloads. It notes that its survey is a non-probability online panel covering selected sectors in four countries, so the associations it reports come with those limitations.
