Underemployment describes people in employment who want to work more hours than they actually do. It is measured in labour force surveys, expressed as a share of people in employment, and defined differently by different statistical offices. As Insee defines it, underemployment (*sous-emploi*) covers people with a part-time job who want to work more hours and are available to do so, whether or not they are looking for a job, and also people who involuntarily worked less than usual — because of short-time working (*chômage partiel*), for example — whether they work full time or part time; it is measured with the French Labour Force Survey (enquête Emploi). Statistics Sweden (SCB) asks respondents to the Swedish Labour Force Survey (AKU) how many hours they usually work per week and how many they would like to work, and counts as underemployed (*undersysselsatta*) everyone whose wish for more weekly hours is not met — from people working a few hours a week to people working full time — and counts them in the unused labour supply together with the unemployed and latent job seekers.

## Usage

Insee presents underemployment alongside the part-time rate. It consists essentially of part-time workers who would like to work more and are available to do so (compare [[DefinedTerm/involuntary-part-time-work]]), together with people on short-time working, whose number rose sharply during the health crisis. According to [[Report/snapshot-of-the-labour-market-in-2024]], 4.3% of people in employment in France were underemployed in 2024, stable after three years of decline, 1.5 points below the pre-crisis level and the lowest since 1992, even as the share of part-time work began to rise again. It was more common among low-skilled clerical and service employees (12.3%), young people (6.9%) and women (6.1% against 2.6% of men).

In 2025, according to [[Report/snapshot-of-the-labour-market-in-2025]], underemployment was almost stable at 4.4% of people in employment (+0.1 point), still 1.4 points below its pre-crisis level, while part-time work continued to rise moderately; people on short-time working had become a very small minority of the underemployed. It again concerned low-skilled clerical and service employees (12.3%), young people aged 15 to 24 (7.6%) and women (6.1% against 2.8% of men) more than others.

In Sweden, according to [[Report/more-people-want-to-work-more-in-hard-times]], about six per cent of the employed want to work more hours per week than they usually do. Underemployment turned upward in 2018 after several years of decline, rose by about 2 percentage points between 2019 and 2020 as employers cut working time rather than staff during the pandemic, and has risen again in recent years, which SCB attributes to inflation and higher living costs. In 2024 it was far more common among people usually working part time (18.4%) than full time (3.4%), and among temporary employees (20.2%) than permanent employees (4.7%). The gap between women and men has narrowed since the early 2000s, when 8–9% of women and 3–4% of men were underemployed, and the extra hours the underemployed want have fallen from just over 12 to about 9 per week.
