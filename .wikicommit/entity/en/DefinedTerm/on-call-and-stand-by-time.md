In EU working time law, on-call and stand-by time are periods during which a worker has to remain available to resume work in case of need. "On-call" describes duty during which the worker must stay at the workplace or another place determined by the employer; "stand-by" describes duty during which the worker must be reachable at all times and ready to respond within a set time, but is not required to stay at a place the employer determines. Because the [[Legislation/working-time-directive]] recognises no intermediate category between [[DefinedTerm/working-time-and-rest-period]], each such period must be classified as one or the other, and the Court of Justice of the European Union has built up detailed case-law on how to do so, which the European Commission summarises in its [[Report/interpretative-communication-on-the-working-time-directive]].

## Usage

**On-call time at the workplace.** In rulings concerning doctors in primary care teams and in hospitals and a teacher in residential establishments for people with disabilities (including cases C-303/98, Simap, and C-151/02, Landeshauptstadt Kiel), the Court held that on-call time during which workers are required to be present at the workplace is working time in its entirety. The workers must be available at a place determined by the employer for the whole period and are kept away from their family and social environment, and excluding such time would seriously undermine the Directive's protection of health and safety. It makes no difference whether the worker actually works during the period, whether the period includes inactivity, or whether a rest room is available where the worker can sleep. Systems of "equivalence" that count on-call hours at a reduced ratio are incompatible with the Directive if they do not ensure compliance with all its minimum requirements. Where the workplace is also, or cannot be distinguished from, the worker's home, the obligation to stay there does not by itself make the time working time; the classification then depends on the intensity of the employer's constraints, as for stand-by.

**Stand-by time.** Since its 2018 judgment in Ville de Nivelles (C-518/15), concerning a volunteer firefighter under Belgian law, and several later rulings, the Court has held that stand-by time is working time in its entirety only where the constraints imposed on the worker objectively and very significantly affect the possibility of freely managing the time during which their services are not required and pursuing their own personal and social interests. Otherwise, only the time actually spent providing services counts as working time. National courts must assess this case by case. The Court has identified two elements, either of which in principle makes the whole period working time: a very short time — a few minutes — within which the worker must return to work, and a high frequency of call-outs combined with a non-negligible duration of the work performed, where that frequency can be objectively estimated. Even a worker rarely called out is not resting if the response time very significantly constrains the use of their time; conversely, even where one of the two elements is present, facilities or leeway granted to the worker may make the inactive part of stand-by a rest period.

Only constraints and facilities that come from the employer, legislation or collective agreements are taken into account. Relevant constraints include an obligation to carry specific equipment or penalties for exceeding the response time; relevant facilities include the use of a service vehicle with special traffic rights, no obligation to respond to every call, or the possibility of carrying on another professional activity during stand-by. Constraints not imposed in this way — natural factors such as a remote location with limited leisure possibilities, or the worker's own choices — are irrelevant, as is the provision of service accommodation at or next to the workplace. Where stand-by takes place during a break, the Court looks only at the constraints resulting from having to be ready for a call-out within a very short time: a break during which a worker must be able to respond within two minutes has been held to be working time.

**Limits on stand-by classed as rest.** The Commission stresses that classifying stand-by as rest does not give employers a free hand over its length and timing. Under the framework directive on safety and health at work, employers must evaluate and prevent all risks to workers' health and safety, including psychosocial risks such as stress or burnout; workers must be able to withdraw from the working environment for enough consecutive hours to neutralise the effects of work, so employers cannot impose stand-by so long or so frequent that it puts workers' health at risk. The Commission notes that the Court appears to recognise that the obligation to be contactable and to react — whose corollary is the deprivation of the [[DefinedTerm/right-to-disconnect]] — may harm workers' health and safety if imposed too frequently, even when the time counts as rest. It also considers that a worker cannot be on stand-by during a period of equivalent compensatory rest, whether or not the stand-by would count as working time. How on-call and stand-by time are paid is outside the Directive, which does not govern remuneration.

**In ILO standards and platform work.** Outside EU law, the International Labour Office's report [[Report/realizing-decent-work-in-the-platform-economy-2024]] (2024) recalls that the Hours of Work (Commerce and Offices) Convention, 1930 (No. 30), defines "hours of work" as "the time during which the persons employed are at the disposal of the employer". The ILO's Committee of Experts on the Application of Conventions and Recommendations (CEACR) has not addressed "waiting time" in platform work specifically, but it has discussed time spent "on call" or "on standby" and concluded that it may or may not be regarded as hours of work under the Convention, depending on the extent to which the worker is restricted from engaging in personal activities during that time; where it is not regarded as hours of work, the employee should still be entitled to some payment in recognition of the time spent on call. Because Convention No. 30 applies to employees only, the report treats waiting periods on platforms, and their remuneration, as a normative gap. Such unpaid time can be substantial: ILO surveys found that workers on microtask platforms spend about 20 minutes on unpaid work for every hour worked or task paid for, and on location-based delivery platforms unpaid time includes waiting between assignments. Few regulations address it. In Chile, working time for platform workers classified as employees is defined as the time during which the worker is at the disposal of the platform company, from access to the digital infrastructure until they voluntarily disconnect; recording it is mandatory and must distinguish "passive" working time — at the platform's disposal without performing work, for reasons not attributable to the worker — from "active" working time, and the law provides that the hourly remuneration of active working time "must not be less than 120 per cent of the minimum monthly income", to account for waiting time. The report's questionnaire asked governments whether a new standard should define hours of work for platform workers as the time they are at the disposal of a platform, "including when they are waiting for work assignments", and whether Members should establish a method to determine their remuneration for such periods.
