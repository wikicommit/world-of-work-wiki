Teleworkability is the property of a job that can be performed remotely: according to the European Commission, a job is "teleworkable" when its remote performance is technically feasible and there is no significant loss of quality due to telework. It describes what is possible, not how much telework actually takes place.

## Usage

In its 2024 consultation document [[Report/first-phase-consultation-on-telework-and-the-right-to-disconnect]], the Commission refers to a teleworkability framework proposed by its Joint Research Centre, which distinguishes three main types of occupation:

- highly teleworkable occupations, involving intellectual tasks and little social interaction (for example finance professionals, ICT professionals and office clerks);
- occupations that are teleworkable but with a loss of quality because they need more social interaction, typically managers and teachers;
- non-teleworkable occupations, which require workers to be physically present at the workplace, such as nurses, manufacturing workers and drivers.

On this basis around 37% of jobs in the EU are estimated to be teleworkable, and the overall prevalence of telework is well below that share. The Commission notes that countries with higher proportions of teleworkers are those with the largest shares of employment in knowledge- and ICT-intensive services, but that telework varies considerably between Member States even within the same sector or occupation, and that larger businesses adopt it more readily than smaller ones. It concludes that implementing telework is not simply a matter of teleworkability: the regulatory framework, digital infrastructure and broadband, collective bargaining, managerial culture, workers' preferences and socio-economic status, teleworker "stigma", gender stereotypes and businesses' ability to redesign jobs all play a role.
