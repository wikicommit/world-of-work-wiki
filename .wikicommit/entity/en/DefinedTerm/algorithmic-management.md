Algorithmic management (AM) is commonly understood as the use of automated monitoring systems and automated decision-making systems that replace functions managers usually perform — allocating tasks, pricing individual assignments, determining working schedules, giving instructions, evaluating the work performed, providing incentives or applying adverse treatment. It may or may not be driven by artificial intelligence. The European Commission distinguishes it from AI adoption in general, which covers the use of AI across business functions such as product development, logistics optimisation or customer analytics, and from the use of generative AI at work: algorithmic management specifically concerns the automated management of the workforce.

## Usage

In its 2026 consultation document on a Quality Jobs Act, [[Report/second-phase-consultation-quality-jobs-act]], the Commission describes algorithmic management as spreading beyond the platform economy into many sectors, and as particularly prevalent in financial services, transport and industry. It gives estimates that automated performance review systems are used for 18–25% of workers in the EU and automated workforce scheduling covers 10–29%.

The Commission presents it as a source of efficiency — cutting idle time, predicting staff turnover, detecting skills gaps, allocating shifts — but also of risk when poorly designed or implemented. Its ongoing analysis finds exposure to algorithmic management associated with lower autonomy, work intensification and higher quantitative, emotional and time-related demands, with performance monitoring particularly impactful; mis-designed scheduling can produce unpredictable schedules, and fine-grained tracking can intrude on privacy and create stress and anxiety. It cites the French data protection authority's finding that a warehouse operator tracked whether an item was stored within 1.25 seconds of the previous one. Existing safeguards come mainly from the GDPR, the AI Act (which prohibits emotion recognition in the workplace) and EU information and consultation rules, but the Commission notes that these have limitations and are complex to navigate, and that there are no employment-specific rules on practices such as neurotechnology or predicting individual workers' behaviour.

The avenues it puts to the social partners centre on transparency and information, human review of significant automated decisions (with decisions such as promotion, disciplinary action or dismissal taken by a human exercising genuine discretion), limits on disproportionate practices, and a stronger role for workers' representatives in reviewing systems. According to the document, trade unions called for binding EU legislation modelled on the Platform Work Directive, while employer organisations opposed new regulation and favoured guidance and better implementation of existing rules.
