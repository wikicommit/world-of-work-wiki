Algorithmic management (AM) is commonly understood as the use of automated monitoring systems and automated decision-making systems that replace functions managers usually perform — allocating tasks, pricing individual assignments, determining working schedules, giving instructions, evaluating the work performed, providing incentives or applying adverse treatment. It may or may not be driven by artificial intelligence. The European Commission distinguishes it from AI adoption in general, which covers the use of AI across business functions such as product development, logistics optimisation or customer analytics, and from the use of generative AI at work: algorithmic management specifically concerns the automated management of the workforce.

## Usage

In its 2026 consultation document on a Quality Jobs Act, [[Report/second-phase-consultation-quality-jobs-act]], the Commission describes algorithmic management as spreading beyond the platform economy into many sectors, and as particularly prevalent in financial services, transport and industry. It gives estimates that automated performance review systems are used for 18–25% of workers in the EU and automated workforce scheduling covers 10–29%.

The Commission presents it as a source of efficiency — cutting idle time, predicting staff turnover, detecting skills gaps, allocating shifts — but also of risk when poorly designed or implemented. Its ongoing analysis finds exposure to algorithmic management associated with lower autonomy, work intensification and higher quantitative, emotional and time-related demands, with performance monitoring particularly impactful; mis-designed scheduling can produce unpredictable schedules, and fine-grained tracking can intrude on privacy and create stress and anxiety. It cites the French data protection authority's finding that a warehouse operator tracked whether an item was stored within 1.25 seconds of the previous one. Existing safeguards come mainly from the GDPR, the AI Act (which prohibits emotion recognition in the workplace) and EU information and consultation rules, but the Commission notes that these have limitations and are complex to navigate, and that there are no employment-specific rules on practices such as neurotechnology or predicting individual workers' behaviour.

The avenues it puts to the social partners centre on transparency and information, human review of significant automated decisions (with decisions such as promotion, disciplinary action or dismissal taken by a human exercising genuine discretion), limits on disproportionate practices, and a stronger role for workers' representatives in reviewing systems. According to the document, trade unions called for binding EU legislation modelled on the Platform Work Directive, while employer organisations opposed new regulation and favoured guidance and better implementation of existing rules.

**On digital labour platforms.** The International Labour Office's report [[Report/realizing-decent-work-in-the-platform-economy-2024]] (2024) treats algorithms as a defining feature of digital platforms. It notes that in many platforms it is an algorithm that offers and grants tasks, defines working time, calculates remuneration, implements ratings and rankings, and ultimately determines whether workers continue to provide services. Location-based platforms commonly use geolocation, delivery time mediation and availability monitoring, while online platforms often use periodic and random screenshots, typing time tracking, daily task logs or task quality control; customer ratings and rankings automate human supervision. The report links these practices to less transparent and sometimes personalized pay rates, pressure to maintain high acceptance rates and work unsocial and variable hours, the risk that customer reviews and biased data reproduce discrimination, psychosocial risks, and [[DefinedTerm/account-deactivation]] without human oversight. It also observes that courts have relied on control exercised through algorithms and geolocation tools to conclude that platform workers were not independent contractors, and it identifies the use of algorithms as a gap that no international labour standard specifically addresses.

The national measures it reviews focus mainly on transparency. Spanish law requires companies to inform workers' representatives of "the parameters, rules and instructions on which algorithms or artificial intelligence systems are based" when these affect working conditions or access to and maintenance of employment; in Malta, platform workers are entitled to an explanation of automated decisions affecting their conditions and to a contact person to discuss them, and platforms must evaluate the risks of automated monitoring and decision-making systems to workers' safety and health, with systems that put undue pressure on workers prohibited; Croatian employment contracts must name a contact person authorized to supervise an automated management system; and in China platform workers have a right to consultation on platform algorithms. In the Netherlands, two 2023 judgments of the Amsterdam Court of Appeal required two transport platforms to give workers "useful" information about their algorithms, and in Italy the Labour Court of Palermo held in 2023 that a delivery platform's refusal to give trade unions legally required information on its algorithm was contrary to freedom of association, while a Bologna court had found a delivery platform's algorithmic allocation of time slots, which downgraded workers who were absent for reasons such as strike action, illness or family care, to be discriminatory. Collective bargaining has addressed the subject too: the agreement between the CCOO and UGT trade union confederations and Just Eat in Spain requires the platform to give workers' representatives the relevant information used by its algorithm to organize work and sets up an "algorithm committee" of two platform and two trade union representatives.
