Overtime is work performed in excess of ordinary working time — the hours a worker is normally required to work under the law, a collective agreement or the employment contract. Because it extends working time beyond the normal schedule, it is usually subject to a limit on how many extra hours may be worked and to rules on how those hours are compensated. Eurofound defines overtime work as work performed by an employee in excess of the normal hours of work that has been officially requested and approved by management, that is not part of the employee's regularly scheduled working week, and for which the employee may be compensated.

## Usage

In Spain, overtime is governed by Article 35 of the [[Legislation/workers-statute]]. According to the Spanish Government's information page on working hours, leave and holidays, overtime is voluntary unless a collective agreement or individual employment contract provides otherwise. It must be compensated either with pay at no less than the rate for ordinary hours or with paid time off, which, in the absence of an agreement, must be granted within the four months after the overtime is worked. The maximum is 80 hours a year, not counting hours worked to prevent or repair accidents and other extraordinary and urgent damage; for workers whose annual working time is shorter than the company's general working time, the cap is reduced in the same proportion, and hours compensated by time off within four months do not count towards it. To calculate overtime, working time is recorded day by day and reflected in the payslip.

In the United States, the [[Legislation/fair-labor-standards-act]] (FLSA) takes a different approach, regulating overtime through pay rather than through a cap on hours. According to the US Department of Labor's Wage and Hour Division, unless specifically exempted, covered employees must receive overtime pay for hours worked over 40 in a workweek at no less than one and one-half times their [[DefinedTerm/regular-rate-of-pay]], and the Act sets no limit on the number of hours employees aged 16 and older may work in a week. It does not require overtime pay for work on Saturdays, Sundays, holidays or regular days of rest as such. Overtime is assessed workweek by workweek — a fixed, regularly recurring period of 168 hours that need not coincide with the calendar week — and averaging hours over two or more weeks is not permitted; overtime pay earned in a workweek must normally be paid on the regular payday for that pay period. The requirement cannot be waived by agreement between employer and employees, and an employer's announcement that overtime is not permitted, or will not be paid unless authorised in advance, does not remove the right to be paid for overtime hours that are actually worked.

Across Europe, Eurofound's comparative report [[Report/overtime-in-europe-regulation-and-practice]] finds that overtime is regulated in all EU Member States, but in different ways. The EU Working Time Directive does not define overtime; it limits it indirectly through a maximum average working week of 48 hours including overtime and a minimum daily rest of 11 consecutive hours. Some countries treat overtime as a normal part of an employee's duties and others as an exceptional measure, and the right to refuse it is seldom absolute. Default annual limits range from 80 hours in Spain to 250 hours in Hungary and can often be extended by agreement. The most common premium is 50% of regular pay, with time off in lieu usually attracting the same premium, although premiums range from 10% to 150% in specific circumstances, and Germany, Ireland, the Netherlands and the UK give no statutory entitlement to a premium. Managers are often exempt from the rules, while some countries preclude groups such as pregnant workers, parents of young children and young workers from working overtime.

Eurofound notes that there are no harmonised regular EU data on overtime and that national figures are not comparable, but that in most countries with data the share of employees working overtime with some regularity reaches two digits, with men, prime-age workers and those with higher education more likely to report it. For employers, overtime is a flexible way to adjust capacity; for workers it can mean extra income but also less free time and rest, poorer work–life balance and greater fatigue. The report summarises research showing that overtime and long working days harm physical and mental health with dose–response effects, and notes that compensation does not undo that harm. It also highlights unpaid overtime, which it finds pervasive where data exist and generally the result of pressure on workers; structural overtime, where employers continuously rely on extra hours instead of recruiting staff; and "grey overtime", extra hours that are not recorded, which the report links to the blurring of boundaries between working life and private life as remote working grows.
