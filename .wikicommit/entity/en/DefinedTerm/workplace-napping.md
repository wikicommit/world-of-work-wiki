Workplace napping is the practice of letting workers take a short sleep during a break at work to temporarily reduce the effects of fatigue. WorkSafe New Zealand's guidance [[Report/managing-the-risks-of-shift-work]] describes napping at work as an effective strategy for this, including before driving home from work, and recommends that employers provide suitable and safe facilities where workers can nap when they choose, particularly before travelling home after a long shift.

## Usage

The guidance discusses napping as one of the control measures for [[DefinedTerm/shift-work]], especially for shifts longer than eight hours. For how napping should be organised, the guidance lists the following: workers should submit a fatigue report when they need to nap, through a process simple enough not to discourage napping; they should tell the shift supervisor where and when they plan to nap and when they return; naps should happen only during a break cleared with the supervisor, so that the worker's absence does not increase fatigue for others on the shift; workers should spend no more than 40 minutes trying to sleep and allow time to recover from sleep inertia; napping procedures should be covered in training; and suitable facilities should be provided.

## When It Applies

Napping applies where workers are fatigued during or at the end of a shift — for example on long or night shifts — and the workplace can release them for a break without increasing fatigue for colleagues. It assumes a policy and procedures governing its use, supervisor agreement and suitable facilities. The guidance warns that its effect on fatigue is only temporary, that it should not be used as a routine strategy for extending shifts, and that it requires policy and procedures around its use ([[DefinedTerm/rest-breaks-at-work]], [[DefinedTerm/sleep-debt]]). Waking from a nap also brings sleep inertia, so time must be allowed to recover before returning to work.
