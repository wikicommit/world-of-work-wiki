The National Standard of Canada for Psychological Health and Safety in the Workplace is a set of voluntary guidelines for Canadian employers on protecting and promoting workers' psychological health and safety. It was published in 2013 by the Canadian Standards Association (CSA) Group and the Bureau de normalisation du Québec (BNQ), commissioned by the Mental Health Commission of Canada. Drawing on the social sciences, law and the workplace health and safety literature, it identifies 13 workplace psychosocial factors affecting psychological health and safety, which employers should measure and monitor with the aim of continuous improvement: psychological support; organizational culture; clear leadership and expectations; civility and respect; psychological competencies and requirements; growth and development; recognition and reward; involvement and influence; workload management; engagement; balance; psychological protection; and protection of physical safety.

## Usage

Statistics Canada researchers took the standard as the starting point for developing a tool to measure psychological health and safety among federal public service employees. Writing in [[ScholarlyArticle/measuring-workplace-psychosocial-factors-in-the-federal-government]], they also argue that providing a psychologically healthy and safe workplace is also fast becoming a legal imperative in Canada, given developments in labour law, employment standards and human-rights decisions.

Read through the [[DefinedTerm/job-demands-resources-model]], all 13 factors are job resources. Turning them into a survey instrument has proved difficult. A 141-item survey aligned with the standard was built for the Department of National Defence and the Canadian Armed Forces; for the wider public service, Statistics Canada researchers instead mapped items of the [[Dataset/public-service-employee-survey]] onto the factors and could measure only 10 of the 13 — psychological support, engagement, and psychological competencies and requirements were not captured. The same study found moderate correlations among the factors, suggesting some redundancy, and recognition and reward and involvement and influence emerged as the factors most strongly associated with [[DefinedTerm/job-satisfaction]].

Employers interviewed in an earlier qualitative study, as summarized in that article, found that the standard resonates with organizational values and can give direction for positive change, but that the scope and complexity of the 13 factors and of the standard's documentation present challenges. The article's authors suggest that future research might identify and focus on a core set of factors that emerge as consistent predictors of important outcomes, as a way of limiting the effort and complexity of measuring, reporting on and improving all 13.
