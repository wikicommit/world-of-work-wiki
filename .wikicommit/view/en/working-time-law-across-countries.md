The wiki holds working-time law for the European Union, the United Kingdom, Spain, Germany, France, Japan, the United States and Canada (federal jurisdiction). This page sets them side by side along the dimensions those pages share: the standard working week, how hours beyond it are limited, how they are compensated, rest and breaks, recording, and how much is left to collective agreement. Each section links to the page that carries the detail; the figures here are as those pages report them.

## Standard hours and the weekly ceiling

Most of these systems define a standard or statutory week and, separately, a ceiling on total hours. They differ in which of the two they make binding and how they average it.

- **European Union.** The [[Legislation/working-time-directive]] does not set a "statutory" working week above which overtime is paid; that is left to Member States. What it sets is a ceiling: average working time in each seven-day period must not exceed 48 hours, including overtime, averaged over a reference period of up to four months as standard, extendable to six months by law for certain activities or twelve months by collective agreement ([[DefinedTerm/maximum-weekly-working-hours]]).
- **United Kingdom.** A worker cannot work more than 48 hours a week on average, normally averaged over 17 weeks; workers under 18 cannot work more than 8 hours a day or 40 hours a week.
- **Spain.** Under the [[Legislation/workers-statute]], working time is set by collective agreement up to a maximum of 40 hours of effective work, as a weekly average over a year, and ordinary working time may not exceed 9 hours a day unless an agreement distributes it differently.
- **France.** The statutory working time is 35 hours a week or 1,607 hours a year; hours worked at the employer's request beyond it are overtime ([[DefinedTerm/overtime]]).
- **Japan.** Article 32 of the Labour Standards Act sets statutory working hours at 40 hours a week and 8 hours a day, excluding breaks ([[DefinedTerm/article-36-agreement]]).
- **United States.** The [[Legislation/fair-labor-standards-act]] uses 40 hours in a workweek as the threshold for overtime pay but places no limit on the number of hours employees aged 16 and older may work. The workweek is a fixed 168-hour period and cannot be averaged with other weeks.
- **Canada (federal).** Part III of the [[Legislation/canada-labour-code]] fixes standard hours of 8 a day and 40 a week and a weekly maximum of 48 hours in most cases. Where work is irregular, hours may be averaged over two or more weeks, and modified schedules must not exceed an average of 40 standard and 48 maximum hours a week over the schedule's period.
- **Germany.** The wiki's page on the [[Legislation/working-hours-act-germany]] does not state a weekly figure; it describes the act through its rest-period rule and its room for deviation, and mentions that collective agreements can extend working time beyond ten hours per working day where it regularly includes readiness for work or standby duty.

## How overtime is limited

The clearest divide is between systems that cap overtime hours and one that regulates overtime only through pay.

| Jurisdiction | Limit on overtime |
| --- | --- |
| EU | No separate overtime cap; overtime counts towards the 48-hour average |
| Spain | 80 hours a year, excluding hours to prevent or repair extraordinary and urgent damage; hours compensated by time off within four months do not count |
| France | Annual quota set by collective agreement, or 220 hours per employee a year without one; hours beyond it may not breach the maximum weekly working time |
| Japan | Overtime is lawful only under an Article 36 agreement filed with the authority; as described in 2015, the agreement had to conform to limits such as 45 hours a month and 360 hours a year for general workers |
| Canada (federal) | The 48-hour maximum can be exceeded only under an excess hours permit, for emergency work, under an averaging plan or under a modified work schedule |
| United States | No limit on hours; overtime is regulated through premium pay |

Across the EU more broadly, Eurofound finds that default annual limits range from 80 hours in Spain to 250 hours in Hungary and can often be extended by agreement ([[Report/overtime-in-europe-regulation-and-practice]]).

Japan's mechanism stands apart in form: the default rule forbids work beyond 8 hours a day or 40 a week, and overtime and holiday work become lawful only through a labour–management agreement concluded with a majority union or a majority representative. The Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare presents the current upper limit on overtime as a separate policy, which the wiki does not yet cover in detail.

Whether a worker may refuse overtime also varies. In Spain it is voluntary unless a collective agreement or contract provides otherwise. In France employees cannot in principle refuse overtime the employer decides on. In federal Canada employees may refuse it to meet specified family responsibilities, except in an unforeseeable situation posing an imminent or serious threat. Eurofound notes that across Europe the right to refuse is seldom absolute.

## How overtime is compensated

- **United States:** at least one and one-half times the [[DefinedTerm/regular-rate-of-pay]] for hours over 40 in a workweek; the requirement cannot be waived by agreement.
- **Canada (federal):** at least 1.5 times the regular hourly wage, or, on the employee's written request and agreement, 1.5 hours of paid time off per hour worked.
- **Japan (as described in 2015):** a premium of at least 25% for overtime, at least 50% beyond 60 hours a month (deferred for small and medium-sized enterprises), and at least 35% for holiday work.
- **France:** where no agreement sets the rates, 25% for the 36th to 43rd hour of the week and 50% from the 44th; an agreement may set other rates, each at least 10%. Hours beyond the annual quota also give a mandatory rest counterpart.
- **Spain:** pay at no less than the ordinary hourly rate, or paid time off.
- **EU:** the Directive does not regulate pay. Eurofound reports that the most common premium across Member States is 50%, and that Germany, Ireland, the Netherlands and the UK give no statutory entitlement to a premium.

## Rest periods and breaks

| Jurisdiction | Daily rest between working days | Break during work | Weekly rest |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| EU | 11 consecutive hours per 24 | When the day exceeds 6 hours; length left to agreement or national law | 24 hours plus the 11 hours' daily rest |
| UK | 11 hours | 20 minutes if working more than 6 hours | 24 hours a week or 48 hours a fortnight |
| Spain | At least 12 hours | At least 15 minutes beyond 6 consecutive hours | A day and a half |
| Germany | At least 11 hours, shortenable by up to two hours by collective agreement with compensation | Not described on the wiki's page | Not described on the wiki's page |
| Japan | Not described on the wiki's pages | At least 45 minutes beyond 6 hours; at least 1 hour beyond 8 | One day a week, or four days over four weeks |
| Canada (federal) | At least 8 consecutive hours between shifts | An unpaid break of at least 30 minutes in every five consecutive hours (paid if the employee must remain at the employer's disposal) | A weekly day of rest |

See [[DefinedTerm/rest-breaks-at-work]] for how the EU rules have been interpreted. The FLSA page records no rest-period requirement; its treatment of rest and meal breaks concerns whether they count as [[DefinedTerm/hours-worked]] for pay.

## Recording working time

Recording is how limits become checkable, and several of these jurisdictions have tightened it ([[DefinedTerm/working-time-recording]]).

- **EU:** after the Court of Justice's 2019 CCOO judgment, Member States must require employers to set up an objective, reliable and accessible system measuring each worker's daily working time.
- **Spain:** the case arose there, and Royal Decree-law 8/2019 amended the Workers' Statute to make registration of working hours compulsory for all employers; companies must keep a daily record of each worker's start and finish times.
- **Germany:** in 2022 the Federal Labour Court ruled that employers must introduce a recording system regardless of company size.
- **Japan:** employers must confirm and record each worker's start and finish times, in principle by direct observation or objective records, and keep records for three years.
- **Canada (federal):** employers must keep records of the hours each employee works each day for 36 months.
- **UK:** employers need to keep some records of working hours.

## The role of collective agreements

Most of these systems leave room for agreement, at different points. The EU Directive allows derogations by collective agreement, including extending the averaging period to twelve months. Spain's Statute sets working time by collective agreement within its limits, and agreements can redistribute daily hours. Germany's act permits many deviations from its core sections through collective agreements or works agreements based on them; the IAB statement summarised on that page sees this as keeping the power asymmetry between establishments and individual employees from working to employees' detriment. In Japan the Article 36 agreement is itself the gateway to overtime. Federal Canada requires union agreement, or approval by individuals or at least 70% of affected employees, for modified work schedules. The FLSA stands at the other end: its overtime requirement cannot be waived by agreement.

## Who is excluded

Several of these systems exclude some workers from their working-time rules, and the exclusions differ. The EU's "autonomous workers" derogation covers those whose working time is not measured or predetermined, particularly managing executives. The UK lets individual workers opt out of the 48-hour week ([[DefinedTerm/working-time-opt-out]]) and lists jobs that may require more. France places senior executives and employees on an annual fixed number of days outside the overtime rules. Japan's recording standard excludes managers and supervisors and workers under deemed working-hours systems. Federal Canada's hours-of-work division does not apply to managers, superintendents, employees who exercise management functions, or members of the architectural, dental, engineering, legal or medical professions. The FLSA's overtime rules apply unless an employee is specifically exempted.
